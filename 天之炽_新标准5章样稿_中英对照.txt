第157章 叹息之墙的归巢

夜色在翡冷翠北郊的丘陵间沉淀下来，远处是密涅瓦机关地面建筑群灰蒙蒙的剪影，近处是叹息之墙黑铁巨门前那盏孤零零的瓦斯灯，火苗被风吹得忽明忽暗。

瓦莲京娜把礼车停在侧门前，引擎熄了，叶尼塞国旗在夜风里疲惫地垂下来。她没有下车，只是把粉色的发带从指间绕到手腕上，转过头来看了西泽尔一眼。

"从这里进去，今晚不要出来。"她说，"明天早上有人来接你们，战场是密涅瓦机关选定的，规则也定了。你们只管去做你们该做的事。"

她顿了一下，唇角微扬，那笑容收敛了方才在翡冷翠街道上的张扬，变得很轻，很浅。

"明天在战场等你证明自己是盖世英雄。"

西泽尔点了点头。他没有说感谢，瓦莲京娜也不需要他的感谢。礼车悄无声息地滑入夜色，粉色的发带在车尾灯的光里闪了最后一下便消失了。

叹息之墙的安检门在他们面前无声地张开。犬齿般的钢铁门扉咬合了四百多年，每一次开合都发出沉闷的叹息。

门内的检测台上，一位穿着灰色制服的技术员把某种带六翼猫头鹰徽记的黄铜仪器贴上了西泽尔的右手手腕。仪器的表盘上那根细针猛地跳了一格——不是红，是极淡的、近乎透明的金。

技术员愣了一下，抬头看看西泽尔，又低头看看表盘。

"红伞能量残留。"他轻声说了一句。

佛朗哥教授不知什么时候已经站在了安检门背后，他穿着一件皱巴巴的黑色长袍，眼底发青，嘴唇干裂，显然已经在这扇门后等了很久。他没有看西泽尔的脸，只是看着那个表盘，点了点头。

"记录在案，归档为'4级非威胁性史前频率残留'，编号T-235。"

他转过脸来，那双深陷在眼窝里的眼睛像两口枯井。

"西泽尔，跟我来。"

西泽尔没问去哪里，佛朗哥没说要做什么，碧儿已经从安检门后方的走廊里小跑出来了。

她怀里抱着一个纸袋，纸袋里散发着刚出炉的面包香气——不是密涅瓦机关食堂那种合成淀粉烤出来的硬邦邦的东西，是坎特伯雷堡的烤炉连夜烤出来的，带着黄油和面粉最朴素的味道。纸袋旁边还夹着一套叠得整整齐齐的干净军装，深青色，肩章上没有任何徽记，是密涅瓦机关内部非公开配发的便装。

"西爷，我算着时间呢，您浑身都湿了，先换衣服。"碧儿把纸袋和军装一起塞进西泽尔怀里，抬起手就帮他解风衣的扣子。她动作快，眼睛却一直在西泽尔的脸上转，看到他苍白的脸色和微微发青的嘴唇时，她没有出声，只是把他解下来的湿风衣接过去搭在手臂上。

"面包是宝儿小姐让厨房做的，烤了两炉，第一炉糊了。"碧儿低声说，"她说您小时候最爱吃刚出炉的，凉了就不香了。"

西泽尔咬了一口面包。黄油在舌尖化开，温热的、柔软的味道顺着食道滑下去，他忽然觉得这一天的寒冷和疲惫都被那口面包融掉了一小块。

临时手术室就在安检门后第二间，原本是密涅瓦机关值班医生的休息室，被佛朗哥临时清空了。房间里没有床，只有一张被推到墙角的窄长金属桌，桌面上铺着消过毒的灰白色亚麻布。

碧儿攥着一条干净的白毛巾守在桌旁。

佛朗哥从随身携带的黑色皮质冰盒里取出一个黄铜密封管，管壁刻着编号"235"。他拧开管盖，里面是一枚比西泽尔后颈现有那枚更细、更短、颜色更浅的黄金套管，套管表面有一层极薄的秘银镀膜。

"计划235适配款。"佛朗哥说，"你的旧套管被红伞能量和欧米茄碎片双重冲击过，内壁已经出现微裂纹，继续使用可能在下一次神经接驳中直接断裂刺入脊髓。我昨晚通宵用初代总长留下的备用材料赶制了这枚。"

他从另一个冰盒里取出一套便携神经接驳仪，仪器的体积只有他平时使用那台的三分之一，黄铜外壳上嵌着六只微型电极。

薇若兰不知什么时候已经换好了工作服，她的长发用一根黑丝带高高扎起，露出颈后那枚属于自己的黄金套管的金属光泽。她把便携神经接驳仪推到金属桌旁，插上电源，校准了仪表。

"局部麻醉。"她对西泽尔说，"整个过程你都会是清醒的，但不会感到剧痛。我会监控你的神经电流，如果出现任何反常波动，我会立刻停止。"

"二十分钟。"佛朗哥说，"不能再长了。"

西泽尔坐上金属桌，背对着佛朗哥和薇若兰。他低下头，碧儿立刻把白毛巾折好垫在他额下，凉丝丝的。

第一针是局麻，针尖刺入后颈皮肤时他几乎没感觉，只有一丝凉意顺着脊椎往下走。第二针开始剥离旧套管周围因反复穿刺而增生的瘢痕组织，他的手指微微收紧，碧儿立刻用毛巾擦去他额角渗出的冷汗。

"再忍一下。"薇若兰低声说，她的声音里没有平日里那种刻意的轻佻和玩味，"旧套管取出来了。"

金属轻轻碰触金属的声音。西泽尔从眼角余光看到佛朗哥戴着放大镜的眼睛，和那双因为通宵而微微发颤的手。

新套管沿着旧套管留下的通道被推入骨膜层。这次没有那四根金色细针的剧痛，秘银镀膜让套管与神经的契合度更高，佛朗哥只用了三枚微型钛钉就将它固定在颈椎第三节和第四节之间。

便携神经接驳仪的表盘上，绿色指示灯稳定地亮起。

"耦合度91%。"薇若兰念出数据，"比你的旧套管高出七个点。佛朗哥，你的焊接手艺还没丢。"

"少废话，检查有没有渗血。"佛朗哥把放大镜推到额头上，眯着眼仔细端详着新套管与皮肤接触的边缘，"渗血量在安全范围内，不用缝合，医用胶水封闭即可。"

整个过程十八分钟。佛朗哥把工具一件件收回冰盒里时，薇若兰已经在便携神经接驳仪上打印出了一份完整的手术记录。

"三天内不要做剧烈神经接驳。"她把记录递给佛朗哥，"明天那场对抗战……你打算让他用多少？"

佛朗哥没有回答。

西泽尔从金属桌上下来，碧儿帮他把新军装的上衣领口理好，刚好遮住后颈那块新换的医用胶布。她垂下眼睛去系他胸前的扣子时，无意间对上了西泽尔的脸。

她愣了一下。

西泽尔的紫色瞳孔深处，有什么东西在极缓极淡地翻涌，像金色与红色交织的细丝被水底的暗流推动着，若不仔细看根本看不见。

碧儿没有声张。她只是从口袋里摸出一小瓶眼药水，拧开盖子递到西泽尔手边。

"爷，您眼睛看着有点干，滴两滴。"

西泽尔接过眼药水，没有滴。他知道碧儿看见了什么。他也知道碧儿不会问。

走廊尽头传来金属零件碰撞的细碎声响。

阿方索蹲在改装室角落的一张工作台前，面前摊着一件被拆到一半的武器。那是一柄从缴获的雷霆牙残骸上拆下来的枪管，枪管外壁已经被他用锉刀打磨出密密麻麻的鱼鳞纹，旁边还摆着一根被截短的新龙炎备用枪管。

他在偷偷把雷霆牙的零件焊到新龙炎的备用枪管上。

这是违反密涅瓦机关武器库管制条例的——备用枪管属于受控武器，未经枢机会命令不得擅自改造。但阿方索的焊接手法极其隐蔽，他将两根枪管以嵌套方式连接，外层是龙炎的备用管，内层是雷霆牙的截短管，如果不拆开根本无法发现。

他听到身后有人经过，抬起头。

是西锡尼。

这位异端审判局的中校刚好来确认叹息之墙的安检权限是否已经完全回收给密涅瓦机关，他穿着黑色军服，腰间挂着那柄被特意藏进枪套的Longinus碎片。

阿方索的手里还攥着那根焊到一半的备用枪管，另一只手里紧紧捏着一样东西——那是他从翡冷翠街头一路带过来的、镶嵌在掌心大小的黑色十字纹蜂巢手铳。

他犹豫了两秒。

两秒钟里他想到了很多事情，想到达斯蒙德那晚在教堂里说过的话，想到西泽尔后颈那块刚刚换上的新套管医用胶布，想到明天那场十米级普罗米修斯与五名新型炽天使之间的死战。

他站了起来，把手铳塞到了西锡尼手里。

"帮个忙。"阿方索说，"明天的战场，万一我回不来，请把它交给碧儿姐姐。"

西锡尼低头看了一眼手里那对冰凉的、散发着淡淡枪油味的手铳，又抬头瞥了一眼改装室深处西泽尔刚换完药、正在碧儿帮助下系军装扣子的方向。

他什么也没多问。

他只是用左手抬了抬军帽的帽檐，向阿方索敬了一个军礼——不是标准的全礼，是半礼，是战友之间不必多言的那种。

然后他转身走了。

改装室另一头，昆提良正拦住了刚从小隔间里走出来的塔拉夏。他那张被翡冷翠的夜风吹得通红的脸上带着一种少年人特有的、不好意思说出口的恳切。

"塔拉夏先生。"他压低声音，"密涅瓦机关的附属医院，是只对内部人员开放吗？"

"原则上只服务机关人员和被授权的相关者。"塔拉夏推了推眼镜，"你问这个做什么？"

"艾莲的妈妈，她现在在马斯顿，肺上的病很重。"昆提良的声音更低了，"我想……等打完这一仗，我想带她来翡冷翠。"

塔拉夏沉默了三秒钟。

"去打你的仗。"他最终说，"打完回来我帮你办。"

临时休息舱的灯是那种密涅瓦机关特有的暗黄色铁罩瓦斯灯，光线被金属罩子切割成一个个不规则的扇形，洒在窄小的行军床上。

西泽尔换好了干衣服，坐在行军床沿。他从口袋里摸出一张被折了好几折的、发黄的小纸片。

那是昨夜姑姑塞给他的字条。他一直没来得及看。

字条上的字迹很潦草，用的是东方式的毛笔，墨迹被雨水晕开过，边缘有浅浅的水渍。字条上只有两行字：

"我儿，
姑姑在骷髅地等你确认真相。"

瓦斯灯的火焰跳动了一下。

西泽尔把那行字看了很久。他的手指摩挲着字条上方那句模糊的、辨认不清的"新神使"三个字，然后慢慢把字条折回去，塞进贴身的内衣口袋里。

后颈的新套管微微发热。紫瞳深处那抹极淡的金红色纹路，又翻涌了一下。


【English】
Chapter 157 — The Wall of Sighs, Homecoming

Night settled into the hills north of Florence the way ink settles into blotting paper — slow, then total. Far off, the Minerva Bureau's aboveground complex made a low, grey smear against the cloud. Closer, beneath the black iron mass of the Wall of Sighs, a single gas lamp hung at the side gate, its flame shoved sideways and shrunk by the wind.

Valentina killed the engine. The Yenisei pennant at the bumper sagged like something too tired to stand. She did not get out. She lifted the pink ribbon from her lap, wound it twice around her wrist, and turned her face to Cesare.

"In from here. Don't come out tonight." She did not raise her voice above what the wind would carry. "In the morning, someone will come for you. The field is the one the Bureau chose. The rules are the rules. You do what you have to do."

A pause. The corner of her mouth lifted — not the broad bright thing she had worn in the Florence streets, but something lighter, and briefer, and the more dangerous for being tender.

"Tomorrow on the field you may prove yourself a hero out of legend."

Cesare nodded. He did not say thank you. Valentina did not need it. The courtesy car slid back into the dark without sound. The pink ribbon caught the taillight for one instant, and then was gone.

The Wall's security gate opened for them in silence. Those iron teeth had been parting and closing for four hundred years, and every closing had been a kind of sigh.

Inside, at the inspection bench, a technician in a grey Minerva uniform pressed a brass instrument — badged with the six-winged owl of the Tribunal — against the inside of Cesare's right wrist. The needle on the dial jumped one notch. Not red. A gold so faint it was almost transparent.

The technician looked up at Cesare. Looked down at the dial. Looked up at Cesare again.

"Red Umbrella residue," he said, quietly, as if to himself.

Professor Franco was standing behind the gate. He had been standing there for some time, by the look of him. The black robe he wore had been slept in. His eyes were sunk in pits the colour of mould. His lips were cracked. He was not looking at Cesare's face. He was looking at the dial.

"Log it," he said. "File as Level Four, non-threatening prehistoric-frequency residue. Case T-235."

He turned. The two pits in his face could not have been emptier had he scooped them out with a spoon.

"Cesare. With me."

Cesare did not ask where. Franco did not say.

Beryl was already running down the corridor beyond the gate, a paper bag in her arms — the kind of bag that breathes warm bread-smell into cold air. Not the hard synthetic starch loaves the Bureau's cafeteria turned out, but the real thing, baked through the night at Canterbury Keep, butter and flour in their plainest forms. Tied to the bag with string, a folded uniform: dark teal, no insignia, the off-record dress coat the Bureau issued to its own and to no one else.

"Master Cesare, I've been watching the clock — you're soaked through, change first." She thrust the bag and the uniform into his arms and reached up and started on the buttons of his wet overcoat. Her hands were quick. Her eyes were quicker. They kept moving over his face, taking in the new white of his skin, the bluish cast at the corner of his mouth. She said nothing about it. She only took the wet coat when it came off and laid it across her arm.

"The bread is from Miss Beatrice. She had the kitchen bake two batches. The first burned." Beryl said it quietly, into the wool of his sleeve. "She said when you were small you would not eat it once it had gone cold."

Cesare bit into the loaf. The butter opened on his tongue, warm and soft, and slid down his throat, and for a moment the cold of the whole day and the weight of the whole day took one small piece of damage.

The makeshift surgery was the second room off the gate — a duty doctor's rest chamber that Franco had cleared out an hour ago. No bed. A narrow metal table pushed against the wall, dressed in boiled grey linen.

Beryl stood at the table's edge, gripping a clean white towel as though it were a rosary.

Franco took from his black leather cold-case a brass-sealed tube, the wall of it engraved with the number 235. He unscrewed the cap. Inside, on a bed of cotton, lay a gold sleeve: thinner than the one already seated at Cesare's nape, shorter, paler. Over its surface a film of mithril, so thin the eye could not be sure it was there at all.

"Plan 235, adapted model." Franco set the tube down on the linen. "Your old sleeve has been through Red Umbrella energies and an Omega shard in the same night. The inner wall has hairline cracks. One more neural coupling, and it will shear and drive into the spinal cord. I worked through the night with the materials the first Grand Master left in reserve. This is the result."

From a second case he took a portable neural interface unit — a third the bulk of the apparatus he ordinarily used. Six micro-electrodes were set into its brass housing, fine as the legs of a spider.

Violante had changed, somewhere in the last few minutes, into a working smock. Her long hair was bound high with a black ribbon, baring the small gold glint of her own sleeve at the nape of her neck. She rolled the interface unit to the metal table, plugged in, calibrated the dials with fingers that did not hesitate.

"Local anaesthetic," she told Cesare. "You will be conscious throughout. There should be no sharp pain. I will be monitoring your neural current. Any abnormal pattern, I stop."

"Twenty minutes," said Franco. "No more."

Cesare sat on the table with his back to them and lowered his head. Beryl folded the white towel and slid it under his forehead. It was cool against the skin. It smelled faintly of soap and of nothing at all.

The first injection slid into the skin at the nape of his neck with only a thread of cold running down the spine. The second was the dissection of the scar tissue that had built up around the old sleeve over years of couplings, layer after pale layer of it. His fingers tightened against the table's edge. Beryl was there with the towel, taking the sweat from his temple the moment it came.

"Hold," Violante murmured. None of her usual mockery. None of the bright-eyed tease she wore the way other women wore jewellery. "The old sleeve is out."

A small sound: metal against metal, intimate as a coin dropped in a pew. From the corner of his eye, Cesare saw Franco's hand — the jeweller's loupe pressed into the bone of his brow, the fingers trembling with the small precise tremor of a man who had not slept. The new sleeve travelled up the channel the old one had worn for him. There was no hot thrust of the four gold needles this time. The mithril took to the nerve more cleanly, more honestly, like a key sliding into a lock that had finally been filed to fit. Three titanium micro-pins seated the sleeve between the third and fourth cervical vertebrae, and that was all.

The green lamp on the portable interface held steady. Held steady. Held steady.

"Coupling at ninety-one percent." Violante read it out, professional, flat. "Seven points above your old sleeve. Franco, your soldering hand is not what it was."

"Be quiet and check the seam for seepage." Franco shoved the loupe up into his hairline and squinted along the join where sleeve met skin. "Within safe bounds. No suture. Medical adhesive will close it."

Eighteen minutes in all. By the time Franco was laying the instruments back in the case, one by one, in their velvet slots, Violante had the printer feed out a clean operative record in two copies.

"No strenuous neural couplings for three days," she said, handing the record over. "As for tomorrow's combat — how much of the new sleeve do you mean to let him use?"

Franco said nothing. He closed the case. The brass catch made the small bright sound of a thing being shut away.

Cesare swung his legs down off the table. Beryl was already there, straightening the high collar of the new uniform jacket over the fresh square of adhesive at his nape. As she worked the buttons up his chest, her eyes lifted to his, and for an instant they held.

She blinked.

Deep in the purple of Cesare's pupils, something was moving. Slowly. Faintly. As though fine threads of gold and red, woven one into the other, were being pushed from beneath by some current at the bottom of a well. You could not have seen it if you had not been looking for it. Beryl had not been looking for it. She had simply been there, with her face close to his, buttoning the coat.

She said nothing. She reached into her apron and took out a small bottle of eye-drops, unscrewed the cap, and held it out to him.

"Sir. Your eyes look a little dry. Two drops, please."

Cesare took the bottle. He did not use it. He knew what Beryl had seen. He knew she would not ask. He put the bottle into his own pocket, and she went on with the buttons, and neither of them spoke of it.

Down the corridor, the small dry sound of metal parts being knocked together — the sound of a careful hand at work.

Alfonso was crouching at a workbench at the far end of the conversion room. In front of him lay a weapon taken halfway to pieces: a gun barrel cut from the carcass of a captured Thunder Fang, its outer wall already filed down by his hand into a dense fish-scale pattern that drank the light. Beside it, a shortened spare barrel from a New Dragonflame.

He was welding the one inside the other.

The act was, strictly, a violation of the Bureau's arsenal regulations. Spare barrels were a controlled item. Modifying one without the Conclave's authorisation was not the sort of thing that earned a slap on the wrist; it was the sort of thing that earned a tribunal, and tribunals in Florence did not always end with the accused still breathing. But Alfonso's work was so careful, so neat, that the Dragonflame spare now sat as the outer sleeve around the cut-down Thunder Fang — a wolf wearing a sheep's coat. Without dismantling the assembly, no inspector would have known.

He heard footsteps behind him and looked up.

Sisinnius.

The lieutenant-colonel of the Tribunal of Heresy had come to confirm that the Wall of Sighs' security clearance had been fully returned to the Minerva Bureau. He wore the black uniform. At his hip, half-hidden in its holster, a shard of Longinus.

Alfonso was still holding the half-welded barrel in one hand. In the other, he was gripping something small and cold — a pair of palm-sized black cross-hive handcuffs he had carried out of the Florence streets and not let go of since. They smelled faintly of gun oil. They were, in a sense, the most personal thing he owned.

He hesitated for two seconds. In those two seconds he thought of a great many things. Of what Father Desmond had said in the church that night, leaning over the rail with the candles guttering behind him. Of the square of fresh adhesive on the back of Cesare's neck. Of what tomorrow would be, when a single Prometheus-class suit walked into a field against five new Seraphs in a contest that no one had agreed was fair.

He stood. He put the handcuffs into Sisinnius's open hand.

"Do me a favour," he said. "Tomorrow, on the field — if I don't come back, give those to Sister Beryl."

Sisinnius looked down at the cold metal in his palm. He glanced once, briefly, down the length of the conversion room, in the direction of Cesare buttoned into a fresh uniform, Beryl's hands on his collar.

He did not ask what they were. He did not ask why.

He lifted the brim of his cap with his left hand and gave Alfonso a salute. Not the full salute of regulation. The half-salute. The one soldiers use between themselves when there is nothing left to say, and the only honest thing is to acknowledge that you have heard it.

Then he turned and walked.

At the other end of the conversion room, Quintilian had stopped Tarasha as he came out of a small cubicle. The boy's face was wind-reddened from the Florence night, and he wore the particular expression of a young man trying to be casual about something that mattered to him very much.

"Mr Tarasha." He kept his voice low, the way you keep your voice in a chapel. "The Bureau's attached hospital — is that only for Bureau staff?"

"In principle, Bureau personnel and authorised affiliates." Tarasha adjusted his glasses with one knuckle. "Why?"

"Elen's mother. She is in Marston. Her lungs are very bad." Quintilian's voice dropped further, as if the words themselves might hurt her somewhere. "I thought — when this is over, when the fighting's done — I'd like to bring her to Florence. For treatment. If that's allowed."

Tarasha was quiet for three seconds. He was not a man who was ever quiet for no reason; his silences had weight to them, the way good coin had weight.

"Go fight your fight," he said, at last. "When you come back, I will see it done."

In the temporary rest cabin, the lamp was of the kind the Bureau favoured — an iron-shielded gas flame, the light cut into a scatter of irregular fans across a narrow campaign cot. The air smelled of brass polish and cold tea.

Cesare sat on the edge of the cot, in dry clothes. From his breast pocket he took a small square of paper, folded several times, the colour of weak tea. The note his aunt had pressed into his hand the night before, in the rain, with a look on her face he had not been able to read. He had not, until now, had time to look at it.

The handwriting was hurried, in an Eastern brush. The ink had been wet when the rain came, and the paper had drunk some of it. The edges bore the ghost of water stains. Two lines only:

"My son,
Aunt awaits you at Golgotha, to confirm the truth."

The gas flame in the iron shade gave a small, sharp jump, as though someone had walked past in the corridor outside, though no one had.

Cesare read the two lines for a long while. His thumb moved over the three characters above them — half-effaced, almost unreadable, the brush strokes gone to a smudge in the rain. The new divine emissary. He had read them already. He read them again. He could not tell, in the bad light, whether the strokes were written in a hand he recognised, or in a hand that had been made to look like one he would.

He folded the paper back along its old creases and slid it into the breast pocket of his shirt, against the skin, where it lay flat and cold and was not warm even after a minute.

At the nape of his neck, the new sleeve gave off a faint heat, like a small coal banked in ash.

Deep in the purple of his eyes, the pale gold-and-red filaments stirred once more.

办公室里安静得能听到维苏威火山底层岩浆流动的声音。

"圣堂装甲师查抄了密涅瓦通往骷髅地的入口闸门。"

佛朗哥看着西泽尔。

"三个入口，全部封死。"

Chapter 158 — The Glacial Jade Drawings of Fury Type III

It was six in the morning, and the underground rail at Minerva was still venting steam.

The sound woke him. Not a bell, not a horn. Just iron on iron, the long low groan of the tracks flexing in the cold.

Cesare sat up. The new golden cannula set into his spine was still faintly warm, as if a small coal had been lodged between the fourth and fifth vertebrae. He could feel it with every breath, a steady dry heat.

Biel had laid his dry clothes at the foot of the cot. A cup of tepid water. Two sleeping tablets on a small tin plate beside the cup. She was always afraid he did not sleep enough.

He did not take them.

When he stepped out of the temporary berth, Quintilian was crouching at the end of the corridor by a standpipe, splashing ice water on his face. Water everywhere, darkening the iron floor in a ragged map. The man was from somewhere south, in the islands, and no amount of Minerva discipline had beaten the habit out of him.

"Morning, boss." Quintilian wiped his face, his voice still gritty with sleep. "There's been noise in the modification room since before first light. Alfonso."

Cesare grunted and turned down the corridor.

The modification-room door was not quite shut. A seam of copper arc-light leaked through the gap, and a thin scorched-metal smell. Alfonso was hunkered under the long worktable, only the back of his head visible. His mechanical arm was sheathed in heat-resistant gloves. He was sleeving one gun barrel inside another, a tight careful fit. The welder hissed in a steady cadence. One stroke, then another. Like a sewing machine somewhere in the dark.

Cesare did not interrupt him.

He went up to the twelfth floor.

Professor Franco's office sat at the end of the corridor, behind a door he had refitted himself. The handle was a six-winged owl, the bronze wings polished to a soft old gold by years of hands. The door was unlocked.

Cesare had barely settled in front of the desk when two pairs of heels came down the hall.

The first rang like hooves on a tin roof.

The second fell at longer intervals, languid, the footfall of something with claws that preferred not to use them.

Violante arrived first.

She wore a high-cut qipao of bottle-green velvet today, slit almost to the hip, her white hair pinned at the nape with a single jade hairpin. The line of her collarbone looked like a curved blade. She opened the door with her knee, the way she always did.

"You are up early," Franco said, not looking up.

"I was afraid if I came later, you would already have burned the secrets to ash." She leaned in the doorframe, and her gaze found Cesare. Rested on him a single second.

Then she stepped aside.

Valentina Romanov was standing in the corridor behind her.

She was not wearing the diplomatic court-dress of the night before. She was wearing what every third mechanic in Minerva wore: a deep grey one-piece coverall, sleeves rolled to the elbows, her long golden hair tied back with a pink ribbon, white boots dark with machine oil. If you did not know her eyes — that born arch in the corner of each, the one that managed to be both arrogant and childlike in the same instant — she could have been the prettiest machinist on the shop floor.

"I snuck in," she said to Cesare, smiling, and the smile still had the warmth of last night's mad dash across the rooftops of Florence. "Professor Violante gave me a temporary work-badge. It says Intern."

Violante's mouth twitched.

The two women looked at each other across the doorframe for a second. Violante put out her hand. Valentina put out hers.

They shook. Short, hard, nails almost breaking skin.

"A pleasure, finally," Violante said.

"Likewise," said Valentina.

Franco lifted his head at last and pushed the cold coffee aside. "Both of you. In. Close the door."

Valentina drew something from the deep pocket of her coverall. It was wrapped in oiled paper, wound tight, sealed at the folds. She unwrapped it on the desk. Inside was a stack of yellowed parchment drawings, their edges worn to a soft down by years of being turned over.

At the top of the stack, an oval silver seal, the imperial kui-dragon of the Yenisei house struck hard into the wax.

"The original designs for Fury Type III," Valentina said, spreading the pages. "The complete set. The version that was never released. My mother — the Yenisei Tsarevna — obtained a copy from your intelligence network in Florence twenty-two years ago."

Franco's fingers stopped when they touched the parchment.

The drawings were not printed. They were drawn by hand. The lines were as fine and clean as a machinist's cardiogram; the annotations set in old Cyrillic and Latin side by side. In the lower right corner of every page, faded red-mercury ink, a thin and decisive hand — a woman's.

"This is my mother's writing," Valentina said, her fingertip resting on a line of annotation. "She studied these drawings her whole life."

Franco pushed his spectacles up the bridge of his nose and bent close.

The annotations crowded the margins. Some were technical parameters. Some were questions. Some were a single exclamation mark. But one line had been retraced three times in the same red-mercury ink, so that it stood up out of the page, almost carved into it:

"Glacial jade of the ice-plain set into the inner lining of the spinal armor — to buffer the shock of nerve current against the spinal cord."

Franco read the line. Looked up at Cesare. Looked at Valentina.

"So this is how Yenisei reduced neural erosion."

"Yes." Valentina nodded. "The spinal lining of Fury Type III is not common orichalcum. It is raw glacial jade, mined from deep under the Yenisei ice-plain, cut into thin wafers by ninety-seven separate processes, and set into the buffer between the armor and the knight's spine. Jade is a cold mineral. It absorbs the surplus current and heat of the neural interface. The shock falls from a tenth of a common Seraph's level to —"

She paused.

" — to almost nothing."

Franco let out a slow breath.

In Minerva, almost nothing was a synonym for miracle.

"But," Valentina's voice dropped, "glacial jade is found only in the deepest stratum of the Yenisei ice-plain. A single kilogram takes thirty miners three months. The annual yield is under two hundred kilograms. Even the Fury Knights do not have enough for themselves."

She looked at Cesare.

"My mother kept these drawings all her life. She said that one day there would be a boy with purple eyes who would need them."

Silence in the office for a few heartbeats.

Franco turned the pages one at a time, drawing in his breath sharply at each. On the thirty-seventh he stopped and stared at a structural diagram for a long time.

"The spinal buffer layer," he murmured. "It can be done. We can adapt it. The spinal lining of the New Red Dragon can be modified. If we transplant the buffer concept from Fury Type III directly into our armor, in theory we can reduce neural erosion by four tenths."

"Four tenths is not enough." Violante came away from the doorframe, walked to the desk, braced her hands on the edge, and leaned in. "It is enough for an ordinary knight. Not for Cesare. His peak neural current is six times the human norm."

"I know," Franco said. "So something else will have to be combined with it."

He turned to the cabinet behind him, drew out a larger sheet, unfolded it, laid it down beside the Fury Type III drawings.

A hand-drawn mine map.

Three inks: red, blue, black. Red was the surveyed main galleries. Blue, the side tunnels. Black, the collapsed or sealed sections. The Minerva six-winged owl was stamped in the lower right corner, and beside it, in scrawled pencil:

Abandoned workings beneath Mount Vesuvius. Depth: -247 meters. Unentered for one hundred and twenty years.

"The candidate battlefield for tomorrow's engagement," Franco said, and his finger drew a slow circle on the map. "The quarry. Complex terrain, multiple drop-offs, workings and surface cross at intervals. A five-man squad has an advantage in that kind of ground — something like Prometheus, ten meters tall, loses its mobility down there."

Cesare looked at the map.

The point Franco had circled: a black worm of a gallery twisting down into the belly of Vesuvius. Beside it, several small red crosses. The crosses meant old collapse points, or, deeper in, places no one had ever charted.

"Here," Franco said, and his finger stopped at the deepest part of the drawing. "The end of the gallery leads to —"

He did not finish the sentence.

He coughed. A single soft cough, and then another. He took a handkerchief from his breast pocket and pressed it to his mouth for several seconds. Violante was at his side in a moment, sliding the cold coffee out, replacing it with a warm cup from the side-table.

"Leads to where?" Cesare asked.

"Somewhere older," Franco said, lowering the handkerchief. He did not answer the question directly. "It does not matter. Tomorrow's battle will not be fought that deep. Not enough lift in the depots. The field will be three hundred meters above the entrance, in the upper quarry."

He folded the map and pushed it to the center of the desk.

"That is the meeting. At two in the afternoon, bring your people here. I will brief you on squad tactics."

Cesare nodded.

They began to break up. Violante stayed at the desk, still bent over the drawings. Valentina drifted over to her, pointed at a line of annotation, began to discuss it in a low voice. Franco, at the other end of the table, was already scribbling, calculating the volume of jade the buffer modification would require.

Cesare walked out of the office.

The corridor went quiet. Only the ventilation ducts above his head, a low even hum. He went to the window at the far end, and stood looking out at the lowest tier of Vesuvius. Far off, the lava river crept in the dark, and the whole underground world was a flat sheet of dim red.

Footsteps behind him.

Valentina.

She came to stand on the other side of the window, leaning against the frame, not looking at him, her own eyes on the same red horizon.

"Cesare," she said, and she put the stress on the second syllable the way Russian did.

"Mm."

"Your mother —" she paused. "You do not want to know more?"

His fingers went, by old habit, to the inside pocket of his shirt. The note from his aunt. The paper was soft now from being touched.

"I do," he said.

"Then live through tomorrow." Valentina turned her head to look at him. Her gold lashes in the red light looked like a row of small flames. "Live, and come back. I will tell you then."

From the other pocket of her coverall she produced a small thing.

A silver ring.

The band was very thin. Set in the flat of the ring, a tiny sliver of glacial jade. In the dim red of the window the jade looked cold, almost transparent, faintly blue.

"A meeting gift," she said, and put the ring into his open palm. "Also a small thing to keep you alive."

Her hand, when it touched his, was cold.

"Jade will stabilize fluctuations in the nerve current," she said. "If you wear it, even if the neural interface overloads, the jade will absorb the surplus shock. Consider it lent to you by my mother."

Cesare held the ring.

The cold of the jade moved up from his palm, and somewhere at the back of his neck the golden cannula gave off its low heat, and the two temperatures met in him, somewhere in the middle, and balanced.

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me." Valentina smiled, and the smile was half the frankness of a soldier, half the mischief of a girl. "Thank my mother. She spent her whole life wanting to meet you. She would have liked to see this."

She turned and walked away.

Her white boots rang on the iron floor, and the sound went cleanly off into the corridor.

Cesare looked down at the ring in his hand. Then he slipped it onto the fourth finger of his right hand.

A perfect fit.

He turned back toward the modification room.

The door was open. Alfonso was still under the long table. The heat-resistant gloves on his mechanical arm were already worn through at the palm, the alloy knuckles showing through. The welder's arc pulsed, on, off, on.

Cesare was about to push the door open when he heard the sound.

It was not the welder.

It was metal breaking.

A clean, short, almost ringing snap — the sound of a man stepping on lake-ice in winter.

Alfonso stood up slowly from under the table.

In his hand, two halves of a part. The fracture face was jagged, the grain of the metal bright and crystalline along the break.

A Thunderfang barrel. Not the one Alfonso had modified himself — a backup pulled from the Minerva stores. The wall thickness between the two had not been the same. When he had sleeved the one inside the other, the thermal expansion rates had not matched. The cooling seam had set up an internal stress, and the spare barrel had simply come apart in his hand.

Alfonso looked at the two pieces of scrap on his palm and said nothing.

He set them down, gently, on the table. Then he crouched back under the workbench. The welder's arc pulsed on.

One stroke. Another.

Cesare watched from the door for a few seconds, then turned and walked away.

He went to the far end of the twelfth floor and pushed open Franco's door.

"Professor Franco."

"Mm."

"Alfonso's backup barrel cracked."

Franco did not look up. "Tell him to use the one he has. No more improvisation before the battle."

"Understood."

Cesare was about to step back out of the office when the old brass telephone on the desk rang.

The bell cut through the quiet of the room like a rusted blade dragged across tin.

Franco frowned, reached out, picked up the receiver.

The moment the earpiece touched his ear, his face changed.

The change was small. Not fright. Not anger. Something deeper — a kind of contraction, as if he had been punched, slowly, in the stomach. His fingers tightened on the receiver for a second.

"...are you sure?"

His voice, when he spoke, was very cold.

"...all three? All three entrances?"

Violante's head came up. Valentina's head came up.

Franco set the receiver down.

The office was so quiet that they could hear the lava of Vesuvius moving somewhere far below.

"The Cathedral Armored Division has sealed the Minerva gate at Golgotha," Franco said, looking at Cesare.

"All three entrances. Closed."

西泽尔的手指在那一行字上停了很久。

冰凉的金属贴在掌心的瞬间，他的紫瞳深处突然闪过一道金红色的纹路。

他的脑海中响起了一个声音。

低沉、平稳、带着一丝不易察觉的沙哑——那是龙德施泰特的声音。

*西泽尔。*

*你不是为复仇而生的。*

声音很短，短到只有一句。

他的后颈隐隐发烫。

黄金套管。

昨夜红伞唤醒的残枝意识，似乎正在和碎片产生某种共鸣。

龙枪看着他，没有说话。

"他……"西泽尔的声音很轻，"他在哪里？"

"已经走了。"龙枪说。

"他不肯见我？"

"他说，见了面就舍不得走了。"

西泽尔没有说话。

他把照片和碎片重新放回锦盒里，盖上盖子。动作很轻，像是在合上一口棺材。

"谢谢教官。"

"不谢。"龙枪转身，向门口走去。走了两步又停下来，"西泽尔。"

"嗯。"

"明天的战场，你不是一个人。"

西泽尔抬起头。

龙枪没有回头，带着两个便装骑士走出了改装室。脚步声在走廊里渐渐远去。

改装室里安静了几秒钟。

就在这时，门外又传来一个声音。

不是金属靴底。

也不是皮靴。

极其深沉的。

Chapter 159 — The Broken Sword and the Fragment

Cesare did not speak as he came out of Franco's office.

All three entrances sealed. The Sanctum Armored Division—the sharpest knife the Curia owned—had shut the road from Minerva to Golgotha, clean, no margin left at all.

He walked the long corridor of the twelfth level. The low-frequency hum of Vesuvius came up through the beryllium-bronze plates underfoot, numbing the soles of his boots.

The door of the modification room stood open. The arc-light of a welding torch bled through the crack, orange, pulsing—one bright, one dim—like the breath of something dying.

He pushed the door.

Alfonso crouched at the workbench. His left hand steadied two barrel segments butted end to end, the torch in his right. Sparks scattered. The two lengths—walls of mismatched thickness—glowed dull red where he worked. The weld seam crawled along the join like a centipede clinging to a pipe. Ugly. Dangerous.

"Did it hold?"

"Barely." Alfonso did not look up. "We'll test it once it's cool. If the internal stress hasn't been fully relieved—"

"It'll burst the breech."

"Yes."

Alfonso set down the torch and straightened. His face was marked with the red weals of arc-burn. The elbow joint of his prosthetic arm was flecked with iron filings.

"Boss, I want to go find Talasha."

Cesare looked at him.

Alfonso was a man of few words. Whenever he had something important to say, he would first find an excuse to leave the room. Cesare knew what he was going to do, and he knew that was not something he should be worrying about right now.

"Go."

Alfonso nodded and turned toward the door. At the threshold he stopped.

"About Talasha—" he said, without turning. "There's something I want to tell her."

"I know."

Alfonso left. The welding light died. The modification room went dim, leaving only the bench lamp with its wire-mesh shade throwing out a sallow yellow glow.

Cesare sat down on the folding chair and closed his eyes.

In his mind, Franco's face surfaced again—the face he had worn when he set the telephone down.

Three entrances. Not one left.

---

Talasha's workshop was at the other end of the twelfth level, past the full length of the ventilation-shaft corridor.

As Alfonso walked, he ran the sentence through his head, again and again. He had been rehearsing it for three days—from the hour Franco announced the new format for the contest of arms, to the day the Sanctum Armored Division sealed the entrances, to the moment the spare barrel cracked, to now.

The sentence was short.

But for him, it was the hardest sentence he had ever had to say in his life.

Talasha's door was half-shut. From inside came the small clicking of meshing gears.

He pushed it open.

Talasha had her back to him, crouching at a workbench, hands busy with something. A shock of green hair was flecked with oil. She wore a work-coat that had been washed so many times the color had gone out of it. The sleeves were rolled to the elbow, showing forearms streaked with black lubricant.

"Talasha."

"Mm." She did not turn.

"The barrel—our spare cracked."

"I heard. Professor Franco wants me to come up with a fix." Talasha stood and turned. "I've got a few options. One is to thin the wall down and re-cut the rifling—accuracy will drop. The other is to find another old barrel of the same spec in the warehouse."

"I didn't come for that."

Talasha blinked.

Alfonso stood in the doorway. His face, in the workshop's light, looked pale. His prosthetic arm hung at his side; his other hand was in his pocket, gripping something.

He drew it out.

A gear-pendant. Small, brass, the edges polished to a shine, strung on a black leather thong.

It had been a gift from the old priest. The year Cesare turned thirteen, the priest's church on Crete was burned. He had walked out with nothing but the pendant around his neck. There was no family left on the island. Franco had taken him in, brought him to Florence. The pendant had hung there ever since.

He took it off.

"I—" he began.

He had never been a man for words. He spoke with the torch and the wrench. He spoke with blueprints and gears. Never with his mouth. Before thirteen he had used his mouth. After thirteen he had come to understand that the mouth was the most useless tool of all.

"Talasha."

"Mm."

"I—"

"Out with it." Her brow knit.

Alfonso drew a breath.

"When this fight is over—will you—"

"Will I what?"

"Will you—come back to Crete with me?"

Talasha's hand stopped in the air.

The workshop went so quiet he could hear the steam moving in the overhead pipes.

"This pendant," Alfonso said, holding it out to her, "is the only thing the old man left me. There's no one left on Crete. I—there's not much point going back alone."

He paused.

"If you don't mind."

Talasha looked at the pendant in his hand. Then at his face.

Then she laughed.

It was the first time she had laughed in days.

"You—" she took the pendant from him, "—who said I minded?"

"I mean—" his face went crimson, "what I'm trying to say is—"

"I know what you're trying to say." She slipped the thong over her head and let the brass gear fall against her collarbone. "But you have to come back alive first."

Alfonso nodded.

"I will."

---

In the modification room, Cesare sat in the corner of the folding chair with his eyes closed.

The barrel was now Talasha's problem. The hours left were few. Departure at four in the morning. The battlefield was an abandoned quarry in the belly of Vesuvius. Five Seraphim against three Prometheuses. Thin-shell rounds only. No solid shot. No resupply.

Franco came in. Franco rarely came to the modification room, but today he did, a cup of cold coffee in his hand.

"Cesare."

"Mm."

"I just took a call."

Cesare opened his eyes.

"The Seraphim Knights."

Cesare's brow moved a fraction.

Just then, the sound came from outside the door.

The metal heel of a boot, tapping the threshold.

Once. Twice. Three times.

A habitual gesture—every Seraph did it before entering an unfamiliar space, a tap of the sole to test the floor, to be sure there was no trap, to be sure the ground would bear the weight of armor.

Alfonso had only just come back in from the corridor. He was the first to stop, to look up toward the doorway.

Franco set his coffee down and muttered, "So the old man has finally decided to show his face."

The door opened.

Three men came in. Plain clothes—coarse jackets, shirts washed thin, ordinary black trousers. If not for the way they carried themselves, the ramrod backs, the right shoulders tipped a fraction forward, Cesare would hardly have known them for soldiers.

The one in front was in his early forties, iron-gray hair, the wrinkles on his face cut deep as if with a knife. A long scar ran above his left eye.

Longinus. Chief instructor of the Seraphim Knights. The man who had trained Cesare from cadet to Seraph, and the man who had struck his name from the order's rolls.

In his hands he carried a small black case, no bigger than a man's palm. It was faced in black velvet, with dark-silver corner pieces.

"Cesare," Longinus said.

"Chief."

"Someone asked me to bring you this."

He set the case on the workbench.

"Who?"

"Open it and see."

Cesare reached out. The tip of his finger touched the silver clasp. The metal was cold—cold as if it had just been taken from an icehouse.

He opened the case.

Inside were two things.

The first was a piece of a blade. A fragment. The broken edge was ragged. The metal had a strange color to it—not ordinary steel, not secret-silver, but a deeper, older sheen, like gold that had been steeped in centuries. There was dried blood on the shard.

Not red.

Gold.

The second was a photograph. Old, the edges yellowed and curled, as if it had been handled again and again.

In the picture two figures stood under an olive tree on the outskirts of Florence. Sunlight filtered through the leaves and brightened their faces.

One was a young Rundstedt, perhaps in his early twenties, his blond hair catching the sun. There was a rare, almost-smile at the corner of his mouth.

The other was a girl. Black hair. A pale face. Her eyes were closed. She wore a plain white dress, like a flower that had not yet opened.

Tiran.

"This is—" Cesare's voice had gone dry.

"Rundstedt asked me to bring it to you. He said it was something he broke on purpose and left in the wreckage at Marston. The Prometheus incident."

Cesare's fingers trembled, a fraction.

"He knew you would find a way to recover it." Longinus went on. "He told me to tell you—the piece you hold is a fragment of the Sacred Sword Apparatus · Excalibur. Of the same origin as the Longinus."

Longinus paused.

"There's something written on the back of the photograph."

Cesare turned the photograph over.

On the yellowed back, in a hurried, firm hand, a line in charcoal:

*A Seraph does not live only for hatred. He must protect the things he wants to protect.*

Cesare's finger stayed on the line for a long while.

Then he reached out and closed his hand around the fragment.

The instant the cold metal touched his palm, a line of gold-red light flickered in the depths of his violet eyes.

The line spread from the edge of his pupils, like some ancient seal being touched awake.

In his head, a voice sounded.

Low, steady, the faintest rasp in it—Rundstedt's voice.

*Cesare.*

*You were not born for revenge.*

The voice was brief. One sentence. Then it was gone.

Cesare stood where he was. The fragment in his palm grew slowly warm—not the warmth of metal heated by a hand, but a deeper warmth, as if something alive moved within it.

The back of his neck burned.

The golden sheath. The remnant consciousness the Red Umbrella had woken in him last night—it seemed to be resonating with the fragment.

Longinus watched him. He said nothing.

"Where is he?" Cesare asked, very quietly.

"Gone," Longinus said.

"He won't see me?"

"He said, if he saw you, he wouldn't be able to leave."

Cesare said nothing.

He set the photograph and the fragment back into the case, and closed the lid. The motion was very gentle, as if he were shutting a coffin.

"Thank you, Chief."

"Don't mention it." Longinus turned and walked toward the door. Two paces out he stopped. "Cesare."

"Mm."

"Tomorrow on the field, you will not be alone."

Cesare lifted his head.

Longinus did not turn. He and the two plain-clothes knights walked out of the modification room. Their footsteps faded down the corridor.

For a few seconds, the room was still.

Then, from the doorway, another sound.

Not metal.

Not leather, either.

A lighter sound, almost inaudible—the sound of feet on fresh snow.

A man stepped in from the shadow of the corridor.

He wore a civilian knight's tunic, washed so often the color had gone out of it. The cuff was frayed. A button was missing from the collar. He wore no visor. His blond hair, in the dim light of the modification room, looked dull.

Rundstedt.

He stood in the doorway. He did not step inside.

Cesare was on his feet, out of the chair, before he knew he had moved.

"Rund—"

"Tomorrow, do not be reckless." Rundstedt's voice was soft, as if afraid to wake something. "Follow orders."

He looked at Cesare. His violet eyes caught a faint light in the dimness.

"You still have family. Friends."

Then he turned and went.

He did not wait for Cesare to answer. He did not wait for Cesare to follow. His back dissolved into the shadow of the corridor, a wisp of smoke into a deeper dark.

Cesare ran to the door. The corridor was already empty.

He stood for a long time.

Alfonso stood behind him. He said nothing.

---

The modification room door was pushed open again.

This time it was an officer who came in. On his shoulder, the black-seraph insignia of the Office of Heresy Trials. His uniform was creased, as if he had not changed in days.

Licinius.

In his hand, a plate of silver metal.

"Cesare."

"Major Licinius."

"Someone asked me to bring this." Licinius stepped in and set the silver plate down on the workbench. "It was left by the first commander of Minerva."

Franco set down his coffee cup.

"Perfect solder," Licinius said. "They say it can mend any broken metal."

Alfonso's eyes lit.

"It can fix the barrel?"

"In theory, it can fix anything. But there is only one plate. Once it is used, there is no more."

Alfonso looked at Cesare.

Cesare looked at the silver plate.

Its surface, under the room's lamp, gave off a soft sheen, as if liquid mercury had been frozen into a sheet of metal.

"Try it."

Alfonso picked up the gun barrel that had been welded together, the broken spare at his elbow. He pressed the silver plate to the broken face.

The instant the plate touched the broken edge—

A sudden blaze of gold light burst from the joint.

The same light as the one from the golden sheath at the back of Cesare's neck.

Alfonso froze.

"What—"

The broken edge began to close.

Not by welding. Not by glueing.

The metal itself was growing.

Out of the broken face, countless fine tendrils reached toward each other—tangling, fusing, drawing together.

The fracture was gone. The barrel was whole.

The gold light faded. It drew back, slowly, into the silver plate.

Alfonso stared at the barrel in his hand.

"Boss—" his voice shook, "—what—what is this?"

Cesare looked at the silver plate.

The golden sheath at the back of his neck was still faintly warm.

"The first commander," Franco said, very quietly, "just how much did he leave behind?"

No one answered.

In the modification room there was only the low hum of Vesuvius, far below.

But beneath that hum, it seemed, another sound was stirring.

Very old.

Very deep.

As if something in the deep strata of the earth had turned over in its sleep.

Chapter 160 — The First Awakening of the Immortal King

Sub-Level One of Mount Vesuvius. A sealed experimental chamber, a hundred and twenty meters below the surface of the world.

The third iron gate fell shut behind them with a sound like a judge setting down a gavel. The Conclave's six-winged owl, stamped into the iron at shoulder height, looked down at the four of them with the patient, incurious gaze of a thing that has seen worse. Franco walked the length of the corridor under the work-lights and threw the last of the manual bolts. The metal was cold under his palm. The air, already stale from the pumps above being throttled down for the test, took on the sealed, faintly sweet smell of a room that would not be opened for some time.

He turned to look at the faces in the chamber.

"From this moment, the air in this level will not move again," he said. "Unless I open it with my own two hands. Even if the Conclave's first cardinal came to knock on that door, you are not going out."

"Why so nervous?" Quintilian asked. He was leaning against the barrier, arms folded, the way a man leans when he would like everyone to know that he is not leaning.

"Because this is the first time the Immortal King has woken up." Franco's gaze moved across each of them in turn, the way a foreman's gaze moves across a fuse-box before a detonation. "And the first time, it may not know how to be gentle."

Alfonso had been crouched in front of the New Crimson Dragon's arm for nearly ten minutes. He had the repaired Thunder Fang barrel laid across his knees, both hands busy, and he was treating it the way his grandfather had taught him to treat a thing that had been broken and was now, by some grace, whole again. He slid the long barrel into the socket cut along the dragon-arm's outer edge, lined the dress of the joint up to the last micron, and began to work his way around the hex screws with a small, fine-toothed wrench. Each screw he tightened, he checked twice.

The break had been ugly. The barrel had been cracked nearly in two during the last sortie over the Ligurian coast, and the engineering corps had written it off as a loss. Then Franco had, without explanation, walked into the foundry archives, opened a drawer that no one had been allowed to touch in the better part of a century, and come back with a small ingot wrapped in oilcloth. The ingot had been a dull, almost blue silver under the cloth, and the men who had been in the room at the time had gone quiet in a way that had very little to do with solder.

Alfonso ran his thumb along the dress of the joint now. The line where the two halves of the barrel met was invisible. The metal around the repair even carried a faint silvery bloom, the last cool breath of the perfect solder as it had set.

"This barrel," he murmured, "is more beautiful than the one that came out of the forge."

"Naturally," Franco said, in the particular tone of a man who is not in the mood to be complimented. "That solder was sealed away by the first Grand Master himself. There is not another ounce of it in the world."

"And you used it on a gun barrel."

"What I repaired was not a gun barrel." Franco's voice went flat and final, the way a sheet of paper goes flat under a press. "What I repaired was a miracle. It will behave as a miracle is required to behave, and not a hair less."

---

The New Crimson Dragon stood still in the center of the chamber, and the chamber, for the moment, seemed to stand still around it. Where the old Red Dragon Mark II had been broad and heavy in the chest, the new one was longer in the body, leaner, built along a line that called something to mind of a hound at the leash. Its plating was sheathed in overlapping dark red scales, and each scale had been engraved, in lines so fine they could only be seen at the right angle to the light, with patterns of interlocking gears. In the center of the breastplate there was a slot the size of a man's fist, set to receive the Immortal King chip.

Violaine was already standing in front of the Babel.

She was dressed in a long black gown, drawn tight at the waist, the high collar at her throat buttoned to the top pearl. Her white hair was bound at the nape with a single red cord, the way a man binds a letter he is about to send, and the binding left the long pale column of her neck bare. In her hands she held a chip no larger than the palm of a hand, its surface etched with the six-winged owl and a dense, almost living tracery of microcircuits.

"The chip is calibrated." She did not look up. "The gold sleeve's neural current channel is open. The new sleeve's coupling is seven percent higher than the old one. In theory, it can take a higher intensity shock."

"In theory," Don Juan repeated, from his corner of the chamber. He was sitting on an ammunition crate, one ankle crossed over the other, and had not stopped watching her since she had come in.

"Theory is theory," Violaine said, and held the chip up to the work-light to inspect it once more. "Practice is practice. I will not pretend to you that I know which one we are going to get."

"Then you can pray that Cesare's life is hard enough to survive it." She lowered the chip. "It is, after all, the only life we have to spend."

Franco stepped to the front of the New Crimson Dragon and laid his palm flat against the breastplate. The metal answered with a low, sleeping hum — the standby note of a red mercury steam core, the kind of sound a kettle makes just before it begins to sing. "Everyone behind the safety line," he said.

Quintilian, Don Juan, and Alfonso retreated to the barrier at the edge of the chamber. It was cast in beryllium bronze, more than twenty centimeters thick at the post, and the lattice of the upper section had been welded by a hand that knew exactly how much a cornered machine could throw. Even if the New Crimson Dragon lost its mind inside the cockpit, it could not put a single fist through that wall.

In the chamber, only Franco, Violaine, and Cesare remained.

"Get in," Franco said.

Cesare said nothing. He walked to the Babel with the unhurried step of a man who has done this a great many times and is no longer in the habit of pretending, to himself or to anyone, that the act means nothing. He undid the buttons of his coat. He drew the coat off his shoulders, folded it along the seam, folded it again across the back, and laid it on the metal stool beside the cockpit with a care that was almost domestic, the way a man lays a coat on the foot of his own bed at the end of a long day. The action was slow. It was plain. It was, in its plainness, the most deliberate thing in the room.

He sat down inside the cockpit.

The seat took him the way a glove takes a hand. The supports at the small of his back, at the back of his skull, settled against him with the small, certain clicks of a thing remembering its fit.

Violaine stepped forward. The chip went into the slot at the dragon's breast. There was a faint, precise click, soft as a pin dropping on a marble floor, and the chip and the slot met with the soft finality of a key turning in a lock. The six-winged owl on the chip's face, for a moment, looked up at the six-winged owl on the gate behind them, and the two of them were the same.

"Begin neural coupling," Violaine said.

The gold needles rose from the back of the cockpit seat, one after another, and found the gold sleeve set into the back of Cesare's neck. The first one entered. Cesare's body gave a small, involuntary shudder, the way a man shivers when a cold hand is laid at the nape of his neck. The second entered. The third. The fourth. Each one he received with his teeth set and his eyes on the dome of the chamber, and he made no sound. By the seventh, a thin line of sweat had begun to gather at his hairline. By the tenth, his knuckles on the arms of the seat had gone white.

Franco watched the numbers on the monitor.

Coupling: 15%.

27%.

43%.

The numbers were climbing faster than they had any right to. Franco's brow drew down, slow, the way a shutter is drawn down over a shop window that is not supposed to be open after dark. This was not right. Under a normal first coupling, the figure should rise the way a tide rises — slow, layered, patient, gathering itself by degrees. What he was watching was not a tide. 61%.

78%.

89%.

92%. The figure Ludensteiger of the Black Dragon had set, in his day — the highest first-coupling score ever recorded in the standing files of the Papal States' Ministry of War. Ludensteiger, who had wept when the first number had come up on his screen, and who had been dead for thirty years.

96%.

103%.

112%.

118%.

120%.

The number held.

---

At the back of Cesare's neck, the gold sleeve began to give off a hard, almost white light. The light crawled up from the sleeve, ran along the line of his spine the way a match runs along a fuse, climbed toward the base of his skull. It went through skin, through muscle, through bone, and arrived at last in his eyes.

In the depths of his violet irises, another pair of eyes appeared.

Not a reflection. Not a trick of the work-lights. Not a stain on the glass of the monitor.

A complete, independent pair of eyes, gold-red, with the pupils slit vertical. They were smaller than Cesare's own pupils, set into the violet of the iris like two miniature suns set into a purple sky at the hour of last light. Their gaze was old. The gaze of something that had spent four hundred years looking at the same problem, and had grown very tired of it, and yet had not, in all that time, learned to look away.

On the monitor, the indicator lamps above the neural hubs went, one by one, from green to gold.

It was a color the system had never produced before.

"What—" Franco's voice failed him. He cleared his throat. The clearing did not help.

Cesare's lips moved.

"The Pope shut me away in the Saintess Tower."

It was not Cesare's voice.

It was a woman's voice. Gentle. Grieving. With the heavy, careful music of a Ceylon accent under every syllable, the way a slow river runs under a bridge.

"He made my heart into the core of the Omega."

Each word was spoken slowly, and each was spoken as if the speaker were reciting a prayer that had been waiting four centuries to be finished, and the world had not, until this moment, been willing to be still enough to hear it.

"He made my blood into the ignition agent of red mercury."

Violaine's hand was already on the emergency shock switch. A bare copper wire ran from an external source across the floor of the cockpit, a length of it coiled at her foot like a sleeping snake. One push, and a violent current would pour through the gold sleeve directly into Cesare's spine, severing every neural connection in his body in a single white instant.

"Cesare." Violaine's voice was sharp. "Can you hear me?"

Cesare did not answer.

His lips went on moving, the way a man's lips go on moving in a dream he cannot wake from.

"The roots of the World Tree lie in the East. Its leaves, in the West."

The gold-red pupils brightened, the way a coal brightens when a breath is blown into it from the side.

"Only when the two are made one can all of this come to an end."

"Now," Violaine said, very quietly, to no one in particular, and pushed the switch.

A violent current ran through the copper wire, into the gold sleeve, and slammed itself against the nerves of Cesare's spine. The New Crimson Dragon, inside its shell, convulsed once, hard, the way a man in a fever convulses when the fever breaks.

But in the next second —

A hand came out of the dragon's interior and caught Violaine's wrist.

It was Cesare's hand. The grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

He had, at some point Cesare alone could have said when, opened his eyes. The violet of his pupils was still centered on the gold-red slits, but the violet itself had come back to itself; it was awake, it was frightened, it was his.

"Don't touch her," Cesare said. His voice was hoarse, urgent, the voice of a man who has just broken the surface of a black water and is still spitting it out of his mouth. "She is part of my mother."

Violaine went still.

The current was still running through her body and into the dragon's spine. She could feel her own hand trembling around the switch she was still holding, and she could feel, faintly, the pulse at his wrist under her fingers, running faster than a pulse should run.

"Cesare," she said, very quietly.

The light in the gold-red pupils began, slowly, to die.

The way a lamp goes out, when the oil at last gives what the oil has been asked for.

The way a soul, after four hundred years of waiting, finally lets go of something it had been holding, all that time, for someone it had not yet been allowed to see.

The pupils were gone.

The violet of his irises was, again, only violet.

Cesare's hand slipped from her wrist. His body softened inside the cockpit, the way a held breath softens when it is at last let out. His head tipped to one side. He was unconscious.

On the monitor, every indicator lamp went from gold back to green, in a single, synchronized sweep, the way a field of wheat goes from one color to another when the wind passes over it.

Then they all went out.

The chamber fell into a silence so total that Franco could hear his own pulse in his ears, and the small, soft click of the monitor cooling.

He stood at the console, his shirt dark at the collar with cold sweat, and did not move for a long time.

---

Then he heard a sound.

Very faint.

Coming from the direction of the New Crimson Dragon's arm.

He turned, sharply, the way a man turns at a noise in a house he had believed to be empty.

The Thunder Fang barrel — the one Alfonso had just fitted, the one dressed at its break with the last of the first Grand Master's perfect solder — was turning on its own. Slowly. Evenly. With no one at the grip.

It turned three full revolutions. The sound of it, the small precise sound of good metal moving well, was the only sound in the chamber.

Then it stopped.

The muzzle lifted. It pointed, with what could only be called deliberation, at the dome of the chamber.

A very fine thread of gold fire came out of the muzzle.

It was not the white of burning red mercury, the color that every gunner in the Papal States knew in his teeth and his eyes. It was pure gold, the gold of old coins and old chalices, and it carried, oddly, a scent like a lotus opening at the surface of a still pond.

The gold fire drew a slow arc against the dome of the chamber, and the arc looked, for a moment, like a lotus mid-bloom, a flower that no one in the room had been expecting and no one in the room was going to forget.

Franco watched the flame. He did not breathe.

"The power of the red mercury ignition," he said, half under his breath. Then, lower: "No."

His voice dropped, at the end, to almost nothing.

"It is the power of Ceylon star-sight blood."

The fire went out.

The chamber returned to dark.

Only at the muzzle of the Thunder Fang, a single, faint, golden ember still held on, the way a coal holds on in the ash long after the fire has been told to stop.

Like something that had been asleep for a long time, in a deep place, behind three iron gates.

And had, at last, opened its eyes.

"比马快三倍。"佛朗哥冷冷地说。

"那我能拐走几个普罗米修斯骑士舱里的女孩吗？"

"不能。"

"哦。"

佛朗哥最后拿起第五具模型。镜面黑色，没有特别的长项，也没有明显的缺陷，浑身散发着一种冷酷的均衡感。

"剑舞者，无人备用甲胄。"佛朗哥把模型放在桌中央，"由塔拉夏远程操控，前线有人倒下即刻补位。这具甲胄是平衡型，没有短板，是我们最后的保险。"

舱里安静了几秒。

西泽尔拿起那张写着名字和定位的战术分工牌，看了一会儿。

纸牌很轻，纸上的字迹却重。

"龙德说得对。"他忽然开口。

所有人都看向他。

西泽尔的声音很轻，像是在跟自己说话："我不是为复仇而生的。我要守护你们，守护阿黛尔，守护那些愿意跟着我的人。"

舱里沉默。

碧儿别过脸去。昆提良低下了头。唐璜把那本小本子收了起来。阿方索轻轻叹了口气，但他的眼睛里，第一次露出了某种近乎温柔的东西。

薇若兰没有说话，只是把空冰淇淋杯子从他手中接走。

"我明天也会参战。"

瓦莲京娜的声音从舱尾传来，清晰而坚定。

所有人都转头看向她。

金发公主从折叠椅上站起来，龙牙长剑拄在地上。她的表情很平静，但眼底有某种不容拒绝的东西。

"用神怒Ⅲ型临时改装的甲胄。"她说，"西泽尔的不朽之王模式只有五分钟续航，五分钟之后，我会顶上去。"

"公主殿下——"昆提良刚要说什么。

"叫我瓦莲京娜。"她打断他，"或者叫副团长。在战场上，我首先是一个骑士，然后才是公主。"

西泽尔看着她，沉默了几秒。

"……谢谢。"

"不用谢。"瓦莲京娜微笑，"你欠我的。"

就在西泽尔伸手从枕头下摸出那枚镶嵌黑色欧泊石的戒指，准备戴回手上时——

休息舱天花板上的广播忽然响了。

"密涅瓦机关外围门岗通报——"一个机械化的女声在舱中回荡，"中山国外交大臣叶素理先生请求进入密涅瓦机关外围，声称要亲自给西泽尔·博尔吉亚少爷送一份礼物。是否准许通行？请佛朗哥总长回复。"

舱里的气氛骤然一凝。

"叶素理？"昆提良皱眉，"中山国的人？"

"上次密涅瓦外围潜进来那个东方人，就是他吧？"阿方索的声音冷淡。

西泽尔没有说话。

他忽然想起了马斯顿。

想起那座被大雨浸透的教堂，想起那个自称是东方流亡贵族的中年人，想起他手中那面紫底白狼头的国旗——中山国之狼。

那是他在马斯顿见到的第一个东方人。

也是第一个对他说"愿为将军效死"的人。

西泽尔慢慢摘下眼罩。

紫色的瞳孔在冷白灯光下显得格外清澈。他看着舱顶的广播喇叭，眼神平静。

"让他进来。"

他顿了顿，又补了一句：

"所有人，把武器准备好。"

碧儿的手指悄悄摸向了腰间藏着的短刀。


【English】
Chapter 161 — The Tactical Division of the New Seraphim

Cesare Borgia came back to the world through the smell of honeysuckle.

Not the acrid bite of red mercury going up in flame. Not the bitter tang of carbolic drifting up from the underground infirmary. Something cleaner. A cold, clear sweetness, the way a honeysuckle must smell when it cracks open at dawn in a snowfield above the tree line. The scent took him by the wrist, gentle as a child's hand, and pulled him up, up, out of the dark at the bottom of himself.

He opened his eyes.

In the violet of his irises, the slitted gold-and-red pupils were gone. What looked back at him from inside his own skull was plain Cesare again — lavender, human, and very tired.

"Awake?"

Veronella's voice came from somewhere above him. Light. Almost careless, the way she said it, as though he had not just clawed his way back from the edge of being unmade, but had merely dozed off after lunch.

He turned his head.

The rest pod was three floors below the Minerva Institute, in a chamber whose unpainted metal walls threw back the cold white of the overhead lamps. Valentina sat at the foot of the bunk on a folding chair, her pale hair pulled back carelessly at the nape, polishing the blade of a sword called Dragon's Tooth with a square of frost-jade cloth. The cloth went up the edge, down the edge, up again, the small sound of it the only music in the room. Alfonso leaned against the far wall with his arms crossed, the tiny mechanical spider-nest on his watch ticking faintly to itself, the way a real spider's nest might tick in a quiet house. Quintilian was curled in the corner with Augustus's great shield cradled in his arms like a sleeping child, half-asleep, his breathing slow. Don Juan sprawled in another chair, leafing through a small leather notebook thick with the names of girls, an unlit cigarette propped between his lips at a careful angle.

Bielle was crouched at the edge of the bunk, close enough to feel his breath. When she saw his eyes open, hers went red at the rims in a single instant.

"You frightened us to death," she said, very quietly.

Cesare did not answer. He sat up slowly, and a line of fire ran down the back of his neck where the golden sleeve of his interface had been scalded by the backwash of red-mercury steam. He let himself look at each of them in turn — Valentina's hand still on the cloth, Alfonso's steady stare, Quintilian's slow grin surfacing through sleep, Don Juan's small book already snapping shut. And at last his gaze settled on Veronella.

Veronella sat on a low metal stool at his bedside, one leg crossed over the other, a paper cup of pink ice cream in her hand.

Strawberry.

"You nearly lost control," she said, and held the cup to his mouth, spoon first. "But the thing inside you let go on its own at the end. Here."

He opened his mouth. The spoon was cold against his lip.

The sweetness broke on his tongue, and he was, for a moment, eleven years younger. Thirteen, dripping with sweat, the inside of the first prototype Seraphim still hissing on his shoulders as he stumbled off the testing floor. His knees had been soft as wet paper. Veronella had been waiting for him at the door with a paper cup of strawberry ice cream in each hand, and she had pressed one into his palm and said: You passed the test, so you get the reward. That is the rule.

Eleven years. The rule, it turned out, had not changed.

"The splinter," he said, after a long moment. "It left on its own?"

"Mm." She scooped another spoonful. "It confirmed something, and then it let go. Which means you can now command a piece of that power — at least without turning into a madman every time you suit up."

He ate the rest of the ice cream without speaking. Every spoonful was cold. Cold down his throat. Cold somewhere deeper than his throat, in the place where a man keeps the worst of what he has been. Bielle, watching from the edge of the bunk, noticed that the fingers wrapped around the empty cup had at last stopped their faint, stubborn trembling.

He set the cup down.

The hatch slid open with a clang, and Franco walked in.

The chief engineer looked terrible. The hollows under his eyes were bruises. The corners of his mouth were crusted with the dark line of dried blood that came from forty hours of forgetting to drink water. In his hands he carried a wooden tray, and on the tray stood five small, precise metal figures — each one no longer than a man's hand, but each one built with the patient, ugly devotion of a man who knew exactly what they would be expected to do.

"You're all awake," he said, in a voice like sandpaper on wood. "Good. Saves me knocking on doors."

He set the tray on the low table in the middle of the pod and lifted the first figure.

Ochre-red. Heavy in the shoulders and across the back. On its spine, twin-linked repeating guns; in one fist, a tower-shield of perfect alloy. He had polished it until it caught the lamp-light in a dull, honest gleam, the way an old sword catches the light after a good oiling.

"Cesare Borgia. New Red Dragon. Core damage, and commander of the unit." He set the model down on the table with a soft click. "Fitted with the Immortal King system. In combat, you will be the one to authorize the controlled frenzy. The neural-current threshold is locked by chip. The risk of losing yourself is reduced — not to zero, but to the minimum a man can be expected to survive."

He lifted the second figure.

Deep blue, the color of a winter sea just before the ice closes over it. The shoulders were built thick as rafters. Across its chest was bolted a rectangular heavy shield — silvered over with mithril, the edges riveted in bronze, and at its center, the closed eyes of a sleeping lion cast in metal.

"Quintilian. Augustus. Heavy-shield mode. You hold the front line. You take every shot Prometheus throws at us, and you keep on taking them until Cesare has his window. You do not step back. You do not turn your face. You are the door the world has to come through if it wants to reach the rest of us."

Quintilian raised his head from the corner. He grinned, the slow grin of a man who has just been offered the only job he was ever really cut out for. "Holding the line," he said, "is what I do best."

The third. Franco lifted it with a kind of clinical care, the way a surgeon lifts a scalpel. Silver-grey. The fingers were long and precise, built for fine work. On the shoulders sat a folding long-range sniping rig and a pair of articulated micro repair-arms, folded up like the legs of a sleeping spider.

"Alfonso. King Solomon. Field repair and long-range sniping. You work the flanks. The moment a brother Seraphim takes a hit, you patch him on the fly, in the open, under fire. And you put rounds into Prometheus's optics and steam-lines whenever you have a clean line. You are the reason we do not run out of brothers in the middle of the second hour."

Alfonso inclined his head, a fraction. He did not speak. He did not need to. The set of his jaw said everything the words would have said.

The fourth. Dark red, the color of a wine that has been poured and forgotten. It was the smallest of the five, but the joints were built to bend in ways metal was not meant to bend, and in its hand it held a curved blade whose edge was plated in perfect alloy — a sickle-sword, run through with a quiet, vicious arc.

"Don Juan. Ghost of Roses. Assassin mode. You go around behind Prometheus. You find the balance-lines. You cut them. You give us our chance to put the thing on its back, and you do it without ever once being seen."

Don Juan sprang up from his chair, leaned over the dark red figure, and his eyes went wide as a boy's at a travelling fair. "Does it run faster than a horse?"

"Three times faster than a horse."

"Can I steal a few girls out of the Prometheus knight-pod while I'm back there?"

"No."

"Oh."

Franco lifted the last figure and held it a moment longer than he had held the others. The lamp-light slid off it the way light slides off still water. Mirror-black. No obvious strength. No obvious weakness. A cold, even balance ran through it from crown to foot, the way a drawn sword is balanced from pommel to tip.

"Sword Dancer. Unmanned reserve. Remote-piloted by Thalassa from the basement relay. The instant a brother falls on the line, Sword Dancer steps into his place. It is built for balance — no flaw, no edge, no glory. It is our last insurance. We pray we do not have to use it."

The pod was quiet for a few seconds after he set the model down. Five small figures stood in a row on the tray, each one a promise or a debt.

Cesare picked up the small card on which Franco had written the names and the assignments, and read it for a while. The card was light. The ink on it was heavy.

"Lund was right," he said, quietly.

Everyone looked at him.

His voice was low, almost to himself, the way a man speaks when he has just understood a thing he has been refusing to understand for a long time.

"I was not born to be a thing of vengeance. I was born to protect you. To protect Adele. To protect anyone willing to follow me."

No one spoke.

Bielle turned her face away, and the quick, hard blink she made was the only tell. Quintilian lowered his head. Don Juan, after a moment, put the small leather notebook quietly away in his breast pocket, as if he had suddenly been embarrassed by the weight of it. Alfonso let out a breath that was almost a sigh, and something almost gentle passed, for the first time, across his eyes.

Veronella said nothing at all. She simply took the empty cup from his hand and set it aside.

"I will fight tomorrow, as well."

Valentina's voice came from the foot of the bunk — clear, level, not to be argued with.

Everyone turned.

The golden-haired princess stood up from the folding chair. The point of Dragon's Tooth rested on the deck beside her foot. Her face was calm, but something in the look of her would not take no for an answer. There was a steadiness in the way she held herself that had nothing to do with training, and everything to do with the long, slow business of becoming the kind of person who says a thing and means it.

"In a Seraphim temporarily refitted from the Wrath of God III pattern," she said. "Cesare's Immortal King mode has only five minutes of endurance. After the five minutes are spent, I take the line."

"Your Highness—" Quintilian began.

"Call me Valentina." She cut him off, gently, as one might cut a child's hand away from a hot stove. "Or call me Deputy Commander. On a battlefield I am a knight first, and a princess nowhere. The order of those two things is fixed."

Cesare looked at her. For a few seconds he said nothing. There was a kind of pale, slow wonder in the way he looked at her, the way a man looks at a wall he has just realized is load-bearing.

"...Thank you."

"You owe me," she said, and smiled — the first real smile any of them had seen on her face in a week.

He reached under the thin pillow for the ring set with the black opal, and began to slide it back onto his finger —

and at that moment the speaker set into the ceiling of the rest pod crackled awake.

"Outer gate post to underground three." A flat, mechanical woman's voice, the voice of a thing that did not sleep, said out into the chamber. "The foreign minister of the Kingdom of Zhongshan, a Mr. Yesuli, requests entry to the outer perimeter of the Minerva Institute. He states that he carries a personal gift for Lord Cesare Borgia. Master Franco, do you grant passage? Please advise."

The temperature in the pod changed.

It was not a thing one could measure. It was a thing one could feel — the small, sharp drop, the way a room feels colder the instant a window is opened over a winter street.

"Yesuli?" Quintilian frowned, sitting up straighter against the wall. "That would be the Eastern minister. The one with the wolf flag."

"The same Easterner who had his man slip into our outer perimeter last time," Alfonso said, his voice flat as a struck coin. "The same one we did not catch."

Cesare said nothing.

He was thinking of Marston.

Of a city he had left a long time ago. Of a rain-soaked cathedral, and a man kneeling before him in the stone cold of the nave, a man who had given his name as an exiled noble of the East. Of the flag the man had carried in beside him — purple field, white wolf's head, the wolf of Zhongshan, the wolf of a kingdom that did not, as far as anyone in the room knew, exist on any map drawn west of the Serpentine.

The first Easterner Cesare had ever seen in Marston.

The first Easterner, also, to kneel before him in a foreign church and say, in a voice that shook only a little: I would die for you, my general.

Slowly, with the careful, deliberate motion of a man who is aware that he is being watched, Cesare drew the patch from over his eye and set it down on the thin pillow.

The violet of his iris looked strangely clear in the white of the lamp. He looked up at the speaker set above his head, and his face was quiet — not peaceful, not yet, but quiet, in the way a drawn blade is quiet.

"Let him in," he said.

There was a small pause in the room, the kind of pause that follows a sentence one is not sure one has heard correctly.

Then, more softly, he added:

"Everyone. Have your weapons ready."

Bielle's hand, very quietly, found the small knife she kept at her belt. Her fingers did not tremble. Hers, it seemed, had learned how not to.
