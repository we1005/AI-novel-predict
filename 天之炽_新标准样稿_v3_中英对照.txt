第157章 叹息之墙的归巢

礼车在密涅瓦机关侧门停稳的时候，雨已经停了。翡冷翠郊外的空气湿冷，带着松脂和铁锈的味道，那扇巨大的黑色机械门在雾气中沉默地矗立着，门面上的六翼猫头鹰徽记在夜色里几乎看不清，只有门缝里透出的青蓝色蒸汽灯光像一道刀痕。

"到了。"瓦莲京娜把车停好，转过头来看着西泽尔，粉色的发带从她肩头滑落，她没有伸手去接，"明天在战场等你证明自己是盖世英雄。"

她说得轻描淡写，像是在说一件理所当然的事。西泽尔点了点头，没有多说什么。该说的在路上已经说过了，瓦莲京娜不需要感激，也不需要承诺，她是那种替你打开一扇门就转身离开的人。

"公主殿下，"西泽尔低声说，"谢谢。"

瓦莲京娜微笑，那笑容在夜色里很淡，像冰河上第一道裂痕。她发动了引擎，礼车在原地转了个弯，朝来路驶去。尾灯越来越远，像两只红色的萤火虫，最终消失在雾里。

唐璜吹了声口哨："老板，我开始理解你为什么要遵守那桩婚约了。"

"闭嘴。"西泽尔说。

他们面前就是叹息之墙。碧儿早已等在门边的雨棚下，她穿着密涅瓦机关的工程师白大褂，怀里抱着一只铁皮箱子，头发被雨雾打湿了几缕，贴在苍白的脸颊上。看到他们，碧儿的眼睛亮了一下，随即又恢复了那种平静的、带着轻微责备的神色。

"你们迟到了七分钟。"她说。

"路上有点小插曲。"唐璜耸肩。

碧儿没有追问，她把铁皮箱打开，里面是叠得整整齐齐的军装和几块用油纸包好的面包，面包的温度透过油纸传出来，带着烘烤的香气。

"坎特伯雷堡连夜烤的，"碧儿说，"知道你不喜欢太甜的面包，我让他们少放了糖。军装是干净的，已经熨过了。"

她说着，从箱底又抽出一件叠好的黑色长大衣，披在西泽尔肩上。大衣是干燥的，带着一股淡淡的皂角味，是那种只有很讲究的人家才会用的老式皂角。

"你们都湿透了。"碧儿皱眉看着他们。

叹息之墙的巨大机械门缓缓开启，门后站着两名荷枪实弹的密涅瓦机关警卫，枪口低垂，眼神警惕。李锡尼站在门内侧的阴影中，黑天使军徽在蒸汽灯下泛着冷光，他面无表情地打量着西泽尔一行人，目光在西泽尔身上多停留了一秒。

"确认身份，"李锡尼的声音平淡如常，"西泽尔·博尔吉亚，旧日三骑士，以及……"他看向碧儿。

"碧儿·拉瑟，密涅瓦机关工程师。"碧儿平静地说。

李锡尼点头，他侧身让开，同时对警卫做了个手势："放行。"

西泽尔走进叹息之墙的瞬间，墙壁两侧的安检装置同时亮起了刺目的红色警报。机械臂从墙壁中伸出，扫描光束从头到脚扫过西泽尔的全身。安检面板上跳出一连串跳动的数字，最终定格在一个让所有人都沉默了的读数上。

"检测到残留红伞级能量信号，"机械女声不带感情地播报，"危险等级：S。建议立即上报。"

李锡尼的眼神微微一变，他走到安检面板前看了一眼，然后转向西泽尔。

"红伞？"他问，语气里没有质问，只有确认。

"昨夜的事。"西泽尔说，没有解释更多。

李锡尼沉默了几秒，他伸手在安检面板上输入了一串密码，红色警报变成了黄色，然后熄灭。墙壁两侧的机械臂缩了回去。

"暂时压下，"李锡尼说，"但我会如实上报枢机会。你的解释时间不多了。"

他说完转身走向侧门，经过西泽尔身边时，视线在西泽尔的后颈上停留了一瞬——那个位置还缠着昨夜仓促包扎的绷带，绷带下隐约可见金属的光泽。

"佛朗哥总长在等你。"李锡尼说完便消失在走廊尽头。

西泽尔没有回头，他知道李锡尼看到了什么。

叹息之墙后面的走廊很长，青蓝色的蒸汽灯把所有人的影子拉得很长。墙壁是粗糙的岩石，每隔几步就有一扇铁门，门上标着编号和危险等级。走廊尽头的铁门敞开着，门后是一间不大的临时医疗室，消毒水的气味扑面而来。

佛朗哥教授站在房间中央，面前是一张铺着白布的金属台，旁边摆着各种精密的医疗器械。他穿着一件洗得发白的旧衬衫，袖口卷到肘部，露出的手臂上布满了烫伤和机械划伤的旧疤。冰盒打开着，里面是几支密封在真空管中的金色细针，针尖在蒸汽灯下泛着微光。

"你来了。"佛朗哥看到西泽尔，疲惫的眼睛里闪过一丝光亮，"坐。"

西泽尔在金属台边坐下。碧儿已经换好了白大褂，她把一卷干净的毛巾搭在手臂上，拧开一瓶眼药水放在手边。

薇若兰从隔壁房间走出来，她穿着机械外骨骼的支架，手里提着一台便携式神经接驳仪。接驳仪的指示灯在闪烁，发出微弱的嗡嗡声。她看了一眼西泽尔后颈的绷带，嘴角微微下撇。

"准备工作235适配款套管，"佛朗哥从冰盒里取出一支金色细针，针身比普通型号略长，尾端连着一小段柔韧的黄金套管，"时间紧迫，只能用这个。神经耦合度会比正式版低百分之三到五，但足够你撑过明天的战斗。"

"多久？"西泽尔问。

"二十分钟。"佛朗哥说，"忍一下。"

碧儿走到西泽尔身侧，她没有说话，只是把毛巾搭在肩上，伸出左手轻轻按住西泽尔的肩膀。佛朗哥解开绷带，露出昨夜留下的伤口——皮肤被红水银能量灼伤后留下的焦黑痕迹，中央是细小的金属探针留下的孔洞。佛朗哥用酒精棉仔细擦拭伤口周围，然后示意薇若兰启动接驳仪。

"开始。"薇若兰说。

接驳仪发出低沉的嗡鸣，电流沿着导线流入黄金套管，套管开始微微发热。西泽尔的身体轻微地绷紧了一下，后颈传来一阵刺骨的酸麻，仿佛有一根极细的针沿着脊椎缓缓推进。碧儿的左手收紧了一点，她的目光紧紧盯着西泽尔的脸，一旦看到他的眉头微微皱起，就立刻用毛巾擦去他额头渗出的冷汗。

"耦合度上升……百分之六十二……"薇若兰盯着接驳仪上的读数，"再推一点……"

"够了。"佛朗哥说。

他关闭了接驳仪，从工具盒里取出一管速干医用胶，小心翼翼地涂在伤口周围。黄金套管的尾端已经从皮肤下微微凸出，像一枚埋在肌肉中的金色铆钉。

"好了。"佛朗哥说，"起来活动一下。"

西泽尔站起身，后颈传来一阵微弱的温热感，和一种奇异的、仿佛有电流在皮肤下缓缓流动的触感。他活动了一下脖颈，黄金套管与神经末梢的耦合已经建立，反应速度比以前迟钝了零点几秒，但还在可接受的范围内。

"谢谢。"西泽尔说。

"别谢我，"佛朗哥疲惫地揉了揉眼睛，"明天的战斗才是正事。我这把老骨头能做的都做了，剩下的要靠你自己。"

碧儿递给西泽尔一件干净的黑色军装，西泽尔走进隔壁的更衣间，脱下昨夜被雨水和汗水浸透的衣服。新军装很合身，面料挺括，肩章是密涅瓦机关的六翼猫头鹰，领口绣着代表炽天骑士团身份的金色火焰纹路。

西泽尔对着更衣间的镜子整理衣领，他的目光扫过后颈——绷带下的黄金套管在镜中泛着冷光，同时映入眼帘的还有胸口挂着的那枚银色项链坠，坠子里的照片已经有些模糊了，但还能看清一个年轻女人的侧脸。

他用手指轻轻摸了摸后颈的凸起，金属的温度透过指尖传来，冰冷而坚硬。

碧儿在更衣间门口等着，手里端着一杯热茶。她帮西泽尔系好领口最后一个扣子时，目光忽然停在了他的眼睛上。

西泽尔的瞳孔是深紫色的，那双紫瞳在蒸汽灯下显得格外深邃。但碧儿看到，在紫色深处偶尔有极淡的纹路闪过，像岩浆在雪地下面流动，转瞬即逝。

碧儿没有声张，她从口袋里掏出那瓶眼药水，递给西泽尔。

"眼睛进灰了。"她说。

西泽尔接过眼药水，点了点头。

"谢谢。"

隔壁房间传来金属碰撞的声音。阿方索坐在改装室的角落，面前摆着拆到一半的雷霆牙零件和各色工具。他左手拿着焊枪，右手扶着一根被剖开的新龙炎备用枪管，枪管的内壁已经被他打磨出了精密的凹槽。焊枪的蓝色火焰在金属表面跳动，火星四溅。

昆提良从门口探进头来："阿方索，碧儿姐姐说饭好了。"

"等一下。"阿方索头也不抬。

昆提良看了他一眼，又看了看他手里那些乱七八糟的零件，耸了耸肩，转身走了。他没有留在改装室等阿方索，而是穿过走廊拐进了一间挂着"后勤管理处"铜牌的小办公室。推开门，暖黄色的灯光照在一堆堆码得整整齐齐的金属箱上，一个头发灰白、脸上带着细密皱纹的中年男人正伏在桌上写什么。

"塔拉夏。"昆提良敲了敲门框。

中年男人抬起头，看到昆提良那张过于高大的身影堵在门口，先是愣了一下，然后露出一种又惊又喜的表情："昆提良？你怎么……你不是被……"他站起来绕过桌子，上下打量着昆提良，像在确认面前这个是不是真人。

"说来话长。"昆提良大步走进来，他在塔拉夏面前站定，这个从炽天骑士团后勤组转业到密涅瓦机关的老熟人，当年帮他修过不止一次甲胄。昆提良不喜欢绕弯子，他直截了当地说，"帮我打听个事。"

"你说。"塔拉夏把椅子推过来。

"密涅瓦有没有自己的附属医院？"

塔拉夏微微一怔，显然没料到他问的是这个："有。在鹰巢地下三层，专供密涅瓦的甲胄骑士和技术人员使用，设备比翡冷翠任何一家教会医院都齐全，神经修复和外科都在行。你问这个……是有人受伤了？"

昆提良沉默了一秒。他的目光落在窗外走廊尽头的那间特护舱——艾莲被安置在那里，身上还带着铁丝勒出的伤痕和劣质酒精灼烧的红斑。那个男人把铁丝套在她脖子上的时候，他的心脏像是被什么东西死死攥住，到现在还没松开。

"我需要一张通行证，"昆提良说，"能让外面的人用密涅瓦的医疗设备。"

塔拉夏看了他两眼，没有问那个人是谁。炽天骑士团出来的老兵都懂，有些事不该问就不要问。他拉开抽屉翻了翻，抽出一张空白的临时医疗许可证，在上面写了几行字，盖上后勤处的钢印。

"明早六点之前带着去地下三层前台登记就行。"塔拉夏把许可证递过去，又从柜子里取出一只纸包塞到昆提良手里，"这个也拿着，上次送来的军用急救药包，里面有神经镇静剂和烧伤软膏。"

昆提良接过东西，点了点头。他转身要走，在门口停了一下。

"塔拉夏。"

"嗯？"

"谢了。"

"回来就好。"塔拉夏说，目光里有一点复杂的东西一闪而过，"活着回来就好。"

昆提良没有再说什么，他攥着许可证和药包大步走向走廊尽头，推开特护舱的门。艾莲坐在床边，脖子上缠着临时绷带，看到他进来，那双疲惫的眼睛里亮了一点微弱的光。

昆提良在她身边坐下，把药包放在床头柜上。

"明天一早我带你去密涅瓦的附属医院，"他说，声音比平时低了一些，"比外面那些教会医院强。"

艾莲点了点头，她的嘴唇动了动，似乎想说什么，最后只是轻轻地"嗯"了一声。

昆提良伸手，把她被汗湿的碎发拢到耳后，动作笨拙却极轻，像是怕碰碎什么易碎的东西。

改装室的角落里，焊枪的蓝色火焰还在跳动。阿方索重新坐回工具台前，拿起那根被剖开的新龙炎备用枪管，继续他的工作。他的动作很慢，每一下焊接都极其小心，仿佛在雕琢一件艺术品。枪管内壁的凹槽越来越深，形状越来越像雷霆牙的炮膛结构。

走廊上传来的脚步声打断了他的节奏。阿方索抬起头，看到李锡尼从叹息之墙的方向走过来，脚步比刚才略快——他刚刚确认了外围警戒的撤岗情况，正要回枢机会复命。

阿方索放下焊枪，从口袋里摸出那把黑色十字纹蜂巢手铳，在手里攥了两秒。他站起来走到改装室门口，看着李锡尼走近。

"李锡尼副官。"阿方索叫住他。

李锡尼停下脚步，目光落在阿方索手里的铳上。那把铳的十字纹蜂巢花纹在蒸汽灯下泛着暗哑的金属光泽，握把磨得发亮，是被长年使用过的痕迹。

阿方索沉默了两秒，嘴唇抿成一条线。然后他把铳递了过去。

"帮我保管。"他说，没有解释更多。

李锡尼接过手铳，他低头看了一眼铳身，又抬眼看向阿方索。他的目光在阿方索脸上停了不到一秒，然后移向走廊另一头——那里是临时医疗室的方向，西泽尔刚刚从那里出来。

"圣堂装甲师那边的动向如何？"阿方索问。

"暂稳。"李锡尼说。他把手铳收进腰间的枪套——那个枪套里原本就有东西，但空位刚好够塞下这把铳。他没有多问，也没有打开检查，只是朝改装室的方向敬了个半礼。

"替我告诉西泽尔，"阿方索说，"我会在他身边。"

李锡尼点头，转身走进走廊深处的阴影中，脚步声渐渐远去。

阿方索重新坐回工具台前。他拿起焊枪，蓝色的火焰再次跳动起来，照亮了他沉默的脸。

走廊另一头的临时休息舱里，碧儿正帮西泽尔换上最后一件干净的内衣。她的动作很轻，手指碰到西泽尔肋骨处那道还未完全愈合的伤疤时，微微顿了一下。

"这是什么时候弄的？"她低声问。

"训练的时候。"西泽尔说。

碧儿没有再问，她把干净的绷带仔细地缠好，又在伤疤上敷了一层薄薄的药膏。她的手指在西泽尔的皮肤上停留了一秒，然后收回。

"好了。"她说。

西泽尔站起身，碧儿递给他一个纸包，里面是还带着余温的面包。

"吃一点再睡。"碧儿说，"明天的战斗需要体力。"

西泽尔接过面包，咬了一口。面包是咸味的，带着迷迭香的气息，是坎特伯雷堡老厨娘的手艺。碧儿看着他吃完，才收拾好东西准备离开。

走到门口时，她忽然回过头。

"西泽尔。"

"嗯？"

"……没什么。"碧儿说，"早点休息。"

她关上了门。

休息舱很小，只有一张行军床和一盏可调亮度的蒸汽灯。西泽尔坐在床边，把口袋里那张昨夜仓促间被姑姑塞进来的字条掏了出来。字条被汗水浸得有些模糊，上面的字迹潦草，是用铅笔写的，墨迹断断续续。

他之前一直没有时间看。

字条上写着几行字，大部分是关于密涅瓦机关内部某些人的警告，以及一个地名——"骷髅地"。字条的最后一行的字迹比前面更轻，几乎看不清，西泽尔把蒸汽灯调亮了一点，凑近了看。

"姑姑在骷髅地等你确认真相。"

"教授，我有一个请求。"

"说。"

"如果寒玉嵌入的技术能成功，"瓦莲京娜说，"请把西泽尔·博尔吉亚作为第一个试验者。"

佛朗哥看了西泽尔一眼。

"为什么？"

"因为他是红龙。"瓦莲京娜说，"红龙的神经反噬是所有炽天使适格者中最严重的，只有他最需要这个技术。"

佛朗哥沉默了几秒。

"我考虑一下。"

"教授，我没有时间等您考虑。"瓦莲京娜说，"明天他就要上战场了。"

佛朗哥看了她很久，然后缓缓点了点头。

"好。"

瓦莲京娜的嘴角终于浮起一个真正的笑容。

"谢谢您，教授。"

她转向西泽尔。

"西泽尔，能借一步说话吗？"

西泽尔站起身，跟着瓦莲京娜走出办公室。薇若兰在他们身后把门轻轻带上。

走廊里灯光昏暗，蒸汽管道低悬在头顶。瓦莲京娜的军靴踩在铁板上，发出清脆的嗒嗒声。

"你的母亲，"瓦莲京娜忽然开口，没有回头，"你真的不想知道更多吗？"

西泽尔的脚步顿了一下。

"你知道什么？"

"我知道的不多，"瓦莲京娜说，"但我母亲在世时曾经提到过一件事。"

她停下脚步，转过身来。灰蓝色的眼睛直视着西泽尔。

"她说，琳琅夫人的手术不是意外。"

西泽尔的瞳孔收缩了。

"谁告诉她的？"

"我母亲没有说。"瓦莲京娜说，"她只说了一句话——'那个女人脑子里藏着一把钥匙，有人想让她永远忘记钥匙插在哪里。'"

走廊里安静得能听到蒸汽管里水流的声响。

"我母亲说这句话的那天晚上就难产去世了。"瓦莲京娜说，"我一直不确定这两件事之间有没有关联。"

她从工装口袋里取出一枚银戒指，塞进西泽尔的手里。戒指的表面嵌着一小块几乎透明的玉石，米粒大小，指尖触及的瞬间，一股冰凉的触感从掌心蔓延开来。

"见面礼，"瓦莲京娜说，"也是保命的小东西。"

"这是什么？"

"冰原寒玉。"瓦莲京娜说，"我母亲留下的，一共只有三块，一块做实验用了，剩下两块。一块在我自己身上，另一块给你。"

她握了握西泽尔的手指，让戒指更紧地贴着他的掌心。

"戴着它上战场。它能缓冲你百分之三到五的神经反噬。"

"百分之三到五？"

"不要小看这百分之三到五，"瓦莲京娜说，"对于红龙来说，这可能是你站着走出战场和被人抬着走出战场的区别。"

她松开手，转身准备离开。

"瓦莲京娜。"西泽尔叫住她。

"嗯？"

"……谢谢。"

瓦莲京娜回头看了他一眼，灰蓝色的眼睛里有什么东西一闪而过。

"别谢我，"她说，"赢给我看就行。"

她迈着军靴的嗒嗒声走进走廊深处，没有回头。

西泽尔站在原地，把那枚戒指套在右手的中指上。银环的尺寸刚刚好，像是量着他的手指打造的。寒玉在蒸汽灯的光线下折射出微弱的冷光，冰凉的触感沿着金属导体传遍全身。

他转身走回佛朗哥的办公室。推门进去时，他看到佛朗哥已经把瓦莲京娜的图纸收好，桌上铺着另一张图纸——很大，几乎覆盖了整张办公桌的表面。

阿方索不知什么时候也到了，正站在桌边，双手抱胸。昆提良和唐璜坐在旁边的椅子上。还有一个三十来岁的男人，穿着密涅瓦机关的通讯兵制服，胸口别着银色的通讯兵徽章。

"塔拉夏，"昆提良介绍道，"今晚的联络员。"

塔拉夏向西泽尔行了个军礼。

"人齐了。"佛朗哥从桌下抽出一样东西，铺在图纸上。

那是一张地图。纸张发黄，边缘被时间磨出了毛边，标注的字体是密涅瓦机关内部的老式密码。地图上画着密密麻麻的等高线，标注着矿井深度、通风井位置、支撑柱分布。

"这是维苏威火山底部废弃矿道的地图。"佛朗哥说，"四十年前前任总长封闭骷髅地时留下的档案。"

西泽尔凑近去看。矿道像一张巨大的蛛网，从维苏威火山的山腰一直延伸到山体最深处。最深处画着一个黑色方框，旁边用红笔标注着"骷髅地"三个字。

"明天的对抗战，"佛朗哥说，"就选在这里。"

唐璜吹了声口哨。

"教授，废弃矿道？那种地方甲胄都展不开。"

"正因为展不开，"佛朗哥说，"才适合你们。"

他的手指点在地图的一个位置——矿道的一个丁字路口。

"第三代普罗米修斯·古洛诺斯身高七米以上，在普通地形上是不可阻挡的钢铁巨兽，但在废弃矿道里，它的体型反而成了劣势。矿道宽度只有九米，层高不到十二米，古洛诺斯的巨刃在矿道里挥不开。"

"它的反应速度呢？"阿方索开口。

"古洛诺斯的电信号从驾驶舱传到四肢末端需要零点三秒的延迟。"佛朗哥说，"在开阔地带可以忽略，在矿道里，零点三秒足够一个剑舞型骑士完成三次突刺。"

唐璜的眼睛亮了。

"所以战术是把它拖进矿道？"

"不是拖，是引诱。"佛朗哥摇头，"古洛诺斯的火力优势在远程，它的背部搭载多门火炮，可以在远处轰击矿道入口。我打算让你们先进矿道潜伏，它追进去之后，你们利用复杂地形分割它的注意力。西泽尔正面牵制，阿方索用狙击火力压制它的远程武器，昆提良挡住正面突进，唐璜找机会刺杀骑士舱。"

"我负责什么？"塔拉夏问。

"通讯和撤退路线的确认。"佛朗哥在地图上画了一条红色虚线，"这条通风井直通山体外部，出口在翡冷翠南郊的废弃采石场。如果战局不利，你们就从这里撤。"

他顿了顿，看着西泽尔。

"但矿道深处有骷髅地。"

西泽尔没有说话。

"骷髅地的事，我管不了。"佛朗哥说，"那是前代总长封的禁区，钥匙在他手里，他五年前就死了。如果你们在战斗中被逼到那个方向，你们会看到一些不该看的东西。"

"我明白。"西泽尔说。

佛朗哥点了点头，把地图卷起来递给塔拉夏。

"五份底片，各人贴身保管，绝不能落入原罪机关手里。"

塔拉夏从胸口取出微缩照相机，"咔嚓"一声，地图被拍成底片。

就在佛朗哥把五份底片分发给众人时，楼下改装室的方向忽然传来一声清脆的金属断裂声。

"咔嚓——"

那声音在安静的夜里格外刺耳，像骨头折断。

西泽尔猛地站起身。薇若兰已经先一步拉开办公室的门，冲向楼梯口。改装室在三层楼板之下，他们沿着铁梯飞快地跑下去，蒸汽灯在头顶一盏盏闪过。

改装室的门半敞着，里面的灯亮得刺眼。

阿方索蹲在地上，手里捏着断成两截的零件。那是一截炮管的内衬管——暴力增强型雷霆牙的核心部件，此刻已经断成了整齐的两截，断面光滑如镜。

阿方索的脸埋在阴影里，看不清表情。

"什么时候断的？"薇若兰问。

"刚才。"阿方索的声音很平，"高温焊接的时候应力集中，金属疲劳了。"

他把手里的两截零件轻轻放在工作台上，站起身。

"备件不够。"

办公室里安静了几秒。唐璜小声骂了一句，昆提良攥紧了拳头。

"还有别的办法吗？"佛朗哥看向薇若兰。

薇若兰走到工作台前，仔细查看断口。她的眉头皱了起来。

"没有。"她说，"这种炮管内衬管是特制的，整间密涅瓦机关只有这一根。"

"原罪机关的……"

"来不及。"薇若兰摇头，"就算我们能搞到原罪机关的同类零件，最快也要十二个小时才能完成改装。"

西泽尔看着工作台上那两截断裂的炮管，脑子里快速计算着。没有暴力增强型雷霆牙，阿方索的火力就少了一半——不，不止一半，那不是普通的远程武器，那是能配合新型炽天使打出战术配合的关键火力点。

明天的对抗战，他们的火力配置被生生削掉了一角。

"阿方索。"西泽尔开口。

阿方索抬起头。

"你还能做什么？"

阿方索沉默了几秒。

"龙炎备用枪管。"他说，"我可以把龙炎枪管改造成适配新型炽天使的近程炮。射程会缩短百分之四十，精度会下降，但能打出足够的火力密度。"

"来得及吗？"

"六个小时。"阿方索说。

"去做。"西泽尔说。

阿方索点头，重新坐回工作台前。他拿起新的焊枪，蓝色火焰再次跳动起来，照亮了他沉默的脸。

西泽尔转身走回佛朗哥的办公室。

佛朗哥还站在桌边，手里拿着剩下的几份底片。他看到西泽尔回来，嘴唇动了动，似乎想说什么。

桌上的电话忽然响了。

铃声沉闷而急促，像丧钟。

佛朗哥皱了皱眉，伸手接起电话。

"喂。"

电话里传来急促的声音，西泽尔听不清在说什么，但能看到佛朗哥的脸色在一瞬间变得惨白。

"你说什么？"佛朗哥猛地站起身，老花镜差点从鼻梁上滑落，"再说一遍！"

电话里的声音更急促了。

佛朗哥的脸色从惨白变成铁青，又从铁青变成某种西泽尔从未见过的表情——像是恐惧，又像是愤怒，又像是一种深深的无力感。

"我知道了。"佛朗哥说，"让所有人提高警戒，原地待命，没有我的命令不得擅自行动。"

他挂上电话，手按在桌面上，整个人像被抽去了力气。

"教授？"塔拉夏问。

佛朗哥缓缓抬起头，看向西泽尔。

他的嘴唇动了动，似乎想说什么，又咽了回去。

然后他开口了。

"圣堂装甲师查抄了密涅瓦通往骷髅地的入口闸门。"

【English】
Chapter 158 — The Ice-Field Blueprint of Divine Wrath III

The steam-lamp died and Cesare lay a long time in the dark.

The iron frame of the campaign bed pressed a ridge into his spine. The pillow still carried the dry, soapy smell of the laundry. Somewhere far below, the great machinery of the Minerva Bureau kept its low, unhurried heartbeat — the Vesuvius furnace, beating like a second heart inside the mountain. He kept his eyes closed and rolled the scrap of paper between his fingers, over and over, the way a man rolls a coin. The pencil writing had bled under his sweat until the last line was nearly invisible. He did not need to read it. He had memorised every word.

Aunt is waiting at Golgotha. Come and confirm the truth.

Golgotha. The forbidden wing of the Minerva Bureau. A sealed chamber the previous Director-General had bricked up and locked with his own hand before he died, said to hold the twitching remnants of a heretical Seraph-class harness. How had his aunt got inside? What was she waiting for in there? And what truth, exactly, was he meant to confirm?

The truth about his mother.

Footsteps in the corridor. Soft-soled boots on iron plating. The rhythm paused at his door, then two small knocks.

"Cesare." Beryl's voice, thin through the seam of the door. "Vice-Director Violante says you are wanted in the Director-General's office in half an hour. There is a guest."

"What guest?"

"Someone from the Yenisei Embassy."

He was silent for two seconds. He folded the note small, slid it into the breast pocket of his shirt, against the skin.

"I understand."

Half an hour later, Cesare stepped into Professor Franco's office.

It sat at the heart of the Minerva Bureau's administrative wing, the four walls hung with cross-section schematics of every harness generation the Order had ever produced. The desk disappeared under stacks of technical dossiers taller than a man. Franco sat behind it, his reading glasses perched low on the bridge of his nose, examining a strip of microfilm. The enamel mug at his elbow held a liquid the colour of dark resin — some house tincture, the colour of a Chinese apothecary's brew.

"Sit," said Franco, without looking up. "The guest is coming."

Cesare sat on the leather settee. The leather had been worn pale in patches, and sighed under his weight. Three floors below, in the modification hall, the welding torch kept up its regular sibilance — a long hiss, a long hiss, a long hiss — Alfonso still adjusting the bore of the Thunder Fang.

A knock at the door.

Violante opened it herself. She was not wearing her cheongsam today. A dark-grey worker's coverall was buckled tight over her long frame, her hair pulled into a knot at the nape of her neck. The usual seductive softness was gone from her face; what was left was a kind of bladed alertness, the look of a knife just drawn from the sheath.

In the doorway stood a woman in a Minerva mechanic's coverall.

She was tall, the trouser cuffs turned up twice at the ankle. The coverall was a little too broad across her shoulders, and the shoulder-flaps had been unpicked with a small pair of scissors, as if the wearer had been at pains to erase her rank. But the boots gave her away. White regulation boots, the leather the colour of fresh snow — tundra-reindeer hide, the sort the workshops of no other nation in Christendom could produce. A mechanic's cap of grey wool had been pulled down over her hair, but from under the brim a pair of grey-blue eyes looked out, the colour of a cold moon over an empty steppe, and no disguise could put that colour out.

Valentina Romanov.

"Vice-Director Violante." Valentina extended a hand, her voice carrying the low, slow vowels of the far north.

"Princess Valentina." Violante took it.

Three seconds.

In those three seconds, neither woman spoke. Violante's hand was pale and long, the knuckles faintly stained with machine-oil. Valentina's was also pale, but bigger in the bone, the back of it marked with a few old white scars — the kind a harness leaves on a wearer after many years inside the cab.

"Alexander Grigori used to speak of you," said Valentina. "He said every gun you designed was a kind of poem."

"Alexander was too kind." Violante. "He was the one who really understood machines."

"He understood many things," Valentina said, and the smile did not move on her face. "For instance, he understood how to plant eyes inside the Minerva Bureau."

Violante's brow moved a fraction, then settled.

"The Princess is jesting. Security in the Minerva Bureau is the direct concern of the Tribunal of Heresy."

"Yes. He never did anything without reason." Valentina nodded. "Just as he did not, for no reason, bankroll a knight under sentence of outlawry and put him back in the saddle."

The air in the room congealed for half a second.

"He had his judgements," Violante said.

"Of course." Valentina. "He had his judgements. I have my mission."

She drew a roll of papers from inside her coverall and held it out to Violante. The roll was bound tight, wrapped in waterproof oiled paper, the knot tied in the regulation Yenisei military bow.

"These are partial technical drawings of the Divine Wrath Mark III," Valentina said. "My late mother annotated them herself. The details of the neural-link system are all inside."

Violante took the roll. Her fingers rested on the surface of the oiled paper for a moment.

"Her Highness the Tsarina of Yenisei."

"Yes." Valentina. "My mother was, in her lifetime, the technical adviser to the Divine Wrath Order. She oversaw the design of the Mark III neural-link from first sketch to finished harness. She died in the birthing-bed, and these drawings are what she left me."

Franco at last set down his magnifying glass.

"Open it."

Violante untied the knot and unrolled the drawings. They covered half the surface of the desk, dense with a bastard shorthand that mixed Cyrillic and Latin. The inks were of three colours. Blue for the original design. Red for later modifications. Green, a very pale, almost transparent green, the colour of lichen seen through melt-water.

"The green is my mother's hand," Valentina said. "She used a special ink pressed from tundra moss. It would only stay liquid below forty degrees of frost."

Franco bent close to the green annotations. Out of his clouded old eyes a sudden, alarming light shone.

"Glacial jade." He breathed the words. "She set glacial jade into the inner lining of the spinal harness."

"Yes." Valentina. "The reason the Mark III's neural backlash is reduced by forty per cent is those stones. The current of the linkage, when it passes through the jade layer, is buffered and cooled. It does not strike the rider's white matter at full force."

Franco's finger traced a green annotation. His lips trembled.

"The jade comes from the permafrost at the very north of the Yenisei tundra," Valentina said. "The yield is small. The conditions of mining are murderous. The whole kingdom, in a good year, extracts less than two pounds."

"So the Divine Wrath Order of Yenisei has the lowest neural-backlash rate of any knightly order in the world." Franco looked up. "Your knights can wear a harness into their thirties."

"Because my mother believed a knight should not be killed by his own weapon."

Silence in the office for a few seconds. The welding-torch from three floors below kept up its slow sibilant pulse — hiss, hiss, hiss.

"I will keep these drawings," Franco said. "I will study them through the night and see whether the embedding method can be carried over to the new Seraph harnesses."

"Professor. I have a request."

"Say it."

"If the jade-embedding succeeds," Valentina said, "let Cesare Borgia be the first test subject."

Franco glanced at Cesare.

"Why?"

"Because he is the Red Dragon." Valentina. "Of every Seraph-compatible candidate in the world, the Red Dragon suffers the worst neural backlash. Only he truly needs this technology."

Franco was silent a moment.

"I will think on it."

"Professor, I have not the time for you to think on it." Valentina. "He rides into battle tomorrow."

Franco looked at her for a long time, then slowly nodded.

"Good."

The corner of Valentina's mouth at last lifted into something that was, at last, a real smile.

"Thank you, Professor."

She turned to Cesare.

"Cesare. May I have a word with you, in private?"

Cesare stood, and followed Valentina out of the office. Behind him, Violante closed the door softly.

The corridor was dim. Steam pipes ran low along the ceiling. The iron plates rang under the hard white heels of Valentina's boots, a clear bright tap, tap, tap, fading into the distance.

"Your mother," Valentina said, suddenly, without turning her head. "Don't you really want to know more?"

Cesare's step faltered for a half-beat.

"What do you know?"

"Very little." Valentina. "But my mother, in her lifetime, said one thing to me."

She stopped, and turned. Her grey-blue eyes fixed on his.

"She said the surgery on Lady Linlang was no accident."

Cesare's pupils narrowed.

"Who told her that?"

"My mother did not say." Valentina. "She said only one sentence. 'There is a key in that woman's head, and someone wanted her to forget, forever, where the key is meant to go.'"

The corridor was quiet enough to hear the water moving in the steam pipes overhead.

"The night my mother said that sentence," Valentina said, "she went into the birthing-bed and did not come out. I have never been sure, until today, whether the two things were connected."

From her coverall pocket she took a silver ring, and pressed it into Cesare's palm. The ring was set with a small piece of stone almost transparent, no larger than a grain of rice. The instant his fingertip touched it, a coldness, thin and precise as a needle, ran from the centre of his palm to the tips of his fingers.

"A meeting-gift," Valentina said. "And a small thing that may keep you alive."

"What is it?"

"Glacial jade." Valentina. "A piece of what my mother left. There were only three pieces in the world. One was used in the experiment. The other two she kept. One is on me, now. The other is for you."

She closed his fingers tighter over the ring, pressed the silver band harder against the lines of his palm.

"Wear it into battle. It will buffer between three and five per cent of your neural backlash."

"Three or five per cent?"

"Do not look down on three or five per cent." Valentina. "For the Red Dragon, that small number may be the difference between walking out of the field on your own feet and being carried out on a stretcher."

She let go of his hand, and turned to go.

"Valentina."

"Yes?"

". . . Thank you."

She looked back at him once. Something moved in the grey-blue of her eyes, and was gone.

"Do not thank me," she said. "Win for me, and that is thanks enough."

She walked down the corridor in the bright tap of her white boots, and did not look back.

Cesare stood still in the dim light, and slid the ring onto the middle finger of his right hand. The fit of the silver band was exact, as though the jeweller had measured his finger in advance. The jade caught the nearest steam-lamp and threw back a thin, cold glint. The chill of it ran up the metal and into him, all the way to the shoulder.

He turned, and walked back to Franco's office.

When he pushed the door open, Franco had already put Valentina's drawings away, and a different drawing now lay on the desk — a great one, almost covering the whole surface.

Alfonso had arrived at some point, and stood with his arms folded. Quentin and Don Juan sat in the chairs against the wall. A fourth man was there, about thirty years old, in the dark blue uniform of a Minerva Bureau signaller, with a silver comms-badge pinned to his chest.

"Taras," said Quentin, by way of introduction. "Tonight's man on the wire."

Taras saluted.

"Everyone is here." Franco drew something from beneath the desk, and laid it on top of the schematic.

Crack.

"No time." Violante shook her head. "Even if we could lay hands on a comparable part from their stores, the re-fitting alone would take twelve hours."

Cesare looked at the two halves of the liner on the bench, and his mind ran the calculation at speed. Without the violence-augmented Thunder Fang, Alfonso's firepower was cut by half. For the morning's duel, a third of their firepower had been cut clean out from under them.

"Alfonso," said Cesare.

Alfonso looked up.

"What can you still do?"

Alfonso was silent a few seconds.

"The Dragonflame reserve barrel," he said. "I can refit the Dragonflame barrel to the new Seraph as a short-range gun. The range drops by forty per cent, the accuracy falls, but the volume of fire can be kept high enough."

"Six hours," said Alfonso.

Alfonso nodded, and sat back down at the bench. He picked up the torch. A blue flame leapt out, and lit, from below, the silent mask of his face.

Cesare turned, and went back up the three flights to Franco's office.

Franco was still at the desk, holding the remaining film copies in his hand. He saw Cesare come in. His lips moved as if to speak, then closed.

The telephone on the desk rang.

The bell of the telephone was a low, urgent sound, the sound of a passing bell.

Franco's brow tightened. He reached out, and lifted the receiver.

"Speaking."

There was a rapid voice at the other end. Cesare could not make out the words, but he could see the colour leave Franco's face. The old skin, in a single instant, went the colour of tallow.

"What did you say?" Franco was on his feet, his glasses sliding half off his nose. "Say that again."

The voice at the other end went faster.

The colour of Franco's face went from tallow to iron, and from iron into an expression Cesare had never seen in him — something that was, at the same time, terror, and fury, and a deep, old, helpless weariness.

"I understand." Franco. "Put every man on high alert. Hold in place. No one moves without my order."

He set the receiver down. His hand stayed on the desk, and for a moment he looked like a man with the bones gone out of him.

"Professor?" said Taras.

Franco slowly raised his head, and looked at Cesare.

His lips moved, as if to say something, and the words caught in his throat.

Then he spoke.

"The Cathedral Armored Division has seized the Minerva Bureau's gate to Golgotha."

那金属板不大，大约两个巴掌大，厚度不到一厘米，表面是纯银色的，在灯光下泛着冷冽的光泽。

"这是什么？"

"密涅瓦初代总长留下的。"李锡尼说，"完美焊料。"

阿方索的眼睛亮了。

暴力增强型雷霆牙的核心内衬管因为金属疲劳断裂，密涅瓦机关没有备用备件。如果有了这块完美焊料……

"它可以修复任何金属的断裂。"李锡尼说，"这是初代总长留下的最后一块，从未使用过。"

他顿了顿。

"我本来应该把它上交给枢机会。"

阿方索愣住了。

李锡尼把金属板放在工作台上，转身准备离开。

"李锡尼副局长。"阿方索叫住他。

李锡尼停下脚步，没有回头。

"谢谢。"

"不用谢我。"李锡尼说，"我只是……做了我该做的事。"

他的脚步声渐渐远去，消失在改装室外的走廊里。

阿方索看着工作台上的那块纯银金属板，眼睛里闪烁着一种近乎虔诚的光。

"完美焊料。"他喃喃自语。

他伸出手，拿起那块金属板，走到暴力增强型雷霆牙的核心内衬管前。

他把纯银金属板贴到内衬管断裂处的瞬间——

一道金色的光芒突然从金属板里迸发出来。

那光芒耀眼得让人睁不开眼睛，几乎照亮了整个改装室，把蓝色的焊枪火焰都压得黯淡无光。

阿方索猛地后退一步，差点摔倒在地。

他抬起头，看到那块纯银金属板正在发光，温暖的金色光芒从金属板的内部透出来，像是有什么东西在金属板里苏醒。那光芒不是反射，而是从金属板的核心处向外涌出，带着一种奇异的脉动，像是心跳。

他下意识地回头看向西泽尔。

西泽尔站在窗边，后颈的黄金套管也在发光。

金色的光芒从套管里透出来，和金属板上的光芒一模一样。

两道金色的光芒在改装室里交织在一起，像是两条沉睡的龙同时睁开了眼睛。那光芒带着一种古老而神圣的脉动，仿佛来自百年前那批最初被锻造的密涅瓦之魂，在呼唤着它们最后的继承者。

阿方索呆呆地站在原地，手里的金属板正在发出柔和的嗡鸣声。

他不知道那是什么。

但他后颈的汗毛一根根竖了起来。

他猛地意识到，这块初代总长留下的"完美焊料"——它从来就不是什么普通的金属。

它和西泽尔后颈的黄金套管，和密涅瓦机关百年来最核心的秘密，有着某种他说不清道不明的联系。

他抬起头，看向窗边的西泽尔。

西泽尔站在那里，脸色苍白，紫瞳深处那金色的纹路正在缓缓流动，像是被某种力量唤醒。他没有看阿方索，只是低头看着自己手中的那半块Excalibur碎片。

改装室里的金色光芒越来越亮，几乎要溢出门缝。

阿方索握紧了手里的金属板，他不知道明天的对抗战会发生什么，但他知道——

这绝不是一块普通的焊料。

而西泽尔后颈的黄金套管，也绝不是普通的神经接驳装置。

明天的战场上，会发生什么，没有人能预料。

【English】
Chapter 159 — The Broken Sword and the Fragment

Francesco said it very quietly.

"The Holy Chapel Armored Division has sealed the Golgotha gate out of Minerva."

The office went so still that the lamp wick could be heard hissing against the brass collar of the oil. Tarashe had been holding a strip of developed film; it slipped from her fingers, scattered across the flagstones, and she made no move to gather it. She only looked at Francesco. Cesare stood at the window, his back to the room, the dark of the Florentine hills cut sharp against the glass. Whatever his face was doing, Francesco could not see it.

"Golgotha is closed," Francesco said. He lowered himself back into the chair, settled his palms flat on the desk, and made himself meet the question Cesare had not asked. "The road to the truth has been cut."

Cesare nodded.

His aunt's note was still in his breast pocket, folded twice. *Come to Golgotha. Come and see for yourself.* He had read it on the train, and again in the corridor, and again while Francesco was speaking. He had been about to go. Now he could not.

There was a battle in the morning.

"Professor," Cesare said, without turning. "The engagement tomorrow. The plan does not change."

Francesco raised his head. The boy at the window was very white. The violet of his eyes caught the lamplight in a way Francesco had never quite learned to look at directly.

"It does not change," he said, "because we have nothing to change it for."

Cesare walked out of the office.

At the far end of the corridor the Minerva workshop was still lit.

Alfonso was at the bench. The spare barrel of the Dragonflame was clamped in the vise, and the blue tip of the soldering torch jumped and dipped against the steel, hissing like a kettle left too long on the hob. He had been at it for more than two hours. The flare of the torch had put red lines into the whites of his eyes. His hands, though, were steady.

"Alfonso."

"Mm."

"The part."

"I'm on it." He did not look up. "Six hours."

Cesare crossed the room and stood at his shoulder. The silver of the half-finished work moved under the blue flame like mercury poured into a mould.

"If the reinforced inner liner of the Thunderfang's core barrel cannot be made sound, the fire plan for tomorrow loses a quarter of its bite," Cesare said.

"I know." Alfonso set the torch down, stood, and began to lay the tools back into their places on the felt mat. "That is why I am going to find Tarashe."

He was about to say something else when—

A small sound at the door.

Not a knock. A rhythm. The three light taps of a metal-soled boot on the threshold.

Click. Click. Click.

Alfonso's hand dropped first. He pulled the black flintlock from beneath the bench and thumbed the hammer back in a single motion, all without looking at the door.

Francesco, who had appeared in the workshop doorway at some point Cesare had not noticed, muttered under his breath: "So the old man has decided to show himself, at last."

The door was pushed open.

Three men came in. They were dressed in plain clothes, not young any longer, broad through the shoulder, careful in their weight. They did not wear the flame of the Seraphim on their lapels, but they wore the air of men who had once worn it, and worn it long. There was a discipline to the way they stood, the way they surveyed the room, that was older than any uniform.

The one in front was tall. A grey coat, washed too many times, was thrown across his shoulders. The look he gave the room was the look of a man who had been in rooms much worse than this one, and who was, on balance, more interested in what he had come to do.

His codename was Lance.

"Cesare Borgia." Lance's gaze travelled the workshop, took in the bench, the torch, the half-built barrel, the old professor in the doorway, and at last settled on Cesare. "We have something to give you."

A man behind him stepped forward and held out a black brocade box, balanced on both palms. The box was small, but it sat heavy in his hands. Gold thread had been stitched around its edges at some earlier time. The thread was tarnished now.

"What is it?" Cesare asked.

Lance did not answer. He gestured, and the man opened the lid.

The lamp over the bench fell into the open box and lay on what was inside.

It was not a sword. It was the half of one. A fragment the size of a man's spread hand, the edges rough, the way a thing looks when it has been broken on purpose, by someone who did not wish to break it. The surface of the metal was darkened here and there by a stain that was not rust. The stain was gold. In the lamp-light it caught and held a strange, almost wet sheen, as if the metal had been weeping.

Blood. But not any blood a man has seen.

Cesare knew the colour. He had seen it on other relics, in other rooms, and once, briefly, on his own hands. It was the colour of human blood after it has been steeped in red mercury for long enough that the two have forgotten which is which.

The relic known as the Holy Sword, *Excalibur*, broken in two.

Alfonso got to his feet so quickly his chair scraped back against the stone. The soldering torch fell from the bench and rang on the floor.

"That is—" Alfonso's voice was dry at the edges. He had to clear his throat. "That is—"

"Left by Lohenstein," Lance said. "At Marston. In the rubble of the Omega."

Cesare did not move. He stood where he was, and looked at the half of the broken sword, and the violet of his eyes was for a moment the deepest thing in the room.

"He knew you would come for the armour," Lance said. "So he broke the blade, and he hid the half of it in the seams of the wreckage."

A pause. Lance's voice did not change.

"He left it for you."

The workshop was quiet for ten full seconds. The lamp wick went on hissing.

Cesare put out his hand and took up the fragment.

It was heavier than it looked. Heavier by a long way, as if a thing of that size had no right to weigh that much. The metal was cold against his palm. He closed his fingers around it, and a strange feeling began to crawl up from the base of his hand, up the inside of his wrist, into the arm.

At the back of his neck, the gold collar-tube went warm.

The thin branch of consciousness the Red Umbrella had woken the night before stirred against his spine.

For a moment, deep in Cesare's violet eyes, a thin gold line passed across the iris. It might have been a letter of some older alphabet. It might have been the pattern of a flame seen at a great distance. It was there, and it was gone, and after it was gone Cesare heard a voice.

A voice he knew. Low, hoarse at the edges, the way a man sounds when he has been talking too long over the noise of his own guns.

*You were not made to live only in hatred.*

It was Lohenstein's voice.

It was the Marston night, and the Omega fire was still burning over the broken sky, and Lohenstein was standing on the ruin of the bridge, and he had said it then, looking at Cesare, and he was saying it now, looking at him still.

*You were born to defend things, Cesare.*

The hand holding the fragment tightened. Cesare's face did not change. The rhythm of his breath missed a single beat, and that was all.

"There is also this," Lance said.

He reached into the lower compartment of the box and brought out a photograph.

It had yellowed. The corners had curled. Two figures stood in it, in an olive grove at the edge of Florence, with the autumn light coming down through the leaves in pieces. The light lay on them the way light lies on a person who does not yet know they are being remembered.

One of them was Lohenstein. He was in plain clothes, his hair longer than Cesare had ever seen it, and on his face was an expression Cesare had never seen on him either. It was not the coldness of the Seraphim commander. It was not the aloofness of the Black Dragon. It was a small, tentative, almost shy thing that lived near the corner of his mouth. A man at the edge of a feeling he had not yet decided to be seen feeling.

Beside him stood a girl.

She wore a white dress. Her hair, the colour of light brown wood, picked up the sun and gave it back as honey. She was turned half toward Lohenstein, and her smile, in the photograph, had the warmth of a Florentine spring.

It was Tirana.

Cesare had known her from files and secondhand accounts. He had never seen her alive. But he knew, in the moment he saw the photograph, that it was her. He knew it because of the look on Lohenstein's face. A man does not wear that look at a person he has not, in some private place, given his whole life to.

"The back," Lance said.

Cesare turned the photograph over.

There was writing, in ink, in a hand that was large and rough and absolutely certain of itself.

*A Seraph should not live only in hatred. He should protect the things he wants to protect.*

And beneath that, smaller, the pen pressing hard into the paper as if the writer had meant to write it and then had thought better and had written it anyway:

*—For Cesare.*

Cesare looked at the line for a long time.

He did not speak.

The lamp over the bench went on humming. Alfonso picked up the soldering torch from the floor, set it back in its cradle, and began to work again without saying a word. The blue flame jumped against the steel, throwing moving shadows up the walls of the workshop.

Lance looked at Cesare once, nodded, and turned to go.

At the door, he stopped.

"One more thing," he said. "Lohenstein asked me to pass this along."

"He will come."

"But he will not step onto the field."

"He says, do not be rash tomorrow. Follow the commanders."

"He says you have a family. He says you have friends."

Lance did not look back to see how this was received. He walked out, and the two men with him followed, and their footsteps went soft along the corridor and were gone.

A different set of footsteps took up where the first had left off.

Light. Patient. The same three small clicks of a metal boot on a stone threshold.

Click. Click. Click.

Cesare looked up.

A man came in out of the shadow of the corridor.

He wore a civilian knight's tunic, the cotton washed almost to nothing, the collar open at the throat. The skin of his neck was dark with sun. His hair was disordered in a way that suggested he had been walking, and for some time, in country air. The cut of his jaw was harder than Cesare remembered, and the eyes that came in with him were not the eyes Cesare had been told about.

There was something in them Cesare had not been warned of.

It was Lohenstein.

Former commander of the Seraphim. The Black Dragon. The man the propaganda posters had made into a saint and the war had made into a rumour.

He stood in the doorway. He did not speak. He looked at Cesare.

The workshop was full of small working sounds — the lamp, the torch, the slow tick of something Alfonso was tightening with a wrench. None of it was enough to fill the silence.

Cesare did not speak either.

Across the bench the two of them looked at each other for three full seconds.

Alfonso's torch threw a fountain of blue sparks, and in their brief light Lohenstein's face was all planes and older weather. The metal sole of his boot still carried the dust of the Florentine country roads.

Then Lohenstein spoke.

"Do not be rash tomorrow," he said. "Follow command."

His voice was rougher than the voice in Cesare's head. He had been on the road for some days, and he had not been sleeping well enough to hide it.

"You have a family. You have friends."

He turned, then, and walked out.

He did not look back. His figure was swallowed by the dark of the corridor, and after a little while the sound of his steps went out into the night air, and after that there was nothing.

Cesare stood where he was, with the half of the broken sword in his hand, looking at the place Lohenstein had been. His face was steady. The knuckles of the hand that held the fragment had gone pale.

Alfonso set the torch down.

"Boss."

Cesare turned.

"The part," Alfonso said. "I have to go out for a while."

"To find Tarashe," Cesare said.

"Yes." Alfonso nodded. "And there is something else. Something I would like to say to her."

His ears were, very slightly, redder than they had been. His voice was level.

"The Thunderfang part, I will leave to her. I will not be long."

He walked to the door. As he went through, the metal sole of his boot struck the stone of the threshold three times — click, click, click — the habit of a man who had learned it somewhere he did not entirely remember, and had never been able to lose.

The auxiliary infirmary of Minerva was at the end of the corridor.

Alfonso went along it, pushed the door open, and found Tarashe at her desk, a report in her hand. She looked up.

"Alfonso?"

"Tarashe." He stood in the doorway. He rubbed his palms together once, the way a man does when he is about to step off a high place. "There is something I want to say to you."

"About the part?"

"The reinforced inner liner of the Thunderfang?"

"No." He said it simply. "About something else."

He drew a thin silver chain out from the collar of his shirt. At the end of the chain was a small gear, worked into a pendant. The metal was old. The cut of the teeth was the old kind, the kind of teeth no one teaches any more, and the surface of the gear had gone the colour of a thing that had been carried against the skin of a man's chest for the better part of a life.

"I was given this when I was a boy," he said, "in a church on Crete. The old priest there, he was a machinist before he took orders. He gave it to me and said it was the first part he had ever made, and he wanted me to carry it with me, and to keep walking, all the way through."

He paused.

"I have carried it. From Crete to Florence. From being a cadet to being a knight. From the Seraphim to—"

He did not finish the sentence.

Tarashe looked at him. There was a thing in her face she had not been aware she was wearing.

"Alfonso," she said. "What are you trying to tell me?"

He took a breath.

"Tarashe."

"Yes?"

"I am in love with you."

Tarashe did not move.

Alfonso's ears were very red now.

"When this is over," he said, "when the fighting is done, will you come back to Crete with me?"

The lamp in the infirmary made a small humming. The wind from the Florentine country pressed along the corridor, and it had the first cold of the year in it.

Tarashe did not say anything.

She only put out her hand, carefully, the way a person handles something that may be older than they are, and took the gear pendant from where it lay against his shirt. She looked at it in her palm. Then she set it gently back against his chest and refastened the catch.

"Alfonso," she said.

"Yes?"

"That solder you used to set the catch. It is not your best work."

He blinked.

"The link has cracked along the joint," she said. Her mouth was not quite a smile. It was the shape a smile would have, if it were deciding whether to be one. "Bring it back to me. When you are finished with tomorrow."

"I will fix it," he said.

"Yes."

Tarashe looked at him, and the smile decided, in the end, to be one.

"Come back to me," she said.

When Alfonso came back into the workshop, Licinius was at the door.

The deputy director of the Holy Office wore the black of his office, the black angel pinned to the breast of his tunic. His face was, as ever, the face of a man who kept a great many things to himself. There was, in his eyes, something Alfonso could not quite name.

"Deputy Director Licinius."

"Alfonso." Licinius inclined his head. "I have something for you."

He took, from an inner pocket of his tunic, a small metal plate.

It was about the size of two spread hands, less than a finger's width in thickness, and the colour of clean silver. The lamp in the workshop glanced off it in a long, cold line.

"What is it?" Alfonso asked.

"Perfect Solder," Licinius said. "Left behind by the first Director of Minerva."

Alfonso's eyes widened.

The reinforced inner liner of the Thunderfang had cracked along a fatigue line. Minerva had no spare part. If this plate was what Licinius said it was—

"It will fuse any break in any metal," Licinius said. "It is the last of its kind, from the first Director's own store, and it has never been used."

He paused, in the way a man pauses when he is about to say something that has cost him.

"It should, by rights, have been surrendered to the College of Cardinals."

Alfonso was still for a moment.

Licinius set the plate down on the bench. He turned to go.

"Deputy Director," Alfonso said.

Licinius stopped. He did not turn around.

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me," Licinius said. "I have only done what I ought to have done a long time ago."

He went out along the corridor, and after a little while the sound of his steps was no longer to be heard.

Alfonso stood looking down at the plate. There was a light in his eyes that did not belong to the lamp.

"Perfect Solder," he said, half to himself.

He picked it up, and went to the broken Thunderfang liner on the vise, and laid the silver plate against the cracked metal.

The instant the plate touched the joint—

A gold light leapt out of the metal.

It was not the colour of fire. It was the colour of something older than fire. It sprang from the heart of the plate and ran across the bench and up the wall and across the ceiling, so that for a moment the whole of the workshop was drowned in it, and the blue of the soldering torch beside it was washed out to nothing.

Alfonso staggered back. He caught himself on the leg of the bench.

The plate was still in the cracked joint. It was glowing. The glow came from inside the metal, not off it — a slow, even pulse, like a heartbeat, like the first slow tick of an engine after a long winter.

Alfonso turned, half by instinct, to look at Cesare.

Cesare was still at the window.

At the back of his neck, the gold collar-tube was glowing too.

The light that came out of it was the same light. It was not the same kind of light, and it was the same kind of light. It came out of the gold at the base of Cesare's skull, spilled down his collar, and met the glow rising from the plate on the bench, and the two lights knew each other the way two rivers know each other when they have come out of the same hill.

The workshop filled with the sound of a long, low, steady hum. It was the sound the inside of a bell makes when it is not being struck. It was the sound of something that had been lying for a hundred years in a dark place, waiting to be called.

Alfonso stood where he was. The plate buzzed softly in his hand. The hair at the back of his neck had gone up, one by one.

He understood, suddenly, that the plate that the first Director of Minerva had left behind — the plate that had been kept in some quiet drawer for a hundred years, through war and purge and schism — was not a piece of solder at all. Or if it was a piece of solder, it was also something else. It was the same kind of thing as the gold tube at the back of Cesare's neck. It was bound up, in some way Alfonso could not have put into words, with the deepest thing the Minerva office had been built around, and had spent a hundred years pretending was not there.

He looked at Cesare.

Cesare had not looked up. He was standing at the window, very pale, with the half of the broken Excalibur still in his hand. In the deep violet of his eyes the gold line was moving again, slowly, the way a thing moves when it has been waked from a long sleep and is not yet sure of its name.

The gold light in the workshop was so bright now that it pressed out into the corridor. It slipped under the door and ran a long way down the flagstones, the way dawn runs, the way fire runs along the edge of a fuse.

Alfonso tightened his grip on the plate.

He did not know what was going to happen on the field tomorrow. He did not know what the thing in his hand was, or what the thing in Cesare's neck was, or what the two of them would do to each other when the firing started. He knew only that the little hum in the metal and the slow moving line in Cesare's eye were part of the same sentence, and that the sentence was older than any of them, and that it had been waiting, the way a sword in a hill waits, for someone to come and read it aloud.

The gold light did not go out.

In the Florentine hills, somewhere out past the olive groves, a man in a plain tunic was still walking, and the metal soles of his boots were clicking softly on the stones of a road that he was not, this night, going to leave.
