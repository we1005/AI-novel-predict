第157章 叹息之墙的归巢

夜色从翡冷翠的城墙上剥落，敞篷礼车驶入郊区小道时，两侧的柏树被风吹得沙沙作响。瓦莲京娜把车速放慢，金色长发在风中低低飘动，她没有再说话，只是偶尔从后视镜里扫一眼后座。

西泽尔闭着眼睛靠在椅背上，看起来像是睡着了。唐璜和昆提良挤在另一侧，艾莲蜷缩在昆提良怀里，昆提良把自己军服外套盖在她身上，袖子从她肩膀垂下来，湿透的裙摆在他膝盖上洇出一小片深色水痕。阿方索坐在折叠座上，低头看着自己腕表的内壁，谁也不知道他在想什么。

车灯切开黑暗，前方小路尽头，一堵黑铁高墙在夜色中浮现，墙面镶嵌着层层咬合的犬齿状钢门，那是叹息之墙。

瓦莲京娜把车停在侧门前几十米处的暗影里，引擎熄灭。她回头扫了西泽尔一眼。

"到了。"

西泽尔睁开眼，紫色的瞳孔在车灯熄灭后反而更亮了一分。他没有立刻下车，而是看了一眼前方那堵高墙，墙面上六翼猫头鹰徽记的浮雕在月光下泛着暗哑的银光。

"你们先走。"瓦莲京娜说，"我在这里确认没有尾巴，半小时后再进去。"

"你一个人？"

"我是叶尼塞特使，他们不敢动我。"她微笑，那种笑容依旧爽朗，像冰河解冻，"明天在战场等你证明自己是盖世英雄，西泽尔·博尔吉亚。别让我失望。"

西泽尔下车，回过头。瓦莲京娜坐在驾驶座上，套着白靴的长腿翘在方向盘旁边，粉色的发带在风中飘。

"谢谢。"

"谢什么？等你当上骑士王还我这个人情就行。"

礼车再次启动，缓缓驶入暗影，尾灯在柏树间若隐若现。

叹息之墙的侧门并非正门，正门是那十二尺高的犬齿状巨门，侧门相对矮小，宽度也只够两人并排通过，但门后仍是层层叠叠的钢铁闸门和验证机关。昆提良把艾莲交给唐璜，自己先一步跑到门前拍响铜环。

门内侧亮起微弱的黄铜灯光，一个身穿密涅瓦制服的中年军官从观察窗后探出半张脸，扫了他们一眼。

"鹰巢确认，炽天使编外人员西泽尔·博尔吉亚及随行三人。"

"放行。"

第一道侧门缓缓打开，铰链发出沉重的金属摩擦声。昆提良扶着艾莲走进去，西泽尔和阿方索跟在后面，唐璜殿后。第二道门在他们走进去之后又关上，然后是第三道、第四道，每一道门都厚达半尺，是铍青铜夹秘银层的复合装甲。

最后一道门后是一个狭长的金属走廊，墙壁上密密麻麻排布着六翼猫头鹰徽记和各种警告标识。走廊尽头是一面看似普通的金属墙壁，但墙壁中央嵌着一块半透明的铍青铜板，板上有一道垂直的细缝，缝隙里透出冰冷的蓝色扫描光线。

"安全检查。"中年军官说，"一个一个来，把外衣脱掉，抬起双手。"

扫描光线从西泽尔身上缓慢扫过。蓝光从他头顶移到脚底，又从脚底回到头顶。铍青铜板上的指示灯在扫描过程中逐个亮起，绿色代表正常，红色代表异常。

绿色。绿色。绿色。

西泽尔的目光平静，但后颈微微发紧。

蓝色光线扫过他后颈的黄金套管位置时，铍青铜板上的指示灯突然变成了一种从未见过的颜色——淡金色。那种金色只持续了不到半秒就消失了，紧接着所有指示灯全部转为红色。

"警告。"金属走廊里响起冰冷的女声，"检测到非常规能量残留，频段无法识别，来源——后颈植入物。"

"频段无法识别？"中年军官的脸色变了，他快速按下墙壁上的通讯按钮，"控制室，这里是叹息之墙侧门三号安检，检测到无法识别能量频段，疑似外部未知来源，申请技术确认。"

"我是佛朗哥。"

通讯器里传来的是总长沙哑的声音，背景里似乎还有金属碰撞和蒸汽泄漏的嘶嘶声。佛朗哥大概还在熔岩之心附近忙碌。

"频段特征报告。"佛朗哥说。

"持续约0.4秒，颜色为淡金色，来源方位为受检者后颈植入物。频段不在我们已知的红水银、蒸汽核心或任何军用能源数据库中。"

通讯器那头沉默了两秒。

"放行。"

"总长？"

"我说放行。那是红伞能量残留，我知道。"佛朗哥的声音忽然变得很轻，"别碰它，别记录，让那个孩子进来。"

中年军官犹豫了一瞬，但还是服从了命令。他按下放行按钮，最后一道墙壁缓缓向两侧滑开，露出密涅瓦机关内部灯火通明的走廊。

"欢迎回来。"他说。

西泽尔没有回应，迈步走了进去。身后的红色指示灯在他经过后逐个熄灭，但那道淡金色的频段数据已经被系统自动记录在档——红伞能量，来源不明，持续0.4秒。文件编号被自动归入密涅瓦机关的A-7级加密档案夹。

佛朗哥在控制室里看着屏幕上跳出的加密标签，沉默了很久，然后伸手关掉了那个窗口。

"碧儿·丹缇？碧儿！"

唐璜在走廊尽头喊了一声。

走廊尽头站着一位身穿密涅瓦制服、披着灰色围裙的年轻女性，怀里抱着一个帆布袋，另一只手提着一只铁皮暖壶。她的头发简单地束在脑后，几缕碎发贴在额头上，像是刚匆忙赶来。她看见唐璜，嘴角动了动，还没说话，眼眶先红了。

"你这是从坎特伯雷堡赶过来的？"昆提良瞪大了眼睛。

碧儿没有回答昆提良，而是直接走向西泽尔，把帆布袋塞进他怀里。

"面包，连夜烤的。干净军装，也带了。还有几瓶净水。"她快速地说，声音里有一丝喘息，"我知道你们要进密涅瓦机关，所以从堡里一路跑过来的，守门的骑士认识我……"

她停顿了一下，抬头看西泽尔的脸。

西泽尔的脸颊上有一道干涸的泥痕，发梢滴着水，军服被雨水和血污弄得不成样子。但他站在那里，紫瞳安静，脊背笔直，像是一棵被风暴压弯过但没折断的白桦。

"谢谢，碧儿。"

"先去消毒室换衣服。"碧儿说，转身就走，"我去找佛朗哥教授要热水和毛巾。"

"碧儿姐姐！"昆提良在后面喊，"艾莲也在，能不能也——"

"我看到了。"碧儿头也不回，"两分钟后热水送到。"

西泽尔抱着帆布袋站在走廊里，低头看了一眼——袋子里的面包还带着余温，是那种加了蜂蜜和葡萄干的甜面包，碧儿在坎特伯雷堡的烤炉里惯常做的款式。他想起很小的时候，每次母亲身体不好，碧儿就会烤这种面包，说是甜味能让人暂时忘记疼痛。

"老板，走了。"阿方索拍了拍他的肩膀。

密涅瓦机关的临时消毒室在地下第三层，门口挂着"战前准备区"的木牌，里面是一排排金属衣架和储物柜。碧儿果然在两分钟后送来了热水和干净毛巾，随后便去帮艾莲处理擦伤。昆提良跟在碧儿后面，一叠声地道谢。

消毒室的隔壁是一间小型手术准备室。佛朗哥已经在那里等着了。

他穿着那件标志性的深灰色实验服，袖口卷到肘弯，露出精瘦的小臂和几道旧烫伤留下的瘢痕。他的脸色比白天更苍白，眼下的青黑色像是被煤炭画上去的，但眼神依旧锐利。

桌上摆着一只银白色的小型冰盒，冰盒旁边放着便携神经接驳仪——一个巴掌大的黄铜盒子，外壳上嵌着六只微型电极，显示灯是暗红色的。薇若兰已经在仪器旁边调试参数，她今天没穿旗袍，而是密涅瓦的工程制服，袖子挽到肘上，头发随意地扎了个髻，几缕碎发贴在脸颊上，混着忍冬香气的汗味从她身上飘过来。

"坐下。"佛朗哥对西泽尔说，指了指手术椅。

西泽尔在金属手术椅上坐下。椅背是弯曲的金属脊椎造型，但内衬比那种正式实验用的诊断椅要柔软得多——这是佛朗哥特制的便携款。

"碧儿，留下来。"佛朗哥说，"帮我擦汗。"

碧儿端着干净毛巾站在西泽尔右侧，咬了咬下唇。

"先局部麻醉。"佛朗哥打开冰盒。

冰盒内层铺着秘银丝隔热垫，垫子中央躺着一只浅金色的金属管——只有成人小指长，比旧款黄金套管更细、更短，表面镀着一层极薄的秘银膜，在冰盒的冷光下泛着温暖的暗金色。

"计划235适配款。"佛朗哥用镊子夹起套管，对着灯光端详了一秒，"我通宵赶制的，旧款被红伞能量和欧米茄碎片冲击出了微裂纹，不换掉迟早会脱落。"

"贴合度？"薇若兰问。

"比旧款高7%。"佛朗哥把套管放回冰盒，拿起麻醉注射器，"秘银膜能屏蔽一部分外部精神侵蚀，理论上也能减轻不朽之王模式启动时的神经反弹。"

他抬眼看了西泽尔一眼。

"会很疼。"

"我知道。"

佛朗哥没再说话，针头刺入西泽尔后颈旧套管周围的皮肤。冰凉的药液注入，十几秒后那片皮肤就变得麻木。

"薇若兰，接驳仪启动，神经电流校准。"

"收到。"

薇若兰按下便携神经接驳仪的启动键。六只微型电极贴在西泽尔颈椎两侧，接驳仪发出一声短促的嗡鸣，屏幕开始显示神经电流波形图。正常人的神经电流波形是平缓的正弦波，而西泽尔的波形从来都带着细微的锯齿——那是星见血脉与机械接驳的融合痕迹。

"波形稳定，开始取出旧套管。"

佛朗哥用手术刀切开旧套管表面的瘢痕组织。一道细细的血线从切口处渗出，碧儿立刻用毛巾按住。

"接驳仪，微电流刺激神经，测试旧套管与神经的粘连程度。"

薇若兰调整参数，一道极弱的电流脉冲从接驳仪发出。西泽尔感觉到后颈一阵酸麻，旧套管周围的肌肉不受控制地微微抽搐。

"粘连度12%，可以取出。"佛朗哥说。

他用镊子夹住旧套管露出的边缘，缓慢而稳定地向外抽。金属与神经组织分离时发出极细微的撕裂声，像是撕开一片极薄的丝绸。西泽尔的手指收紧，指节发白，但脸上没有表情。

旧套管完全取出。佛朗哥将它放在托盘里——那只旧套管表面有几道细如发丝的裂纹，裂纹中隐约可见暗金色的光泽，那是红伞能量留下的烙印。

"新套管植入。"

佛朗哥从冰盒中取出新的"计划235适配款"，沿原有通道推入。一丝冰凉从后颈蔓延到整个脊椎，西泽尔打了个寒战。

"固定。"

微型钛钉被铆入骨质层，固定住新套管。佛朗哥检查了渗血情况，用医用胶水封闭创口。整个过程不到三分钟。

"接驳仪，二次校准。"

薇若兰再次调整参数。这次神经电流波形发生了明显变化——锯齿状波纹变得更加规则，振幅也略微增大，那7%的贴合度提升在数据上清晰可见。

"耦合度98.3%。"薇若兰念出数据，眉头微皱，"比昨天的旧套管高了将近一个百分点。"

"够了。"佛朗哥关掉接驳仪，拆下电极，"缝合。"

他用可吸收缝线封闭了最外层的切口，贴上防水敷料。

"手术时间——"薇若兰看了一眼墙上的挂钟，"十七分钟。"

"比预期快了三分钟。"佛朗哥摘下手套，丢进医疗废物桶，"今晚之内不要碰水，不要剧烈运动，明天上战场前再检查一次。"

"谢谢，教授。"

"别谢我。"佛朗哥走向门口，路过西泽尔身边时停了一下，"你姑姑留给你的那张字条，看了吗？"

西泽尔微微一怔。

"看过了。"

"看仔细了。"佛朗哥推开门，"有些字不是写给别人的。"

门关上了。佛朗哥的脚步声在走廊里渐行渐远。

碧儿帮西泽尔擦掉后颈残留的消毒液，又检查了一遍敷料。温热的毛巾在后颈上停留了几秒，她的手微微发抖。

"疼吗？"她问。

"还好。"

"你眼睛里——"碧儿忽然停住。

"什么？"

碧儿没有继续说。她从围裙口袋里摸出一小瓶眼药水，放在西泽尔手里。

"睡前滴两滴，消消疲劳。"

西泽尔接过眼药水。他知道碧儿想说什么——他刚才在后视镜里看到过自己的眼睛，紫瞳深处偶尔有极淡的金红色纹路闪过，像是水底有金鱼游过。碧儿也看到了，但她没有说。

"谢谢，碧儿。"

碧儿收起毛巾，匆匆走了。

消毒室隔壁的改装室里，阿方索独自坐在角落的工作台前。台面上铺着一张油布，油布上散落着几块金属零件——那是两截截短的雷霆牙枪管，断面参差不齐，是从阿方索偷偷拆解缴获的雷霆牙残骸上切下来的。

他的左手边是新龙炎的备用枪管，银白色的精密管壁上还有出厂时的蓝色防锈涂层。右手边是便携焊接枪，枪口闪着微弱的蓝色火苗。

阿方索没有立刻动手。他从战术背包的最深处掏出一只黑色小袋，拉开袋口。袋中是一对黑色手铳，铳身蜂巢式镂空，枪管上螺旋着上弹机构，象牙柄上刻着十字花纹——这是碧儿在他离开克里特岛前交给他的防身武器，他从未离身。

他把手铳放在工作台上，盯着看了几秒。

门外传来脚步声。阿方索抬头，看见改装室半开的门缝里闪过一件军服的衣角——黑色制服，胸口和袖口带着细线般的银光。

是李锡尼。

阿方索站起来。临时消毒室的门开着，从这个角度恰好能看到西泽尔后颈的防水敷料和佛朗哥刚缝好的缝线。西泽尔正在碧儿的帮助下换上干净的密涅瓦军装，旧军服被丢在旁边的筐里。

阿方索犹豫了两秒。他重新拿起那对手铳，走向门口。

李锡尼正从走廊那头走过来，步伐沉稳，腰间佩着军刀和短枪枪套。他的脸在走廊灯光下显得格外棱角分明，下颌有一道新添的擦伤，大概是昨夜行动留下的。

"李锡尼中校。"

阿方索在改装室门口喊住他。

李锡尼停下脚步，看了他一眼。

"圣堂装甲师的动向？"阿方索问。

"暂时稳住了。"李锡尼简短地回答，"西塞罗阁下正在枢机会拖延时间，圣堂的人没有接到正式命令。但不会太久。"

阿方索点头，然后从身后拿出那对黑色手铳，递了过去。

"如果我明天回不来——"他的声音平稳，但语速比平时慢了一拍，"请把这对手铳交给碧儿。告诉她这是她当年送我的那对，我一直没舍得用。"

李锡尼低头看着那对手铳。蜂巢式镂空的铳身在改装室的灯光下闪着冷光，象牙柄上的十字花纹像是某种古老的家族纹章。

他的目光越过阿方索的肩膀，透过临时消毒室半开的门，看到了西泽尔后颈那块新换的防水敷料。缝线细密，敷料贴得一丝不苟。

李锡尼没多问。他接过手铳，塞进腰间的空枪套里。

然后他抬手，行了一个标准的半礼——右手按胸，肘部微屈，军人的送行礼，但只做了一半就收回。

阿方索也回了一个半礼。

李锡尼转身走了。他的脚步声在走廊里渐行渐远，军刀和枪套碰撞发出细微的金属声。

阿方索看着他消失在走廊拐角，然后回到改装室，关上门。他重新坐回工作台前，拿起便携焊接枪。

蓝色火苗在枪口跳动。两截雷霆牙枪管和新龙炎备用枪管并排放在油布上，断面锯齿般交错，像某种古老图腾的碎片。

他深吸一口气，按下焊枪开关。

蓝色火苗变成白色，焊锡丝在火苗中融化，沿着断面渗入金属纹理。

*如果回不来。*

他这样想着，手上动作却稳定得像是机器。

走廊另一头，昆提良靠在临时休息舱的门框上，看着塔拉夏手里的一叠表格。塔拉夏刚从密涅瓦附属医院回来，密涅瓦制服上别着一枚淡蓝色的工牌。

"附属医院的情况怎么样？"昆提良问。

"还在运转。"塔拉夏说，"如果你们明天能活着回来，我可以帮你们办准入——南方的海岛人、肺病、重症……密涅瓦附属医院收。"她翻了翻表格，"但规矩是规矩，得有推荐人和担保人，佛朗哥教授可以做推荐人，担保人需要你自己找。"

"艾莲的母亲……"

"先让她本人来翡冷翠。"塔拉夏看了他一眼，"到了密涅瓦附属医院我们可以安排详细检查。你比我更清楚，南方海岛的医疗条件比翡冷翠差太多，与其让她在海岛上熬着，不如来这里赌一把。"

昆提良沉默了一瞬。

"我明白了。"

"不过，准入名额有限。"塔拉夏说，"不是所有病人都能进，得排号。你那位母亲能撑到什么时候？"

昆提良没有回答。他的目光落在走廊尽头改装室紧闭的门上，门缝里透出焊枪蓝白色的光芒。

"她能。"他说。

塔拉夏点了点头，没有再问。她把那叠表格收进文件夹，转身走向电梯。

"明天我会在无人备用甲里远程操控剑舞者。"她说，"别给我添麻烦，南方人。"

"是是是。"昆提良向她挥了挥手，"塔拉夏姐姐最可靠了。"

"别叫我姐姐。"塔拉夏头也不回。

西泽尔换好军装后，碧儿带他穿过几条走廊，来到密涅瓦机关地下第三层尽头的一间临时休息舱。舱室不大，只有一张行军床、一把折叠椅和一个金属洗手台，墙壁上嵌着一盏白炽灯，灯光昏黄。

"这就是你今晚住的地方。"碧儿说，"隔壁是唐璜和昆提良，再过去是阿方索和改装室。"

"碧儿，你也去休息。"

"我等会儿就走。"碧儿从围裙口袋里掏出几样东西放在折叠椅上——一小瓶碘酒、几片退烧药、一块干净的纱布和一支笔，"如果半夜发烧或者伤口渗血，按墙上的红色按钮叫护士。"

"知道了。"

碧儿走到门口，又回头看了一眼。西泽尔已经坐在行军床边缘，低头看着自己空空的双手。

"老板。"

西泽尔抬头。

"明天……"碧儿犹豫了一下，最终没有说完，"……早点睡。"

门关上了。走廊里的脚步声远去。

西泽尔独自坐在行军床上。军装是干净的，碧儿在每个口袋都塞了叠好的干净手帕，连鞋带都换成了新的。但军装下面的身体仍然疲惫——昨夜的狂奔、特洛伊酒店的枪声、艾雷斯男爵的挑衅、叶尼塞公主的敞篷礼车——短短十几个小时内发生了太多事。

他站起来，走到洗手台前。

镜子是黄铜面的，边缘被岁月磨得发亮。镜中的自己脸色苍白得近乎透明，紫色的瞳孔在昏黄灯光下显得格外深邃。

他抬起右手，摸向后颈。

防水敷料下面，是新植入的"计划235适配款"黄金套管。隔着薄薄的敷料和纱布，他能感受到金属的轮廓——比旧款更细、更短，贴合得也更紧密。

然后他的手指移到了胸口。

军装内袋里，有一条细细的银色合金链。链子末端挂着一枚掌心大小的坠子，套在圆环里的倒置五芒星。那是母亲留给他的——琳琅·星见的遗物。

他轻轻触碰坠子的表面。金属冰凉，纹路精细，五芒星的五条线嵌在莲花形的框架里。那种纹路他太熟悉了——从记事起就看着它挂在母亲胸前，在翡冷翠昏暗的灯光下轻轻摇晃。

然后他摸到了另一样东西。

一张折得很小的纸条，揉皱的，夹在坠子的圆环里。昨夜在教堂或者逃亡途中什么时候从口袋里掉出来，被坠子的圆环卡住了。他一直没有注意到。

他把纸条抽出来，展开。

纸张是樱红色的，质地带着东方风味，边缘印着极细的齿轮和莲花暗纹。字迹纤细而清晰，用的是东方古语和教皇国官方文字的对照书写。

*西泽尔——*

*我无法在密涅瓦机关里把一切说清楚。*

*你需要知道的是：你的血脉里有比教皇告诉你的更古老的东西，比博尔吉亚更古老的东西，比世界树残枝更古老的东西。*

*去骷髅地找我。*

*那里有答案——关于你母亲，关于紫瞳，关于初代教皇在维苏威火山留下的四百年布局。*

*带上你最信任的人。但要小心机械神教。*

*姑姑在骷髅地等你确认真相。*

西泽尔把纸条放在洗手台上。

镜中的自己看着镜外的自己。紫色瞳孔深处，有一道极淡的金红色纹路慢慢浮现，又慢慢消失。

*姑姑在骷髅地等你确认真相。*

圣堂装甲师已经封死了密涅瓦通往骷髅地的全部三个入口。

西泽尔闭上眼睛，深深呼出一口气。灯光昏黄，走廊外传来金属碰撞的余响和蒸汽管道的低鸣。密涅瓦机关的心脏在地底深处跳动，维苏威火山的熔岩在更深处翻涌。

明天就是战场。

他重新睁开眼睛，紫瞳平静得像一潭深水。他把纸条重新折好，塞回坠子的圆环里，然后从折叠椅上拿起碧儿留下的眼药水。

滴两滴。消消疲劳。

他躺回行军床上，闭上眼睛。灯没有关，昏黄的光落在他的睫毛上，落在他苍白的脸颊上，落在他胸口那条银色合金链上。

*骷髅地。*

*姑姑。*

*真相。*

走廊尽头传来改装室便携焊接枪的微弱嘶嘶声，那是阿方索还在忙碌。再远一些，隐约能听到昆提良和塔拉夏在低声交谈——关于医院，关于艾莲的母亲，关于战后。

*明天。*

西泽尔没有再想下去。他把右手放在胸口，掌心覆盖着坠子的位置，感受着金属圆环下那枚倒置五芒星微弱的存在感。

*明天在战场等你证明自己是盖世英雄。*

瓦莲京娜的话忽然浮现在脑海里，带着冰河解冻般的爽朗笑意。

他把眼药水放在枕边，翻了个身，面对着墙壁。

墙上有一个小小的红色按钮，按下去就能叫来护士。

他没有按。

密涅瓦机关的通风管道在头顶低声嗡鸣，输送着恒温的循环空气。维苏威火山深处，熔岩之心沉默地燃烧，红光透过层层岩壁透出，映在叹息之墙最深处的黑铁巨门上。

门后的世界树残枝，安静地等待着被唤醒。

或者——被永远封印。

【English】
Chapter 157 — The Wailing Wall at Nightfall

Night peeled off the walls of Florence in slow black sheets. The open carriage had been gliding for twenty minutes through the suburbs, and the cypresses on either side of the lane had gone on whispering to the wind as if rehearsing some complaint no one had asked for. Valentina eased back on the throttle. Her hair, the colour of hammered gold, hung low in the slipstream. She did not speak. From time to time her eyes went to the driving mirror and rested, just for a heartbeat, on what sat in the back.

Cesare Borgia had his eyes closed against the headrest, and to anyone who didn't know him he would have looked like a man asleep. Don Juan and Quintilian were crushed against the far seat. Eileen lay against Quintilian's chest, his officer's coat spread over her like a borrowed wing; one of the sleeves drooped over her shoulder, and her soaked hem was spreading a slow dark patch across his knee. Alfonso sat on the folding stool, studying the inside of his wristwatch, the way a priest studies a relic he doesn't quite believe in.

The lamps carved a corridor of yellow through the dark. At the end of the lane, the road simply stopped being a road and became a wall.

The Wailing Wall rose out of the night as if it had been there before the city had been dreamt of. Black iron, twenty feet high, the surface studded with rows of interlocking tooth-shaped steel gates, each row set at a slightly different angle so the whole façade, when a cloud crossed the moon, looked like a mouth working silently in a dream. Somewhere inside that mouth, a man was supposed to find safety.

Valentina killed the engine some forty paces short of the side gate and let the dark swallow the sound. She turned and looked at Cesare.

"We're here."

Cesare opened his eyes. Without the carriage lamps to soften them, the violet of his irises seemed to brighten, the way coals brighten when you take the bellows away. He did not look at her. He looked at the wall, at the relief of the six-winged owl stamped in the iron, dull silver under a thin moon.

"You go first," Valentina said. "I'll sit on the lampless stretch and make certain no one is behind us. Half an hour. Then I come in."

"Alone?"

"I'm the Yenisei envoy. They don't dare touch me." She smiled. The smile was still the same — bright, the way an ice floe is bright the moment before it goes out. "Tomorrow on the battlefield I expect you to prove yourself the greatest knight-king this side of the Tiber, Cesare Borgia. Don't disappoint me."

Cesare stepped down. He turned back. She was already re-settling herself in the driver's seat, one long white-booted leg cocked up against the wheel, a pink ribbon fluttering loose at her temple.

"Thank you," he said.

"Thank me when you're king. Pay me back the favour then."

The carriage rolled away into the cypress dark. Its red lanterns threaded in and out between the trunks like a pair of dropped coins being slowly spent.

The side gate was not the front gate. The front gate stood twelve feet high, a single slab of iron teeth, and would have admitted a howdah. The side gate was meaner: low, narrow, barely room for two abreast. Behind it lay more steel — three more gates, each thicker than the last, each with its own brass keyhole and its own little black eye of an observation slit. Quintilian handed Eileen over to Don Juan, jogged forward, and beat the copper ring on the door.

A lamp came on inside. A face appeared in the slit — a Minerva officer in middle age, one half of him cut off by the shutter. His eyes went from Quintilian to Cesare to the rest of them and back.

"Eagle's Nest confirms. Seraphim auxiliary, Cesare Borgia, plus three."

"Let them in."

The first gate groaned on its hinges — iron on iron, the kind of sound that lives in the back of the teeth. Quintilian took Eileen through. Cesare and Alfonso followed. Don Juan brought up the rear. The second gate sealed itself behind them. Then the third, and the fourth, each slab a half-foot thick of beryllium bronze sandwiched over a secret-silver core. By the time the fourth was shut, the world outside felt as if it had happened to somebody else.

Beyond the last gate stretched a long, narrow metal corridor, every inch of its walls stamped with the six-winged owl and warning placards in three languages. At the far end, the wall looked like any other wall — grey, riveted, indifferent — except for a single panel of translucent beryllium bronze at its centre, in which a fine vertical slit gave off a thin blue scanning light.

"Security," the officer said. "One at a time. Coats off. Hands up."

Cesare stepped forward. The blue line moved down his body, head to toe, then up again. As it swept him, the indicator lamps on the bronze plate lit up one after another — green, green, green, the colour of a clean conscience in a country that no longer believed in them.

Cesare's face gave nothing. The muscles of his jaw were quiet, his eyes quiet, the set of his shoulders quiet. But at the back of his neck, a band of skin was beginning to tighten, the way skin tightens when the weather is about to change.

The blue line crossed the place where the old gold coupling sat. The lamps on the plate did something none of the men in the corridor had ever seen them do. They turned a pale gold. Just for the fraction of a second, less than a breath. Then every lamp in the row went red.

"Warning," said the cold female voice from the walls. "Non-standard energy residue detected. Frequency unrecognised. Source — cervical implant."

The officer's face went out of him like a candle in a draught. He slapped the wall comm. "Control, this is Wailing Wall side gate, bay three. Unknown frequency detected on a subject. Possible external origin. Requesting technical confirmation."

A long hiss of steam and the clatter of metal answered him. Then Franco's voice, hoarse, coming from somewhere that sounded like a foundry floor.

"Frequency signature."

"Sustained for approximately zero-point-four seconds. Colour: pale gold. Origin point: subject's cervical implant. Frequency does not match any known signature in our red-mercury, steam-core, or military power databases."

Two seconds of silence. You could almost hear Franco not breathing.

"Let him through."

"Sir?"

"I said let him through. That is Red Umbrella residue. I know what it is. Do not touch it. Do not log it. Get the boy inside."

The officer hesitated. He was a Minerva man, and Minerva men were not paid to hesitate. He pressed the release. The last wall slid apart on oiled rails and a long, brightly lit corridor of the Minerva Bureau opened up beyond, all brass lamps and white enamel.

"Welcome back," he said.

Cesare said nothing. He walked in. Behind him the red lamps went out one by one, the way a line of soldiers dismisses. The pale-gold frequency reading, however, had already been written — automatically, silently, into a file folder no one in the corridor had clearance to see. File reference. A-7 encryption. Bureau archive.

In his control room somewhere below the lava, Franco stared at the encrypted label on his screen for a long time. Then he reached across and closed the window with one finger.

"Belle? Belle Danti!"

Don Juan was calling down the corridor before his boots had finished echoing.

At the far end of the corridor a young woman in Minerva greys, an apron thrown over the top of it, was standing with a canvas bag hugged to her chest and a tin hot-water bottle dangling from her other hand. Her hair was pulled back in a working knot; a few dark strands stuck to her forehead the way they do when you have come too fast from too far. She saw Don Juan. The corner of her mouth moved before any sound came out of her, and the whites of her eyes were already shining.

"Did you come down from Canterbury Keep for this?" Quintilian stared. "In the dark?"

Belle did not answer him. She walked straight up to Cesare and pushed the canvas bag into his arms.

"Bread. Baked through the night. Clean uniform, also brought. A few bottles of clean water." The words came out clipped, with a little breath between each. "I knew you were coming into the Minerva Bureau, so I ran down from the Keep. The gate-sergeants know me there —"

She stopped. She looked up at Cesare's face.

A line of dried mud ran down his left cheek. The ends of his hair were still dripping. The uniform was ruined, soaked in places with rainwater and in others with something darker. He stood there, very still, the violet of his eyes steady, his back straight as a stave of birch that the storm has bent but not broken.

"Thank you, Belle."

"Disinfection room first. Get changed." Belle was already turning. "I'll find Professor Franco. Hot water and towels."

"Belle!" Quintilian, from the rear. "Eileen's here too, can you —"

"I've seen her. Hot water in two minutes."

Cesare stood holding the bag. Inside it, the bread was still warm. Honey and raisin bread — the loaf she always made at Canterbury when his mother was laid up, the one she said had enough sugar in it to make a person forget, just for a minute, what hurt.

"Boss," Alfonso said, and put a hand on his shoulder. "Move."

The Bureau's field disinfection room was three floors down, behind a wooden placard that read, in a hand that looked as if it had been carved with a chisel, PRE-WAR PREP. Inside there were rows of metal hangers and lockers. Belle, as promised, appeared in two minutes with the water and the towels, and went off to see to Eileen, with Quintilian hurrying after her offering thanks in three different languages.

The room next door was a small surgical prep. Franco was already there.

He wore the long dark-grey work coat that everyone in the Bureau knew, sleeves rolled to the elbow, the bare forearms showing a few pale scars of old burns. His face was paler than it had been in daylight, and the bruise-coloured hollows under his eyes looked as if they had been drawn on with coal. The look in those eyes, though, was the same one he always had when he was working: a thin, dry, exacting look, like a wire drawn too tight.

On the table, an open silver-white cold-case. Beside it, the portable neural coupler — a brass box the size of a man's palm, studded with six tiny electrodes, its indicator lamp the dull red of old blood. Verolan was already there, bent over the box, adjusting its dials. She was in Minerva engineering coveralls, not the cheongsam; her sleeves were pushed past her wrists, her hair dragged into a hasty knot, a few strands stuck to her cheek. The smell of her was sweat and honeysuckle.

"Sit," Franco said, pointing at the surgical chair.

Cesare sat. The chair's back was a stylised metal spine, but the cushioning was softer than any official diagnostic chair he had ever sat in — a private model of Franco's, made for the road.

"Belle, stay." Franco did not look up. "Wipe for me."

Belle took her place on Cesare's right, the clean towel folded over her hand. She bit the inside of her lip.

"Local first."

Franco opened the cold-case. Inside, on a bed of secret-silver thread, lay a coupling the colour of pale gold. It was shorter than a child's little finger, and thinner than the old one, plated with a film of secret silver so fine that it caught the case's light and gave it back as a warm, dull gold.

"Project 235, fit-to-spec," Franco said, holding the coupling up in his forceps. "I have been working on it since last night. The old one had micro-fractures — the Red Umbrella surge and the Omega shrapnel did that. Sooner or later it would have fallen out."

"Fit?" Verolan asked.

"Seven per cent better than the old." He laid the coupling back. He picked up the anaesthetic. "The silver film will screen out a portion of any external psychic intrusion. In theory, it should also reduce the neural rebound when the Immortal King protocol engages."

He looked up at Cesare.

"It will hurt."

"I know."

The needle went into the skin around the old coupling. The liquid was cold, and after a few seconds the small square of territory at the back of his neck was no longer his. Franco took the surgical knife and opened the scar tissue over the old implant. A thin red line of blood welled up. Belle pressed the towel to it without being told.

"Coupler. Micro-pulse. Test the adhesion of the old coupling to the nerve."

Verolan turned a dial. A small current went through. Cesare felt a slow acid numbness run down the back of his skull, and the muscle around the implant gave a single, involuntary twitch.

"Twelve per cent adhesion. It will come out."

Franco gripped the edge of the old coupling with the forceps. He drew it out, slow and steady, and the sound of metal separating from nerve was the smallest sound in the room — the sound of a single thread of silk being torn. Cesare's fingers went white at the knuckles. His face did not change.

He set the old coupling on a steel tray. Its surface was laced with hairline cracks. In the cracks there was a faint dark gold, the brand the Red Umbrella had left behind.

"New coupling in."

Franco lifted the Project 235 piece from its bed and slid it into the old channel. A line of cold ran from the back of Cesare's neck all the way down the centre of him, and he shivered once, in spite of himself.

"Anchor."

The tiny titanium rivets went into the bone, and the coupling was fixed. Franco checked the bleed, closed the skin with medical glue. Three minutes from first cut to last.

"Coupler. Second calibration."

Verolan turned the dial again. This time the wave on the little screen changed. The serrated edge that had always been a part of Cesare's trace — the trace of the Star-seer blood meeting the machine — was smoother now, the amplitude a little larger, the seven per cent visible in the data as clean as a man who has just shaved.

"Coupling at ninety-eight point three," Verolan said. She frowned a little. "Nearly a full per cent up on yesterday."

"Sufficient." Franco killed the coupler. He pulled the electrodes off. "Closure."

He closed the outer skin with a dissolving suture and put a waterproof patch over it.

"Operating time?" Verolan asked, glancing at the clock on the wall.

"Seventeen minutes."

"Three under estimate." Franco pulled off his gloves and dropped them into the bin. "No water on it tonight. No exertion. I will check you again before you go to the field tomorrow."

"Thank you, Professor."

"Don't thank me." Franco was already at the door. He paused beside Cesare. "The note your aunt left you. Have you read it?"

A small stillness passed over Cesare.

"I have."

"Read it carefully." Franco pushed the door open. "Some of those words were not written for anyone else."

The door shut. Franco's footsteps went down the corridor, soft-soled and unhurried, the way a man walks when he is thinking about something he cannot yet say.

Belle cleaned the disinfectant from the back of Cesare's neck and checked the patch. The warm towel rested on his skin for two or three seconds longer than it needed to. Her hand was trembling a little.

"Does it hurt?"

"Manageable."

"Your eyes —" Belle began, and stopped.

"What about them?"

She did not answer. She reached into her apron and took out a small bottle of eye drops, which she set in his palm.

"Two drops at bedtime. To settle the strain."

Cesare took the bottle. He knew what she had been about to say. He had caught his own eyes in the carriage mirror — violet, but with a faint thread of gold-red stirring deep in the iris, the way a goldfish stirs in dark water. She had seen it too. She had not said it.

"Thank you, Belle."

Belle took up her towel and left, quickly, before he could see anything else in her face.

In the room next door, the gun room, Alfonso sat alone at the workbench. A sheet of oilcloth lay on the bench, and on it a few bits of metal — two short sections of Thunder Fang barrel, their cut edges ragged as a torn collar, sliced from a Thunder Fang wreck he had quietly salvaged. To the left of them lay the spare barrel of a new Dragonbreath rifle, the precision wall still wearing its blue factory coat of anti-rust. To the right, the portable welder. Its muzzle gave off a thin blue flame.

Alfonso did not pick anything up. He reached to the bottom of his pack and drew out a small black pouch. He untied the drawstring. Inside, two black pistols. The breeches were honeycombed, the loading mechanism spiralled around the barrel, the ivory grips carved with a crosshatch pattern. They were the pistols Belle had given him the day he left Crete, and he had not been parted from them since.

He set the pistols on the bench and looked at them for a while.

A footstep passed the half-open door. A flicker of uniform — black, with a thin line of silver down the breast and the cuff.

Licinius.

Alfonso stood. From where he stood, through the half-open door of the disinfection room, he could see Cesare's new waterproof patch and the fine black line of Franco's stitch. Cesare was being helped into a clean Minerva uniform by Belle. The old one lay in a basket.

Alfonso hesitated for two seconds. He picked the pistols up and went to the door.

Licinius was coming down the corridor, his step measured, his sabre and holster riding his hip. His face, in the corridor's white light, looked more cut from stone than usual. A fresh scrape ran along the line of his jaw — the previous night's work, most likely.

"Colonel Licinius."

Licinius stopped. He looked at him.

"The Cathedral Armour Division?" Alfonso asked.

"Holding." Licinius's answer was short. "Cardinal Cicero is buying us time in the College. The Cathedral men have no formal order. But the window is small."

Alfonso nodded. Then he held out the pistols.

"If I don't come back tomorrow —" His voice was even, but slower than usual. "Give these to Belle. Tell her they are the ones she gave me. I never used them."

Licinius looked down at the pistols. The honeycombed breeches caught the light of the gun room in cold little flashes. The crosshatch on the ivory grips looked like a family crest from a country that no longer existed.

Over Alfonso's shoulder, through the half-open door, he saw Cesare's patch and the neat line of Franco's stitch.

He did not ask.

He took the pistols. He slid them into the empty holster at his hip. Then he brought his right hand to his chest, elbow a little bent, the half-salute of a soldier seeing a soldier off — and brought it down again before it was finished.

Alfonso returned the half-salute.

Licinius turned. His boots went down the corridor, the metal of the holster and the sabre making a small, conversational clink.

Alfonso watched him round the corner. He went back into the gun room, shut the door, and sat at the bench. He picked up the welder. The blue flame in its muzzle trembled.

He pressed the trigger. The flame went white. Solder wire melted into the joint of the barrels and ran into the metal.

*If I don't come back.*

He thought it, and his hand stayed as steady as a part of a machine.

At the other end of the corridor, Quintilian was leaning against the door of the ready-room, watching Tarasya flip through a stack of forms. Tarasya had come up from the Minerva Infirmary; a pale-blue staff card was pinned to her coveralls.

"The Infirmary?" Quintilian asked.

"Still running." Tarasya turned a page. "If any of you make it back alive tomorrow, I can put the paperwork in. The southern islanders, the lung cases, the bad ones — the Infirmary will take them. But the rule is the rule. You need a referee and a guarantor. Professor Franco will referee. You'll have to find your own guarantor."

"Eileen's mother —"

"Get her to Florence first." Tarasya glanced up. "When she's at the Infirmary we can do a full work-up. You know as well as I do that the medicine down on the southern islands is a candle in a hurricane. Better to come up here and gamble."

Quintilian was quiet for a moment.

"I understand."

"The places are limited, though." Tarasya turned another page. "Not everyone who comes gets in. There's a queue. How long can your mother last?"

Quintilian did not answer. His eyes had gone to the closed door of the gun room at the end of the corridor, where a white-blue light was leaking from under the sill.

"She can last," he said.

Tarasya nodded. She did not press him. She gathered the forms into a folder, turned, and walked towards the lift.

"Tomorrow I will be running the Sword Dancer remotely from a reserve frame." She did not look back. "Try not to make work for me, southerner."

"Yes, yes." Quintilian waved after her. "Sister Tarasya, most reliable —"

"Don't call me sister." The lift doors closed on her.

When Cesare was dressed, Belle took him through two more corridors to a small ready-room at the very end of the third sub-level. There was a camp bed, a folding chair, a metal basin and tap, a single bulb in a wall socket, the light the tired yellow of an old man's teeth.

"This is where you sleep tonight." Belle set the iodine, the fever tablets, the clean gauze and a small pencil on the folding chair. "If you run a temperature in the night, or if the patch bleeds through, press the red button on the wall. A nurse will come."

"I will."

Belle went to the door. She stopped there and turned her head.

"Boss."

Cesare looked up.

"Tomorrow —" She hesitated. The hesitation went on long enough to become a different sentence.

"— sleep early."

The door closed. The corridor took her footsteps.

Cesare sat on the edge of the camp bed. The uniform was clean. Belle had folded a clean handkerchief into every pocket and even put new laces in the boots. The body inside the uniform was still the same body that had sprinted through the rain the night before, that had heard the gunshots in the Troy Hotel, that had been baited by Baron Aires, that had been driven to this wall by a Yenisei princess in a ribbon. Twelve hours. That was all.

He stood up and went to the basin.

The mirror was brass, the edges worn down to a pale metal by years of hands. The face that looked back at him was so pale it was almost transparent. The violet of his eyes, in the tired yellow light, looked deeper than the mirror.

He raised his right hand and felt for the back of his neck. Under the patch, under the gauze, he could make out the shape of the new coupling — shorter than the old one, finer, fitted tight as a key in a lock.

His hand moved on, to his chest. In the inside pocket of the new uniform, a thin chain of silver alloy. On the chain, a pendant the size of a palm — an inverted five-pointed star set inside a ring of lotus petals. His mother's pendant. Linlang Starjian's last piece of him.

He touched the surface. The metal was cold. The five lines of the star were very fine. He had known them since before he could remember — the way they had swung from his mother's throat in the dim rooms of Florence, the way the light had caught them as she bent to kiss him.

His fingers found something else.

A small folded paper, creased, caught in the ring of the pendant. It must have fallen from his pocket in the church, or somewhere in the running, and the ring of the pendant had caught it and held it. He had not noticed.

He drew the paper out. He unfolded it.

The paper was the colour of cherry bark, faintly Eastern in its grain, the edge stamped with a fine watermark of gears and lotus. The handwriting was small and exact, written in two scripts side by side — an old eastern script, and the official hand of the Papal States.

*Cesare —*

*I could not say all of this inside the Minerva Bureau.*

*You must know that there is something in your blood older than what the Pope has told you. Older than the Borgias. Older than the branch of the World Tree.*

*Come to Golgotha and find me.*

*There is an answer there — about your mother, about the violet of your eyes, about the four-hundred-year plan the first Pope laid down at the foot of Vesuvius.*

*Bring the ones you trust most. But mind the Mechanicum.*

*Your aunt waits for you at Golgotha, to know the truth.*

Cesare set the paper on the basin.

The man in the mirror looked at the man outside the mirror. Deep in the violet of his eyes, a single thread of gold-red stirred, the way a fish stirs once under a still sheet of water, and was gone.

*Your aunt waits for you at Golgotha, to know the truth.*

The Cathedral Armour Division had sealed all three routes from Minerva to Golgotha.

Cesare closed his eyes and let out a long breath. The bulb threw its tired yellow across his lashes, across the pallor of his cheek, across the silver chain on his chest.

*Golgotha.*

*Aunt.*

*Truth.*

At the far end of the corridor, the gun room gave out the thin hiss of the welder, a sound too small to mean anything unless you knew that someone behind that door was making something of himself before morning. A little further, the low murmur of Quintilian's voice, talking to someone who was not there about a mother on a southern island and the war that had to end before she could be brought up to Florence.

*Tomorrow.*

Cesare did not think the word. He picked up the paper and folded it back, slipped it into the ring of the pendant, and took up the bottle of eye drops.

Two drops. To settle the strain.

He lay back on the camp bed. He did not switch off the light. The tired yellow lay across his face like a hand laid gently down by someone who could not stay.

*Tomorrow on the battlefield I expect you to prove yourself the greatest knight-king this side of the Tiber.*

Valentina's voice came back to him, bright as the sound of a river giving up its ice.

He turned the bottle in his hand. He set it on the stool by the bed. He turned on his side, to the wall.

The red button was there. A nurse would come if he pressed it.

He did not press it.

Above his head, the Minerva Bureau's ventilation pipes went on murmuring in their long, even breath, feeding him air at the same temperature, the same pressure, hour after hour. Below him, far below, at the foot of Vesuvius, the Lava Heart went on burning in its own silence, and the red of it, leaked through rock after rock, was thrown at last on the black iron of the deepest gate in the Wailing Wall.

Behind that gate, the World Tree's last branch waited in the dark, patient as a held breath.

To be woken, in the morning.

Or never to be woken at all.

第158章 神怒Ⅲ型的冰原图纸

清晨的密涅瓦机关地下走廊弥漫着机器油脂和蒸汽的混合气味，那是密涅瓦特有的味道，金属被切割时冷却液蒸发后的清冽，混着铍青铜粉在空气中悬浮的微甜。灯管还没有全亮，只有隔三十米一根的应急灯投下惨白的光，把走廊切割成明暗交替的片段。

西泽尔沿着走廊往第十二层改装室走，脚步声在空旷的管道间回响。昨夜睡得不多，但精神反而比前几天清醒，像一把被反复淬火的刀。

改装室的门半敞着，门缝里漏出焊接的蓝色火花。他推门进去，看见了阿方索。

阿方索蹲在地上，周围散落着雷霆牙的零件和工具。便携焊接枪还在嘶嘶冒烟，但他的双手搁在膝盖上，盯着面前一截断成两半的枪管，一动不动。

西泽尔没有说话。

他在阿方索对面蹲下来，顺着对方的目光看向那截断裂的枪管。断口不整齐，带着明显的高温烧蚀痕迹，但问题显然不是出在焊接上——枪管的内壁出现了一条极细的纵向裂纹，从尾部一直延伸到中段，裂纹周围还有一圈暗蓝色的金属氧化晕。

红水银侵蚀的痕迹。

"这根枪管有问题。"阿方索的声音很平，像在陈述一道数学题的解法。"昨晚焊接备用枪管的时候发现的。雷……薇若兰副总长留给我的那批备用件里，这一根的内层合金有气孔。我把它接上去试了一下，红水银蒸汽一通过去，金属就被腐蚀了。"

他说"雷"字的时候顿了一下，改口叫了薇若兰的职称，显然那是一种刻意的生疏，像是在提醒自己记住什么。

西泽尔伸手拿过断成两截的枪管，对着灯光看。断口处的合金呈层状剥落，最内层几乎被烧成黑灰色——那是红水银蒸汽在管道内壁剧烈反应后留下的腐蚀痕，正常情况下至少要使用三百小时以上才会出现。这根枪管显然没有被使用过三百小时。

"其他的备件呢？"西泽尔问。

"其他七根都正常。塔拉夏帮我全部检过了。"阿方索站起来，从旁边的工具台上拿起一叠检测报告递给他。"问题只出在这一根。而且裂纹的位置很奇怪，不是随机分布，是沿着内壁的纵向纹理走——这种纹理只在旧式普罗米修斯的备用管件上才看得到。"

他指了指墙上挂着的三十二只机械蜘蛛的蛛巢，然后看向西泽尔："原罪机关的东西。"

西泽尔把报告合上，没有追问。现在不是追究来源的时候，距离对抗测试还有不到二十个小时，每一根枪管都是命。

"还剩七根。"他说。

"七根够打半场。"阿方索说，"但如果我需要中途更换管件，就只剩六根了。"

走廊里传来两个人的脚步声，节奏不同——一个轻而快，带着金属敲击地面的脆响；一个稳而重，每一步都踩得很实。

薇若兰出现在改装室门口。

她今天穿的是密涅瓦的深灰色工作服，袖口卷到手肘，露出两条白皙的小臂，上面沾着几滴冷却液。左胸口别着机械师工牌，照片比本人老了十岁。头发随便扎成一个高马尾，露出的脖颈在灯管下白得刺眼。

她没有看西泽尔，目光直接落在阿方索手中的断裂枪管上。

"雷管裂了。"她的声音不带任何感情色彩，像在念一行实验日志。

阿方索把手里的枪管递给她。薇若兰接过去，翻了个面，断口对着灯光。她盯着裂纹看了三秒钟，然后忽然笑了。

那笑容很轻很淡，像冬天里一小片还没来得及落地的雪花。

"裂纹内壁有残留的红水银结晶，不是蒸汽渗透，是接触渗透。"她把枪管还回去。"这根管子曾经被用来盛装液态红水银，然后又经过了简单的清洗就送回备件库。清洗的时候缝隙里残留了一滴，两滴——"

她伸出右手食指，在空中画了一个小圈。

"足够在蒸汽通过时引发局部高温。"薇若兰说，"也就是说，原罪机关把一根曾经接触液态红水银的旧普罗米修斯备用管件混进了密涅瓦的备件库。不是失误，是故意。"

阿方索的表情没有变化，但他握着枪管的手指收紧了。

"还有别的吗？"西泽尔问。

"其他七根我都检查过了，没有同样的问题。"薇若兰走到工具台前，拿起另一根备件管轻轻弹了一下，管壁发出一声清脆的金属回响。"但备件只有这八根，密涅瓦的库存已经见底了。佛朗哥教授把能找到的资源都调来了，连样品库的管件都清空了一多半。"

她放下管子，转头看了西泽尔一眼。

"我建议把剩余的管件全部做一次接触试验。"她说，"让我亲自来。"

西泽尔点头。

薇若兰转身往门外走。走到门口时她停了一下，没回头。

"瓦莲京娜来了。"她说。"穿着我们的工装混进来的。在叹息之墙侧门碰到的。我让人把她带到了第二十三层会议室。"

她停顿了一下，声音里多了一丝极淡的、像是被什么东西烫过的微颤。

"她给你带了东西。图纸。"

——

第二十三层会议室是密涅瓦机关的正式会议空间，比第一层的小型作战室大一倍，长方形的核桃木长桌能坐二十人。墙上挂着几幅精密的矿道地形图和一张标注着"密涅瓦造·绝密"的水印。

西泽尔进门的时候，看到的并不是他预想的画面。

瓦莲京娜·罗曼诺夫站在长桌的一头，正双手撑在桌面上，低着头看摊开的一卷图纸。她穿着密涅瓦的工装，袖口扎得很紧，腰间甚至系了一条工具腰带，乍一眼看上去和密涅瓦的普通女机械师没什么区别。但她的金发在脑后扎成一个利落的低马尾，发梢在灯下泛着浅金的光。

她抬起头，冲西泽尔笑了笑。

那笑容和昨晚在特洛伊酒店外面一样，冰河解冻似的，但比昨晚多了一点别的什么——一种很认真的、像是在下棋的专注。

"早上好，未婚夫。"她说。

西泽尔还没来得及回答，会议室的门在身后再次被推开。

薇若兰走进来，步子和之前一样不紧不慢。她看了一眼瓦莲京娜，又看了一眼桌上的图纸，眉毛挑了一下。

"叶尼塞使馆的人来的比预想的早。"薇若兰说。语气像在自言自语，但声音刚好能让整个房间都听见。

瓦莲京娜迎上她的目光，没有躲闪。

"薇若兰副总长。"她伸出手。"久仰。"

"久仰。"薇若兰和她握了手。两个女人的手都是白皙的、骨节分明的，握在一起时都用了力——不是那种社交场上礼节性的轻触，是两个都清楚彼此分量的女性之间的试探。

薇若兰的指节微微发白。

"亚历山大让我代他问好。"薇若兰松开手，漫不经心地说。"他昨晚跟我提过你很多次。"

"他向我提过你更多次。"瓦莲京娜的回答同样漫不经心。

西泽尔在旁边站着，感觉自己像是被两把没出鞘的刀夹在中间。他认识这种气氛——两个强势的女人第一次见面时，不动声色的较量。她们比的不是谁的手劲大，是谁先松手。

瓦莲京娜先松了。

她转回长桌前，把摊开的图纸往前推了推。

"这是神怒Ⅲ型的全套原始设计图纸。"她说，"没公开过的那一份。"

她顿了顿，目光从薇若兰身上移开，落在图纸的右下角——那里有几行用极细的钢笔字写的批注，墨水颜色已经微微泛黄，是很多年前的手迹。

"这些批注是我母亲留下的。"她的声音忽然放轻了。"她在世的时候，花了三年时间研究这份图纸。她把叶尼塞降低神经侵蚀的独家技术写在了上面。"

薇若兰走近长桌，低下头看那几行批注。

她看得很慢，一行一行地读。

批注的字迹纤细工整，是标准的夏国式蝇头小楷，但用的是叶尼塞王国少见的朱砂墨——只有皇家档案室才会用这种墨水。每一行批注的旁边都画有极小的齿轮纹样，叶尼塞军工特有的标志。

"冰原寒玉。"薇若兰轻声念出批注的第一行。

她抬起右手，食指悬在图纸上端，没有触碰。那一行的批注密密麻麻，像一页被反复修订过的论文摘要。

"叶尼塞的冰原寒玉。"她重复了一遍。"嵌入脊椎甲胄内衬，作为神经接驳系统的缓冲层。可以把神经冲击降低至普通炽天使的十分之一。"

"但产量极低。"瓦莲京娜说，"冰原寒玉只产于叶尼塞冰原最深处，开采一公斤需要三十名矿工挖三个月。全年总产量不超过两百公斤，神怒骑士团自己都不够用。"

她抬眼看向西泽尔。

"我母亲去世前研究这份图纸，是为了一个目的。"她的声音平静得近乎冷漠，但眼底有极淡的光在闪。"她在赌一个假设——如果把冰原寒玉的内衬技术用在其他型号的甲胄上，也许能救一个人的命。"

她没有说"那个人"是谁。

但整个房间都知道。

薇若兰直起身，看了一眼西泽尔后颈的位置——那里什么也看不见，新植入的黄金套管隐藏在皮肤之下，但她的目光仿佛能穿透肌肉和骨骼，看到那枚泛着淡金色微光的金属管。

"我老师通宵为你换了一个新套管。"薇若兰忽然说，"效果比旧的好，但神经接驳的反弹依然存在。冰原寒玉如果真的能降低到十分之一——"

她没有把话说完。

她看向西泽尔，眼神里有一种很复杂的东西。像是在说"这太冒险了"，又像是在说"但我愿意试试"。

"图纸可以留在密涅瓦。"瓦莲京娜说，"算是我母亲留给未来女婿的最后一份礼物。"

她说"未来女婿"三个字的时候，声音里有笑意，但眼睛里没有。眼睛里只有一种很深的、近乎悲悯的认真。

——

佛朗哥是在第二十三层会议室里开会的。

会议室的灯全开了，比平时亮一倍。佛朗哥今天穿着一件洗得发白的旧工装，袖口有烧焦的洞，头发比平时更乱，像是一夜没睡。

西泽尔坐在长桌的一侧，依次是阿方索、昆提良、唐璜。塔拉夏通过电话接入，声音从桌上的黄铜扬声器里传出来，断断续续的。

薇若兰站在佛朗哥身后，双手撑在椅背上，像一只随时准备捕猎的猫。

佛朗哥的面前铺着一张比他身体还大的图纸。

"维苏威火山底部废弃矿道。"他用炭笔在图纸中央画了一个圈。"深一百二十米，方圆五公里，密涅瓦一百年前采矿时挖出来的主矿道，后来因为通风管道改线就废弃了。"

炭笔在图纸上划出曲折的线。

"主矿道分三层，上层是运输道，宽十二米；中层是采石场遗址，到处都是堆叠的巨石和塌方形成的坑道；最下层是已经灌水的地下水层。"他抬起头，看向在座的每一个人。"最复杂、最适合新型炽天使小队作战的地方，是中层。"

他用炭笔在图上画了几个叉。

"这里、这里、还有这里，是矿柱支撑点。普罗米修斯是十米级的巨型傀儡，在狭窄的矿道里行动会受限，但它的肩部火力点可以覆盖前方一百八十度的扇面。"佛朗哥放下笔。"你们要利用矿柱的遮挡，让他们的巨剑和火炮打不到你们。"

他转头看向薇若兰。

"原型机和量产机的图纸你比我都熟，说说看法。"

薇若兰绕过椅子，走到图纸前。

"三架普罗米修斯。"她伸出一根手指，点在图上。"根据我们目前掌握的情报，原罪机关会派出三架第三代量产机。我们是五个人。"

她数了数手指。

"西泽尔负责核心输出，开不朽之王模式冲散对方阵型；昆提良的奥古斯都扛重盾正面牵制；阿方索的所罗门王负责远程狙击和维修支援；唐璜的蔷薇之鬼潜入敌后破坏关键管线。"

她顿了顿。

"第五人。"她看向扬声器。"塔拉夏。"

"在。"扬声器里传来塔拉夏的声音，带着些许电流杂音。

"剑舞者的远程操控权交给你。"薇若兰说，"不穿骑士服，全关节柔韧性测试。密涅瓦唯一一具无人操控的甲胄。"

"明白。"塔拉夏说。"但有一个问题——剑舞者的通讯延迟。密涅瓦的通讯线路在地下矿道里会出现三到五秒的延迟，前线有人倒下时我的反应可能来不及。"

"所以是备用。"薇若兰说。"前线有人倒下，剑舞者即刻补位。延迟三到五秒，够了。"

"薇若兰。"佛朗哥忽然开口。

"老师。"

"五对三。"佛朗哥看着图纸。"你确定他们只派三架？"

薇若兰沉默了一秒。

"我确定。"她说。"原罪机关一共只有三架第三代量产机可以投入实战。古洛诺斯是特装机，胡安还在医院里。即使原罪机关主人调来试制机，也需要重新校准。二十个小时不够。"

"三架量产机就够可怕了。"昆提良嘀咕。

"所以你们要快。"佛朗哥说。"普罗米修斯的弱点不在装甲，在平衡管线。"

他拿起另一根炭笔，在图纸上的一个位置重重画了一个圈。

"腘窝。"他说。"巨型傀儡的腘窝金属关节处，平衡管线最密集。切断那里的管线，对应腿的平衡阈值会下降三成。"

"三成不够让它倒。"阿方索说。

"三成不够。"佛朗哥点头。"但如果三架同时切断三条腿的管线——"

"三条腿同时失去平衡。"唐璜接话。"十米高的铁疙瘩直接摔下去。"

"摔下去之后就别想站起来。"佛朗哥说。"巨型傀儡翻身困难，给你们的射击窗口大约十秒。十秒内，瞄准骑士舱、蒸汽管道、武器挂载点。"

"十秒。"唐璜重复了一遍。

会议室里安静了一会儿。

西泽尔看着图纸上那些曲折的矿道线，手指无意识地在桌面上轻轻敲了三下。

"如果我开不朽之王模式，能维持多久？"他问。

薇若兰看了他一眼，没有立刻回答。

她转身走到窗边，背对着所有人。窗外是密涅瓦的维苏威火山通风管道，巨大的铜制管道在晨光中泛着冷光。

"按照目前的燃料储备，新红龙在不开不朽之王模式的情况下，能维持高强度战斗四分半。"她说。"开不朽之王模式，神经接驳系统会加速燃料消耗，同时完美合金稳定器可以将维持时间翻倍。"

她转过身。

"翻倍后，从原本的三十秒到一分钟，提升到一分钟到四分钟。"

"也就是说最多四分钟。"佛朗哥说。

"四分钟。"薇若兰点头。

"四分钟打完普罗米修斯三架。"唐璜的声音带着一种想笑又笑不出来的味道。

"四分钟，加上其他人。"薇若兰说。"不是他一个人打。"

她走回长桌前，目光依次扫过每一个人。

"分工明确。"她说。"昆提良正面牵制，吸引普罗米修斯的火力；阿方索的所罗门王在侧翼掩护，寻找射击窗口；唐璜的蔷薇之鬼绕到敌后破坏管线；西泽尔在合适时机开不朽之王模式突入收割。"

"收割？"阿方索忽然开口。

"对。"薇若兰看着他。"收割。"

阿方索沉默了一秒，点了点头。

"明白了。"

——

会议结束的时候，密涅瓦的地下时钟显示是凌晨四点四十分。

塔拉夏挂断了电话，扬声器里传来一阵电流声后归于沉寂。佛朗哥把图纸卷起来，用红绳系好，递给薇若兰。

"去准备吧。"他说。"六点钟之前，密涅瓦的所有人员必须撤到地下三百米。"

他说完，低头看了一下自己的手。手指在微微发抖，他连忙把手插进口袋里。

"佛朗哥教授。"西泽尔忽然开口。

佛朗哥抬起头。

"瓦莲京娜的图纸，您看了吗？"

"看了。"佛朗哥点头。"冰原寒玉的技术如果真的有效，我们可以考虑在新型红龙的脊椎内衬上加一层。"

"时间够吗？"

佛朗哥看了一眼墙上的时钟。

"不够。"他说。"但可以试。"

他转身往门口走，走了两步又停下来。

"瓦莲京娜那个丫头……"佛朗哥忽然说，"她母亲当年研究神怒Ⅲ型图纸的时候，我就认识她了。叶尼塞王妃是我见过的最聪明的女人。"

"她知道您？"

"她知道我。"佛朗哥说。"但她知道我的时候，她已经病得很重了。"

他没有再往下说，推门走了出去。

走廊里传来他拖着脚步走远的声音。

西泽尔站在会议室里，看着佛朗哥留下的那卷图纸。

瓦莲京娜不知道什么时候已经走到了他身边。

"我母亲去世前三个月，写完了这些批注。"她说。"她把所有她认为重要的东西都写在了这份图纸上。包括冰原寒玉的技术，也包括……"

她顿了顿。

"一些关于你母亲的事。"

西泽尔转头看她。

瓦莲京娜的脸在灯光下白得近乎透明，但她的眼睛是蓝色的，深得像叶尼塞的冰湖。

"我母亲和你母亲认识。"她说。"不是在翡冷翠认识，是在更早的时候。在锡兰。"

"锡兰？"

"我外祖父是锡兰末代宰相。"瓦莲京娜说。"锡兰灭国的时候，我母亲还是个孩子。她和我外祖父一起逃出来，辗转到了叶尼塞。后来她嫁给了老沙皇，但她在锡兰的那些年……"

她的声音低了下去。

"她见过星见家的人。"

西泽尔的手无意识地按在胸口。坠子的轮廓隔着衣服传到他掌心。

"你母亲的事，你真的不想知道更多吗？"瓦莲京娜问。

她的语气平静，没有逼迫的意思。只是一句问话。

"我想。"西泽尔说。

"那就活着回来。"瓦莲京娜说。"活着回来，我把所有的事都告诉你。包括我母亲告诉我的，包括我在叶尼塞王宫地下金库里找到的那些东西。"

她忽然笑了一下，那种笑法和平时不一样——平时她是爽朗的、冰河解冻式的；这一次的笑很轻很轻，像雪落在雪上。

"我母亲去世前跟我说过一句话。"她说。"她说：'我这一辈子研究图纸、研究技术、研究怎么让一个人活过三十岁，最后发现最关键的东西不在图纸上。'"

她伸出手。

手心里躺着一枚银戒指，戒面上嵌着极细的碎冰原寒玉，在灯光下泛着幽幽的冷蓝色光泽。

"见面礼。"她说。"也是保命的小东西。冰原寒玉可以稳定神经电流的波动，吸收过载的冲击。我母亲留给我的嫁妆。"

西泽尔没有接。

"叶尼塞的寒玉产量不是极低吗？"

"是极低。"瓦莲京娜说。"所以这一枚是用掉的最后一小块。"

"为什么？"

"因为我母亲说过。"瓦莲京娜说。"她研究了一辈子，就是为了在见到那个人的时候，能帮上一点忙。"

她把戒指塞到西泽尔手里，转身就走。

走到门口的时候她停了一下。

"早上好，未婚夫。"她说。"别死。"

门关上了。

西泽尔站在原地，手心里是那枚冰凉的银戒指。

寒玉的冷意顺着掌心往手腕上走，不刺骨，但很清晰，像是在告诉他——

有人在等你。

——

他回到改装室的时候，阿方索已经把断裂的枪管收起来了。

地上散落的工具被归位，备用枪管整齐地排列在桌上，一共七根，泛着冷光。便携焊接枪关着气阀，不再冒烟。

阿方索蹲在角落，正在用一块绒布擦手。

"瓦莲京娜走了？"他问。

"刚走。"

"图纸呢？"

"留下了。"

阿方索点了点头，没再说话。

改装室很安静，只有通风管道低沉的嗡鸣。西泽尔在阿方索旁边的工具箱上坐下来，低头看着手里的银戒指。寒玉的冷意在掌心慢慢扩散，让他后颈那个新植入的黄金套管周围的皮肤微微发紧——一冷一热，像两种力量在相互制衡。

走廊里忽然传来急促的脚步声。

不是改装室的人。节奏不对，太快了，而且带着某种压抑的慌乱。

改装室的门被猛地推开。

佛朗哥站在门口，脸色比纸还白。

"出事了。"他说。"圣堂装甲师——"

他的声音在发抖。

"圣堂装甲师查抄了密涅瓦通往骷髅地的入口闸门！"

他的手里捏着话筒，铜制的话筒在他掌心里被捏得变了形。

"三个入口——三个全部被封死——"佛朗哥的声音像是从喉咙深处挤出来的，"他们提前行动了——比我们预想的提前了六个小时——"

【English】
Chapter 158 — The Ice-Plain Blueprint

The corridor beneath Minerva smelled of machine-oil and steam — that particular mineral sharpness the workshops gave off when beryllium-bronze filings were still hanging in the air, when the cutting-fluid had just boiled off a fresh cut. The lamps had not all come on. Every thirty paces an emergency bulb threw a slab of pallid light, and between them the dark drank the rest of the tunnel.

Cesare walked toward the Level 12 refit bay. His boots rang on the iron floor, the sound taking its time coming back. He had slept poorly, and he was the better for it. Like a blade that had been quenched too many times and come out harder than the smith had asked for.

The bay door stood half-open. A seam of blue welding-spill leaked from the crack.

He pushed the door and went in.

Alfonso was on the floor.

Parts of the Thunder Fang were laid out around him in a careful semicircle, the way a surgeon lays out his tools before an operation he is not certain to survive. The portable torch was still ticking, a thread of smoke rising from the nozzle. Alfonso's hands were on his knees. He was looking at a barrel that had been broken in two, and he was not moving.

Cesare said nothing.

He crouched opposite Alfonso and looked where the other man was looking.

The break was not clean. There was heat damage at the lip, dark and obvious, but the trouble was not in the weld. Running down the inside of the barrel, almost hair-thin, was a longitudinal crack. It began at the breech and walked all the way to the midpoint. Around it, a halo of dark blue oxide — that bruise-metal color any Minerva mechanic learned to recognize by the time he was fourteen.

Red mercury had been here.

"This barrel is bad," Alfonso said. His voice was even, the voice of a man describing the solution to an equation he had already finished. "I found it last night when I was fitting the spares. The first-piece set Veronika left me — one of them had porosity in the inner alloy. I ran steam through it once. The metal went."

He caught himself on the first syllable of her name, paused, finished on the title. A small, deliberate distance. Something he was keeping on purpose.

Cesare took the two halves from him and tilted them under the lamp. The alloy was flaking in layers, the inner face burned almost to black-grey. That was the signature of red mercury vapour in violent reaction against an internal wall — three hundred hours of steady use would do it. This barrel had not seen three hundred hours.

"The rest of the spares?" he asked.

"The other seven are clean. Taras walked every one of them with me." Alfonso stood, took a sheaf of inspection slips from the bench, handed them over. "The fault is only in this one. And the crack follows the longitudinal grain — that grain doesn't show up in current production. You only see it in older Promethean spares."

He tipped his head toward the wall, where the nest of thirty-two mechanical spiders crouched in their charging rack, then looked back at Cesare.

"Something from the Cardinal Apparatus."

Cesare closed the slips. He did not ask how. There would be time for that, or there would not. In nineteen hours the sparring test began, and every barrel that fired was a life.

"Seven left," he said.

"Seven will do a half-fight," Alfonso said. "If I have to swap mid-engagement, six."

Two sets of footsteps in the corridor — one light and quick, with the bright tick of metal on iron; one slow and placed, the way a man walks when he has decided exactly how much weight his legs are going to carry today. They were not the same person.

Veronika came through the door.

She was in Minerva grey today, sleeves pushed to the elbow, two white forearms marked with a few drops of cutting-fluid. The engineer's badge at her breast was older than the photograph on it. Her hair was pulled back in a careless knot; the throat beneath it was the kind of white that looked wrong under fluorescent light, the kind of white that could not be imitated by paint or powder.

She did not look at Cesare. She looked at the broken barrel in Alfonso's hand.

"The rake-barrel cracked," she said, in the same register one would use to read a line out of an experimental log.

Alfonso handed it to her. She took it, turned the broken face to the light, and studied the crack for three seconds. Then she smiled.

It was a small smile. Almost a nothing-smile. A snowflake that had not yet decided whether to land.

"The inner wall has residual red mercury crystals. Not vapour-permeation — contact-permeation." She gave the barrel back. "This piece was used, at some point, to hold liquid red mercury. Then it was cleaned — not very thoroughly — and put back into the spares. A drop, two drops, left in a seam."

She lifted her right hand and drew a small circle in the air with one finger.

"Enough," she said, "to set up a local hot-spot the moment steam passed through. The Cardinal Apparatus did not make a mistake. They put a contaminated old Promethean spare into Minerva's rack on purpose."

Alfonso's face did not change. The fingers of his hand around the barrel closed tighter.

"Anything else?" Cesare asked.

"I walked the other seven last night myself. No same signature." She moved to the bench and tapped one of the spares with her knuckle. The barrel gave back a clean, cold ring. "But these eight were all we had. Franco pulled in everything he could reach. The samples stores are half-empty."

She set the barrel down and looked at him.

"I want to run a contact test on the remaining seven. Personally."

Cesare nodded.

Veronika turned to the door. At the threshold she stopped, not turning back.

"Valentyna is here," she said. "Wearing our kit. She came in through the Sighing Wall side gate — I met her there and had her escorted up. She is in the twenty-third-floor conference room."

A pause, very small.

"She brought you something. A blueprint."

—

The twenty-third-floor conference room was Minerva's proper council chamber, twice the size of the first-floor operations bay, a long walnut table with room for twenty. The walls held mining-survey maps and one sheet stamped with the Minerva mark and the word RESTRICTED in red ink.

What Cesare saw when he came in was not what he had been expecting.

Valentyna Romanova stood at the far end of the table, both hands flat on the wood, head bent over an unrolled sheet of drafting paper. She wore Minerva's work tunic, sleeves tied tight at the wrist, a tool-belt cinched at the waist — at a glance she could have passed for any of the female mechanics who worked the upper bays. But the hair, drawn back in a low knot, caught the light at the ends like pale gilt.

She looked up. She smiled at him.

The same smile as last night, outside the Troy Hotel — the breaking of an ice-river in spring — but with something else added. A quiet, board-game attentiveness.

"Good morning, betrothed," she said.

He had not had time to answer. The door behind him opened again.

Veronika came in at the same unhurried pace. She glanced at Valentyna, glanced at the paper, lifted one eyebrow.

"The Yensei legation is early," Veronika said. The tone was that of a woman talking to herself. She pitched it, precisely, to fill the room.

Valentyna met her look and did not step back from it.

"Vice-Master Veronika." She put out her hand. "I have heard your name."

"I have heard yours," Veronika said, and they shook.

Both hands were white. Both had the long tendons of women who worked with their fingers for a living. Both gripped, briefly, hard — not the social brush of people being polite, but the test-grip of two people who already knew how much the other weighed.

Veronika's knuckles paled.

"Alexander sends his regards," Veronika said, releasing. "He spoke of you a great deal last night."

"He spoke of you more often," Valentyna said, at the same register.

Cesare stood between them and felt like a man caught between two sheathed blades. He knew this weather. Two strong women meeting for the first time, measuring without drawing — the contest was not about the grip. It was about which one let go first.

Valentyna let go first.

She turned back to the table and pushed the unrolled sheet forward.

"This is the full original design set for the Wrath Mark III," she said. "The unpublished copy."

She let the words settle, and then her gaze moved to the bottom-right corner of the paper. There, in a fine steel nib, in ink that had yellowed with many years, were a few lines of handwritten annotation.

"The notes are my mother's," she said, more quietly. "She spent three years on this set. She wrote the Yensei method for reducing neural-erosion into the margins."

Veronika came to the table and bent her head over the notes.

She read them slowly, line by line.

The handwriting was narrow and precise — a style of small-character script more common to summer courts than to the snow capitals. The ink was cinnabar-red, the kind only used in royal archive rooms. Beside each line, drawn very small, was the gear-and-spoked-wheel motif that marked every piece of Yensei military engineering.

"Ice-Plain Cold Jade," Veronika said, reading the first line aloud.

She raised her right hand and held her index finger above the paper, not quite touching it. The first line was crowded tight, like the abstract of a paper that had been revised too many times.

"Embedded into the spinal lining of the armour, as a buffer for the neural-bridging system." Veronika said it again, half to herself. "It can cut neural shock to one-tenth of what a standard Seraph suit delivers."

"The yield is very low," Valentyna said. "Ice-Plain Cold Jade is mined only from the deepest stratum of the Yensei ice-plain. Three months of work by thirty miners, to produce a single kilogram. The yearly total is under two hundred. The Wrath Order cannot even keep its own knights supplied."

She looked up at Cesare.

"My mother spent her last years on this blueprint for one purpose." Her voice was almost flat. The light in her eyes was not. "She was betting on a hypothesis — that if the Cold Jade lining were transplanted to a different suit of armour, it might keep a particular man alive."

She did not name him.

The room did not need her to.

Veronika straightened. Her gaze went to the back of Cesare's neck. Nothing could be seen there — the new gold sleeve sat under the skin — but her eyes moved as if she could see through muscle and bone to the dull gold filament of the metal within.

"My teacher fitted a new sleeve for you overnight," Veronika said. "It is better than the last. The rebound at the neural bridge is still there. If the Cold Jade can cut it to a tenth —"

She did not finish the sentence.

She looked at him, and in the look there were two things at once. The first said this is too dangerous. The second said I will do it anyway.

"The blueprint can stay at Minerva," Valentyna said. "You can consider it my mother's last gift to her future son-in-law."

She said future son-in-law with a smile. Her eyes were not smiling. In them was a serious, almost pitying attention.

—

The meeting was held on the twenty-third floor.

Every lamp in the chamber was on, twice its normal brightness. Franco wore a work-tunic washed almost white, a small burn-hole at the cuff, his hair more disordered than usual, the look of a man who had not been to bed.

Cesare took one side of the long table. Down from him, Alfonso, Quintilian, Don Juan. Taras was on the brass speaker-pipe, his voice breaking up the way underground lines always did, a phrase, a hiss, a phrase.

Veronika stood at Franco's shoulder, hands flat on the back of his chair, the posture of a cat waiting to spring.

On the table, unrolled, was a drawing larger than Franco's body.

"The disused workings at the base of Vesuvius," Franco said. He took a charcoal stick and drew a circle. "One hundred and twenty metres down, five kilometres across. The main haulage roads of the old Minerva dig. We abandoned them when we rerouted the ventilation."

The charcoal moved in crooked lines.

"Three levels. The top is the haulage, twelve metres wide. The middle is the old stope — broken stone, fallen pillars, water-collecting pits everywhere. The bottom is flooded. The middle is where we will fight. It is the worst ground a ten-metre Prometheus can be asked to move through."

He made three crosses on the plan.

"Here, here, and here. The ore-pillar supports. A ten-metre puppet cannot turn freely between them. But the shoulder-mount arcs cover a hundred and eighty degrees of forward cone. Use the pillars for cover. Keep out of the cones."

He looked up at Veronika.

"You know the prototype and the production model better than I do. Talk."

Veronika came around the chair to the head of the table.

"Three Promethei." She touched the plan with one finger. "By what we have, the Cardinal Apparatus will send three production-line Marks. We are five."

She counted off on her fingers.

"Cesare takes the central role — open the Immortal King mode, break their formation. Quintilian in the Augustus, shield-tank on the frontal line. Alfonso in Solomon's Throne, long-range covering fire and field repairs. Don Juan in the Rose Fiend, infiltration, cut the lines at the back."

A beat.

"The fifth," she said, to the speaker. "Taras."

"Here," the line said, with a crackle.

"The Sword Dancer's remote-control authority goes to you. No rider. Full joint-flexibility trial. The only unmanned suit Minerva has."

"Understood. One problem," Taras said. "Sword Dancer's comms-link. Three to five seconds of delay in the underground line. If a man goes down in front of me, I may not be fast enough to cover."

"Then you are the reserve. The moment a suit goes down, Sword Dancer steps in. Three to five seconds will do."

"Veronika." Franco cut in. "Five against three. Are you sure they will only bring three?"

Veronika was still for a moment.

"I am sure," she said. "The Cardinal Apparatus has exactly three production Marks in service. The Gnosis is a one-off, and Juan is in the hospital. Even if the Apparatus Master calls in a trial piece, calibration alone is more than twenty hours."

"Three production Marks is still terrible," Quintilian said under his breath.

"So be quick," Franco said. "The Promethei are not weak in armour. They are weak in the balance piping."

He picked up another piece of charcoal and made a heavy circle on the plan.

"The popliteal joint. The back of the knee. The balance line is densest there. Cut that line and the leg's stability margin drops by a third."

"A third is not enough to put one down," Alfonso said.

"No," Franco said. "It is not. But if all three of them lose a leg at the same time —"

"Three legs out at once," Don Juan said. "A ten-metre iron carcass goes straight down."

"And it does not get back up. A puppet of that size cannot right itself. The shooting window after a fall is about ten seconds. Ten seconds at the cockpit, the steam trunks, the weapon hardpoints."

"Ten seconds," Don Juan repeated.

The room went quiet for a moment.

Cesare looked at the crooked mining lines on the paper. His fingers, without his noticing, tapped the table three times.

"If I open the Immortal King mode," he said, "how long can I hold it?"

Veronika looked at him. She did not answer at once.

She went to the window, and stood with her back to the room. Outside it was morning. The great copper air-shafts of Minerva's Vesuvius rose in the new light, cold metal in a cold sky.

"With our present fuel, the new Red Dragon, even without the Immortal King mode, can hold a high-intensity action for four and a half minutes." She turned. "With the mode on, the neural bridge burns fuel faster, but the Perfect-Alloy Stabiliser doubles the time we can stay in the mode."

"And the doubled time is —" Franco said.

"From thirty seconds to a minute. Up to a maximum of four minutes."

"So four minutes is the ceiling," Franco said.

"Four minutes," Veronika said.

"Four minutes to put down three Promethei," Don Juan said, in the voice of a man trying on a smile that would not fit his face.

"Four minutes, with the rest of us," Veronika said. "Not him alone."

She came back to the table. She looked at each of them in turn.

"Roles. Quintilian tanks frontal fire, draws the Promethei onto himself. Alfonso flanks, picks his shots, covers the team. Don Juan works the rear, cuts the lines. Cesare chooses his moment and opens the Immortal King mode into the centre of their line to take them apart."

"Take them apart?" Alfonso said.

"Yes," Veronika said, looking at him. "Take them apart."

Alfonso was quiet for a second. He nodded.

"Understood."

—

When the meeting ended, the underground clock read four-forty in the morning.

Taras cut the line. The brass speaker hissed, then went dead. Franco rolled the plan, tied it with a red cord, handed it to Veronika.

"Go and prepare," he said. "By six, all of Minerva's people are to be three hundred metres below ground."

He finished speaking and looked down at his own hands. The fingers were shaking slightly. He put them into his pockets at once.

"Professor Franco," Cesare said.

Franco looked up.

"Valentyna's blueprint. Have you looked at it?"

"I have. If the Cold Jade holds in practice, we can try a layer of it inside the new Red Dragon's spinal lining."

"Is there time?"

Franco glanced at the clock on the wall.

"No," he said. "But we can try."

He turned to the door. Two steps out he stopped.

"That girl, Valentyna —" he said, half to himself. "I knew her mother when her mother was studying the Wrath III set. The Yensei queen was the cleverest woman I have ever met."

"Did she know you?"

"She knew me. But by the time she knew me, she was already very ill."

He did not say more. He pushed the door open and went out, his steps heavy and unhurried down the corridor.

Cesare stood at the table, looking at the rolled blueprint Franco had left.

Valentyna was at his elbow. He had not heard her come up.

"Three months before my mother died, she finished those notes," she said. "She wrote down everything she thought mattered. The Cold Jade technique, and —"

She stopped.

"And some things about your mother."

Cesare turned to her.

Valentyna's face, in the lamplight, was almost translucent. But the eyes were the blue of the Yensei ice-lakes in deep water.

"My mother and yours knew each other," she said. "Not in Florence. Earlier. In Ceylon."

"Ceylon?"

"My grandfather was the last chancellor of Ceylon. When Ceylon fell, my mother was still a child. She and my grandfather got out, and after a long road, they came to Yensei. She married the old Tsar, in the end. But the years she had in Ceylon —"

Her voice dropped.

"She met the Hoshimi family."

Cesare's hand, without his telling it to, closed on the shape of the locket under his shirt.

"You do not want to know more?" Valentyna asked.

No pressure in the question. Just a question.

"I do," he said.

"Then come back alive." Valentyna said. "Come back alive, and I will tell you everything. Everything my mother told me. Everything I found in the Yensei palace's sub-vaults."

She smiled. It was a different smile from the morning smile — from the ice-river smile. This one was very light. Snow falling on snow.

"My mother said a thing to me before she died," she said. "She said, I have spent my whole life on blueprints, on technique, on how to keep a man alive past thirty, and in the end the most important thing was not on any blueprint."

She put out her hand.

In her palm lay a silver ring. The face of the ring was set with a fine sliver of Ice-Plain Cold Jade, and in the lamp it gave back a thin, cold blue light.

"A betrothal gift," she said. "A small thing to keep you alive. The Cold Jade steadies the neural current. It absorbs overload. My mother's dowry, in the end."

Cesare did not take it.

"You told me the Cold Jade yield was very low," he said.

"It is."

"Then this is the last piece."

"It is."

"Why?"

"Because my mother said —" Valentyna said, "that she had spent her whole life studying, in order to be of some use, when at last she stood before him."

She pressed the ring into his hand and turned to go.

At the door she stopped.

"Good morning, betrothed," she said. "Do not die."

The door closed.

Cesare stood still, the ring cold in his palm.

The cold of the Jade crept up his wrist, not biting, but very clear, the way a voice is clear when it is speaking only to you. It reached the new gold sleeve at the back of his neck, and there — cold and gold, like two principles in argument — it stopped.

Someone was waiting for him. The coldness said so.

—

He got back to the refit bay and Alfonso had already put the broken barrel away.

The tools on the floor were back in their places. The seven good spares stood in a row on the bench, cold and orderly. The torch was off. The room was quiet, except for the low hum of the ventilation pipe.

Alfonso crouched in the corner, drying his hands on a piece of felt.

"Valentyna is gone?" he asked.

"Just left."

"The blueprint?"

"Left here."

Alfonso nodded. He did not speak.

Cesare sat down on a tool-chest at his elbow and looked at the silver ring in his hand. The Cold Jade was quiet in his palm, the cold spreading slowly outward. In the back of his neck, under the new gold sleeve, the skin pricked — one coldness, one heat, two things in balance.

Footsteps in the corridor. Not anyone from the bay. The rhythm was wrong — too fast, and with the pressed-down panic of a man trying to be quiet about the worst news he had ever carried.

The refit bay door banged open.

Franco was in the doorframe. His face was the colour of paper.

"Trouble," he said. "The Holy Order Armoured Division —"

His voice was shaking.

"The Holy Order Armoured Division has just sealed the gate at the Skull Ground. All three entrances — all three are shut."

The brass speaking-horn was still in his hand. The metal of it was bent out of shape, where his grip had crushed it.

"They have moved six hours ahead of when we thought they would."

他的声音很低。

"别冲动，听指挥。"

他看着西泽尔，紫色的眼睛在西泽尔身上停了几秒。

"你还有家人朋友。"

说完这句话，他转身就走。

西泽尔站在原地，看着那个身影消失在走廊的赤金灯光里。

他没有追出去。

因为龙德施泰特不会让他追。

——

改装室里的空气慢慢回温。

龙枪教官咳嗽了一声，把锦盒的盖子合上。

"红龙，我们就送到这里。"他说。"老团长说，让你好好用那块碎片。"

"我会的。"西泽尔说。

"我们……"龙枪教官看了一眼身后的汉斯和卡尔。"我们明天也会去。"

西泽尔抬起头。

"你们？"

"老团长说，去不去我们自己定。"龙枪教官说。"但我们三个商量了一下——都去。"

他顿了顿，又说：

"把命卖给红龙，不亏。"

说完他转身，带着汉斯和卡尔走了出去。

三个穿着便装的老兵，肩并肩走进了走廊的赤金灯光里。

——

改装室又安静了下来。

阿方索蹲回地上，开始继续检查那些备用枪管。但他的动作比刚才慢了很多，眼神也飘得厉害。

"老板。"他忽然说。

"嗯？"

"你说……"阿方索的手里拿着那根雷霆牙断裂的枪管，眼神却不知道飘到哪儿去了。"塔拉夏这个时候在哪儿？"

西泽尔看了他一眼。

"他在鹰巢。"他说。"你找他有急事？"

阿方索没有立刻回答。

他站起来，把枪管放回工作台上，拍了拍身上的铁屑。

"我出去一下。"他说。

西泽尔看着他，点了点头。

阿方索走到门口的时候，忽然又停了下来。

他从脖子上解下一个东西。

那是一根细细的银链子，链子末端挂着一枚小小的齿轮吊坠。吊坠是黄铜的，已经磨得发亮，上面刻着精密的齿轮纹路。

西泽尔从来没见过阿方索戴这个。

"这是什么？"他问。

阿方索低头看着那枚吊坠，过了一会儿才说：

"小时候，老神父送给我的。"

西泽尔愣了一下。

老神父。那个在克里特岛收养阿方索的教堂神父，那个在火灾里死去的老神父。

"他说，"阿方索的声音很轻，"戴上这个，做什么都会顺。"

他把吊坠放回脖子上，又掖进了领口里。

"等我回来再说。"他说。

然后他走了出去。

西泽尔看着他的背影，眉头微微皱了一下。

他很少见阿方索这样。

——

阿方索沿着走廊往鹰巢走。

走廊里到处都是密涅瓦机关的技术人员，他们行色匆匆，脸上带着紧张的神色——圣堂装甲师封死骷髅地入口的消息已经传开了，谁都知道要出事。

但阿方索没有看他们。

他一直在想那枚齿轮吊坠。

老神父给他的。他十三岁那年，老神父在火灾里死了，他什么都没来得及拿，就这一枚吊坠，是老神父生前最后一个圣诞节送给他的。

老神父说：戴上这个，做什么都会顺。

阿方索十五年都没信过这句话。

但是明天他要去打仗了。

明天之后，也许他就再也不会回来了。

鹰巢的门是虚掩的，里面透出微弱的蓝绿色灯光。塔拉夏坐在操作台前，正在远程操控剑舞者的某些系统，手指在键盘上飞快地跳动。

"塔拉夏。"阿方索站在门口，叫了一声。

塔拉夏的手指停了一下。

他转过头，看到是阿方索，眉头微微一挑。

"怎么了？"他问。"有事？"

阿方索走进鹰巢，顺手把门带上了。

鹰巢不大，到处都是线缆和操作台，空气中弥漫着微弱的电火花味。

阿方索在塔拉夏旁边的操作台边缘坐了下来，支支吾吾了半天。

"我……"他开口，又停下了。

塔拉夏看着他，眼神里带着一点困惑。

"阿方索？"他问。"你没事吧？"

"没事。"阿方索说。

"那你——"

"塔拉夏。"阿方索忽然说。

塔拉夏停下了手里的动作。

阿方索从领口里掏出那枚齿轮吊坠，放在掌心里，递到塔拉夏面前。

"这个……"他支支吾吾地说。

塔拉夏看着那枚吊坠，又抬头看着阿方索的脸。

"老神父送我的。"阿方索说。"他死之前给我的。"

塔拉夏没有说话。

"我戴了十五年。"阿方索说。"从来没告诉过任何人。"

他的声音低了下去。

"明天……"他停了一下。"明天我可能回不来了。"

塔拉夏的眼神变了。

"阿方索——"

"所以我想……"阿方索抬起头，看着塔拉夏的眼睛。

"所以我想在你这里寄放一下。"

塔拉夏愣住了。

阿方索的手指有些抖，但声音很稳。

"等这次仗打完，"他说。"我……"

他深吸了一口气。

"我喜欢你。"

塔拉夏的眼神一动。

"能不能……"阿方索的声音更低了。

"等这次仗打完，"他说。"和我一起回克里特岛？"

鹰巢里安静了几秒。

塔拉夏看着阿方索的眼睛，脸上的表情很复杂。

"阿方索。"他说。

"嗯。"

"你知道克里特岛离翡冷翠有多远吗？"

"知道。"

"你回去干什么？"

"不知道。"阿方索说。"但我想带你回去看看。"

塔拉夏沉默了一会儿。

然后他伸出手，接过了那枚齿轮吊坠。

"好。"他说。

阿方索的眼睛里忽然闪过一点光。

"但你必须活着回来。"塔拉夏说。

阿方索点头。

"一定。"

塔拉夏把齿轮吊坠小心翼翼地放进了自己的工具盒里。

"我等你。"他说。

——

阿方索从鹰巢出来的时候，走廊里的赤金灯光已经变成了惨白色。

那是密涅瓦机关最高级别的警报被触发了。

阿方索愣了一下，脚下加快了步伐，往改装室走。

改装室的门还开着。

西泽尔坐在工作台前，手里握着一块银白色的金属板——

不。

是李锡尼站在工作台前，手里拿着一块银白色的金属板。

阿方索停下脚步。

"李锡尼中校。"他叫了一声。

李锡尼转过头来。

他穿着一身异端审判局的中校制服，胸口别着黑天使徽章，脸上没有表情。

"阿方索。"他说。

"您怎么来了？"

"送东西。"李锡尼说。

他转回身，把手里的那块金属板放在了工作台上。

西泽尔站在工作台旁边，看着那块金属板。

那是一块手掌大小的纯银金属板，表面有六翼猫头鹰的徽记，徽记旁边刻着一行极小的字——阿方索凑近了才看清："密涅瓦初代总长封存。完美焊料。"

"完美焊料。"西泽尔低声念了出来。

"嗯。"李锡尼说。"初代总长留下的东西。可以修复任何金属——只要是教皇国造得出的金属，都能修。"

阿方索的眼睛一亮。

"能修雷霆牙吗？"

"能。"李锡尼说。

西泽尔抬头看着李锡尼。

"你从哪里找到的？"

"叹息之墙的废弃甬道里。"李锡尼说。"初代总长把它封在一个小盒子里，盒子上刻着齿轮徽记。"

他顿了顿。

"盒子里还有一张纸条。"

"写的什么？"

"写的是——"李锡尼看着西泽尔的眼睛。

"'给最需要的人。'"

改装室里安静了几秒。

西泽尔伸出手，把那块完美焊料拿了起来。

焊料在他掌心里是凉的，没有任何特别的地方。

阿方索已经把那根断裂的雷霆牙枪管递了过来。

"老板。"他说。

西泽尔把焊料按在了断裂处。

——

就在焊料接触枪管断口的一瞬间。

整个改装室突然亮了。

不是电灯的光，不是火的光。

是金色的光。

从焊料和枪管的接触处，喷涌出一道耀眼的金色光芒——那光芒跟西泽尔后颈黄金套管发出的光一模一样。

古老的、温暖的、像凝固的血液一样的金色。

改装室的金属墙壁被那光芒映得雪亮。

西泽尔的手被那道光震得发麻，但焊料像是被某种力量焊在了枪管上，正在缓慢地、自动地修复那道裂口。

"这是……"阿方索后退了一步，脸上的表情震惊。

李锡尼也愣住了。

只有西泽尔一动不动，他看着掌心里那道金色的光，眼睛里慢慢浮起金红色的纹路。

跟刚才他握紧Excalibur碎片时一模一样的纹路。

后颈的黄金套管热得几乎要烧起来。

他脑海里又响起了龙德施泰特的声音——

"你不是为复仇而生的。"

然后是另一个声音。

一个女人的声音。

轻柔的，像莲花香气一样的。

"世界树根在东方，叶在西方。"

"只有两者合一，才能结束一切。"

西泽尔猛地睁开眼睛。

金光已经慢慢消退了。

完美焊料消失了。

但那根雷霆牙枪管——

完好如初。

没有裂口，没有焊痕。

像从来没有断过一样。

"老板……"阿方索走上前，看着那根枪管。

西泽尔没有说话。

他转过头，看向改装室门外。

惨白色的灯光还在闪。

走廊里传来急促的脚步声和喊叫声——是密涅瓦机关的技术人员们正在紧急集合。

佛朗哥说过，最高警报意味着枢机会下达了公开命令。

他看着那根修复如初的枪管，又看着工作台上那个黑色锦盒里的Excalibur碎片。

明天。

明天他就要上战场了。

但今晚他还不困。

他要把那根枪管装回雷霆牙上。

然后去找他的同伴们。

——他还有家人朋友。

他得让他们都活着。

金色的枪管在惨白灯光下泛着微弱的光。

改装室外，走廊里传来钟声。

密涅瓦机关的钟。

凌晨四点了。

【English】
Chapter 159 — Broken Sword and Fragment

The air in the modification room had frozen.

Even before Franco finished speaking, a stuttering of pneumatic valves ran down the ventilation shaft — the warning breath of the Minerva Agency's highest-grade intrusion alert, tripped somewhere above them. Amber light from the corridor bled in under the door in slow, mechanical pulses, laying itself across Alfonso's jaw like a sickly gilt.

"Three entrances," Franco said again. "All sealed. They moved six hours early."

He was gripping the speaking-horn so hard the copper had begun to buckle in on itself, the rim biting into the meat of his palm.

Cesare stood by the tool chest with Valentina's ring still pinched between his fingers. The cold of the jade had climbed from his palm all the way to the wrist. His knuckles had gone the color of old candle wax.

"How long do we have?"

"Six hours. Until the Consistory issues a new decree." Franco drew a long breath through his nose and let it out through his mouth, the way a man does when he is trying to remember the shape of his own dignity. "But the Hall's Armored Division has already acted. They went over our heads. They have welded beryllium-bronze armor plate across all three gate-valves into Golgotha. They did not even leave the inspection hatches."

"Why?"

"I do not know." Franco shook his head. "But I would guess the people at the Original Sin Bureau have begun to smell what we are doing."

The amber light pulsed once more. It caught, briefly, the seven spare barrels laid out in a neat row on the workbench — long steel tubes, cold as bone, waiting to be fitted.

Alfonso got up from the floor, dusted iron filings from his knees, and looked at Cesare. That was all. He had learned, after all these years in the Minerva Agency, that when Cesare asked questions in a voice that sounded like his, it was because the questions were the only order left.

"Chief." Alfonso's voice was low. "Do we go ahead with the original plan?"

Cesare was quiet for a few seconds.

"We go ahead with the original plan," he said. "We have no other road left."

The room said nothing.

Franco looked at them both, and then, with the careful motions of a man trying to remember how his body used to behave in public, he turned and walked out. At the door he paused.

"I am going to try the central reliquary's reserve armory. Perhaps I can scrape together a different way through."

The door closed behind him.

Alfonso crouched back down over the broken parts of the Thunder Fang. He did not ask Cesare what was coming next. He had learned this too — that when Cesare said *the original plan*, it meant he had already walked every other corridor in his head and found them all blocked. What remained was not a plan. It was a direction.

Cesare sat on the edge of the tool chest. He slid the ring onto the third finger of his right hand. The jade settled against his skin and lay there, so cold it almost felt warm.

"Alfonso."

"Mm."

"Valentina gave me the diagrams. The full original schematic of the Divine Wrath III. The ice-plain cold-jade spinal buffer is in there."

Alfonso's hands stilled on the broken barrel.

"That thing." His voice dropped. "I thought it was only a story."

"You have heard of it?"

"There was an old man on Crete. Back when the old priest was still alive, he mentioned it once." Alfonso's voice went quieter. "He said, deep in the Yenisei ice-plain, there is a stone that can freeze a man's soul. That nothing could ever eat into it. I thought he was telling us a fairy tale."

Cesare did not answer. The dim yellow of the workshop light lay across the side of Alfonso's face, and in it he looked younger than he was, and also more tired.

"The old priest," Cesare said, after a while.

"Yes," Alfonso said. He did not say more.

The room settled. Only the hum of the ventilation shaft. Only Alfonso's breathing, soft, in the cold light off the spare barrels.

——

Cesare was studying the ring on his hand when a sound came from the corridor.

Not hurried footsteps. Not Franco's contained panic.

Three light, even knocks.

*Tok. Tok. Tok.*

Metal boot-tips against the threshold.

Alfonso was the first to react. He stopped what he was doing and looked straight at the door.

Cesare looked up too.

The sound was a familiar one. Every knight in Florence knew it. It was the way the Master of the Seraph Knights Order announced himself when he entered a room — the soft triple knock of steel on stone, like a man saying *I am here* before he had even crossed the threshold.

In the corridor, the amber light flickered. A shape in a plain, washed-out knight's tunic slid past the door-slit.

At the far end of the hall, Franco had stopped to speak with one of the technicians. He saw the shape and went still for a moment.

"So the old man has finally decided to show himself," he muttered under his breath. He did not sound aggrieved. He sounded relieved.

The door of the modification room was pushed open.

Three men came in.

Not Ronsenthaldt.

Three old soldiers of the Seraph Knights Order, in plain clothes. The one in front was built like a hearth, broad across the shoulders, with a dueling scar that ran clean from his left ear to his right — taken off a Macedonian pike in the old Ceylon campaigns. The Dragon Lance Instructor.

The two behind him Cesare also knew. Hans. Karl. All three had served under him in Ceylon. After his exile they had taken their discharges and moved out to one of the small towns on the edge of Florence, where they made their living patching steam-engines and selling cheap liquor.

"Red Dragon." The Dragon Lance Instructor stood in the doorway. His voice was the color of gravel. "The Old Master sent us with something for you."

Cesare stood up.

"The Old Master?"

The Instructor did not answer. He reached into his coat, brought out a long box wrapped in black silk, and set it on the workbench with both hands. The box was no more than a foot long, half a foot wide. The silk at its corners had been rubbed pale by years of someone's hand.

"He said it was for you," the Instructor said. "He broke it himself, in the Marston Omega incident. He knew you would go and fetch it. So he left it there for you."

Cesare looked at the box.

He did not open it at once.

"What else did he say?"

The Instructor hesitated.

"He said there are not many men in the Seraph Order who deserve to hold that sword." He paused. "But he thought you were one of them."

The room seemed to grow colder.

Cesare reached out and lifted the lid.

——

Inside, the box was lined with black velvet.

On the velvet lay half a piece of metal.

It was no larger than a man's palm. The break was ragged, as though something enormous had torn the thing in two. The surface of the metal was dull to look at, but if you let your eye settle into it, you could see, deep inside the grain, a kind of ancient gold — not the yellow shine of jewelry, but a darker, older, heavier gold. The color of clotted blood. The color of time.

Cesare's breath caught.

He knew the metal.

Excalibur.

The holy regalia. Excalibur. The personal weapon of the Master of the Seraph Knights Order. Of the same source as the Longinus. Since the founding of the Order, fewer than twenty knights had ever truly been permitted to grip it.

It was the only weapon in the world that could resonate with an Omega fragment.

And now half of it lay in a black silk box on his workbench, the broken edge dark with old gold that had dried into rust.

The smell that rose from the metal was strange. Not iron. Not blood. Older. Like lotus, like beeswax, like a metal that had been smelted before men had a name for smelting.

Cesare's hand reached down. It was shaking. He closed his fingers around the broken half.

The moment the metal touched his skin, the gold sleeve at the back of his neck flared hot.

Not warm. Not even hot.

*Hot.*

He almost cried out. His right hand spasmed, almost opened.

A thread of gold-red ran across the violet of his iris, like a match struck somewhere behind his eyes.

"Chief!" Alfonso was on his feet.

"Don't." Cesare's voice was low, but very steady. "Don't come closer."

He closed his fist tighter. He closed his eyes.

A voice began in his head.

It was low. Cold. With a metallic undertone, like a battle horn heard at the far end of a winter field.

"Cesare."

Ronsenthaldt's voice.

"You were not born for vengeance."

The words were faint. They sounded far away. They sounded, also, as though they had risen from somewhere inside him.

"A knight of the Seraph should not live only inside his hate."

Cesare opened his eyes.

The gold-red thread in his iris was still moving, slowly, like something alive. He looked down at the broken metal in his palm. The dull gold of it had begun, very faintly, to brighten. The heat in the sleeve at the back of his neck was rising and falling in time with it.

"Chief." Alfonso took one step closer. He was staring at Cesare's face. "Your eyes—"

"I am fine." Cesare's voice was barely a breath.

He set the metal down on the bench, carefully, as though it were a sleeping child.

And then he saw that something else was lying in the box.

A photograph, yellowed at the edges.

——

It had been taken with ordinary silver-salt photography, decades ago. The corners were beginning to curl.

It showed a stretch of river outside Florence. The Arno. Willows along the bank.

Two figures stood by the water.

One was Ronsenthaldt. He was wearing a plain dark coat, no armor, no helm. His long hair had been blown untidy by the wind, and the cold, carved look he usually wore was gone. He was, in fact, smiling.

It was a smile Cesare had almost never seen on a living face.

Beside him stood a girl. Very slight, very fair. Hair the color of weak honey. Eyes the same pale blue as Ronsenthaldt's. She was wearing a white dress; the wind had taken the hem of it, and you could see the small pale bones of her shins. She was laughing. She was laughing harder than Ronsenthaldt.

Tiran.

Cesare's fingers stopped on the photograph.

"This—"

"The Old Master kept it to himself," the Instructor said, very quietly. "He never let anyone see it."

Cesare turned the photograph over. On the back, in Ronsenthaldt's small, careful hand, the ink long faded, a single line had been written.

*A knight of the Seraph should not live only inside his hate. He should defend the things he wants to defend.*

Cesare read the line.

He thought of a number of things at once. He thought of Ronsenthaldt in the training yard of the Order, teaching them swordwork, never saying *be stronger*, never saying *be victorious*, only ever saying *stay alive*. He thought of the rainy night Ronsenthaldt had walked out of Florence, a black shape dissolving into the rain while Cesare stood on the city wall and watched. He thought of the last time he had seen him, at the Marston Omega incident, when Ronsenthaldt had pressed a single bullet into his palm and said *you were not born for vengeance*.

"Where is he now?" Cesare asked.

The Instructor hesitated.

"He is just outside."

Cesare looked up.

——

The door of the modification room was still open.

The amber light in the corridor was still pulsing.

A figure in a plain, washed-out knight's tunic was standing in the shadow of the wall.

He was leaning against the stone, one shoulder dropped slightly, as though he had been waiting for a long time.

It was Ronsenthaldt.

He was thinner than Cesare remembered. The cheekbones stood out more. The eyes were deeper in their sockets. His long hair, unbound, lay on his shoulders, and at the ends it had gone the color of old snow.

But the back was straight. The back was always straight.

Cesare stood up.

"Master," he said.

Ronsenthaldt looked at him. He did not speak at once.

His eyes — not violet, never violet, the deep blue they had always been — rested on Cesare's eyes for a moment.

He saw the gold-red line that was still moving, slowly, in Cesare's iris.

"So you saw it," Ronsenthaldt said. Not a question. A fact.

"Yes."

"I left it in the fragment," Ronsenthaldt said. "Only you could hear it."

Cesare did not ask why.

Ronsenthaldt pushed himself off the wall.

"Tomorrow," he said.

His voice was low.

"Do not be rash. Follow the plan."

He looked at Cesare. The deep blue of his eyes held there for a few seconds.

"You still have family. You still have friends."

He said this, and then he turned, and walked away.

Cesare did not follow him.

He knew that Ronsenthaldt would not let him follow.

——

The air in the modification room began, very slowly, to warm.

The Dragon Lance Instructor cleared his throat. He closed the lid of the black silk box.

"Red Dragon. We will leave it with you, then. The Old Master says, use the fragment well."

"I will."

"We—" The Instructor glanced at Hans and Karl behind him. "We will be there tomorrow, too."

Cesare looked up at him.

"You?"

"The Old Master said, whether we go is our own choice." The Instructor paused. "But the three of us have talked. We go."

He was quiet for a moment, and then he said, very evenly:

"To stake our lives on the Red Dragon is not a bad bargain."

He turned. He and Hans and Karl walked out together, three old men in plain clothes, shoulder to shoulder, into the amber light of the corridor.

——

The room was quiet again.

Alfonso crouched back down by the bench. He picked up a spare barrel and began to turn it over in his hands. His movements were slower than before. His eyes kept sliding off the work.

"Chief."

"Mm."

"Where do you think Taras is right now?"

Cesare looked at him.

"He is in the Eagle's Nest. Did you need him for something urgent?"

Alfonso did not answer at once.

He stood up. He set the barrel back on the bench. He brushed the iron filings from his trouser legs.

"I am going to step out for a moment," he said.

Cesare looked at him, and nodded.

Alfonso was almost at the door when he stopped.

He reached up to his own throat and untied something from around his neck.

It was a thin silver chain, and at the end of the chain hung a small gear-shaped pendant. The metal was brass, rubbed to a deep shine, the gear-teeth picked out in fine, careful engraving.

Cesare had never seen Alfonso wear it.

"What is that?" he asked.

Alfonso looked down at the pendant. A moment passed before he answered.

"It was given to me by the old priest. When I was a boy."

Cesare did not speak.

"He said," Alfonso's voice was very quiet, "that if you wore it, everything you did would go smoothly."

He tucked the pendant back under his collar.

"Wait for me to come back."

Then he went out.

Cesare watched him go. A small line drew itself between his brows.

It was rare, to see Alfonso like that.

——

Alfonso walked down the corridor toward the Eagle's Nest.

The corridor was full of Minerva technicians now, moving fast, their faces tight. The news that the Hall's Armored Division had welded the Golgotha gates had spread; everyone could feel that something was coming.

Alfonso did not look at any of them.

He was thinking about the gear pendant.

The old priest on Crete. The man who had taken him in. The fire in which the old priest had died. He had not even had time to take anything with him from that church. He had run out barefoot, with nothing, except this one pendant — the last gift the old priest had ever given him, the Christmas he was thirteen.

*The old priest had said: if you wear it, everything you do will go smoothly.*

Alfonso had not, in fifteen years, believed him.

But tomorrow he was going to war.

And after tomorrow, perhaps he would not come back.

The door of the Eagle's Nest was half-closed. A thin green-blue light leaked out of the gap. Inside, Taras was at the console, his hands moving fast over the keys, piloting some of the Sword Dancer's systems from a distance.

"Taras." Alfonso stood in the doorway.

Taras's hands paused.

He turned. He saw who it was, and one brow went up a fraction.

"What is it? Is something wrong?"

Alfonso walked in and pulled the door shut behind him.

The Eagle's Nest was not large. It was full of cable and console, and the air in it tasted faintly of burnt wire.

Alfonso sat down on the edge of the console next to Taras. He opened his mouth. He closed it again. He opened it once more.

"I—" he began, and stopped.

Taras looked at him, the beginnings of confusion in his eyes.

"Alfonso? Are you all right?"

"Yes."

"Then what—"

"Taras." Alfonso said the name, and stopped again.

Taras let his hands fall still on the keys.

Alfonso reached into his collar and took out the gear pendant. He laid it in his palm and held it out, between them.

"This," he said. "It was given to me by the old priest. Before he died."

Taras said nothing.

"I have worn it fifteen years. I have never shown it to anyone."

His voice went lower.

"Tomorrow. I may not come back from it."

He saw Taras's expression change.

"Alfonso—"

"So I wanted—" Alfonso looked up and met Taras's eyes. "I wanted to leave it with you. Just for safekeeping."

Taras went very still.

Alfonso's fingers were trembling. His voice, somehow, was not.

"When this is over," he said. "I—"

He drew a long breath.

"I love you."

Something moved behind Taras's eyes.

"Would you," Alfonso said, more quietly, "when this is over — come back with me to Crete?"

The Eagle's Nest was quiet for a few seconds.

Taras looked at the gear pendant in Alfonso's palm. He looked at Alfonso's face.

"Alfonso."

"Yes."

"Do you know how far Crete is from Florence?"

"Yes."

"What will you do when you get there?"

"I don't know." Alfonso's voice was very even. "I want to take you there. I want you to see it."

Taras was quiet for a long moment.

Then he reached out and took the pendant from Alfonso's palm.

"All right," he said.

A small light kindled somewhere behind Alfonso's eyes.

"But you have to come back alive," Taras said.

Alfonso nodded.

"I will."

Taras put the gear pendant carefully into the small tool-box at his belt.

"I will wait for you," he said.

——

When Alfonso came out of the Eagle's Nest, the amber light in the corridor had gone the color of bone.

The Minerva Agency's highest-grade alert was sounding.

He stood still for a moment. Then he began to walk, faster than before, back toward the modification room.

The door was still open.

Cesare was not at the workbench.

Standing at the workbench, in his place, was a man in the uniform of the Heretical Tribunal — a lieutenant-colonel's insignia, the black angel pinned at his breast. His face was unreadable.

Licinius.

Alfonso stopped in the doorway.

"Lieutenant-Colonel Licinius," he said.

Licinius turned his head.

"Alfonso."

"What are you doing here?"

"Delivering something." Licinius turned back to the bench. He set down, carefully, a small rectangle of metal.

Cesare was standing beside the bench, looking at it.

It was a piece of silver-white alloy the size of a man's palm. On its surface was stamped the six-winged owl of the Minerva, and beside the owl, in very small letters, Alfonso had to lean in to read them: *Sealed by the First Director of the Minerva. Perfect Solder.*

"Perfect Solder," Cesare said, very quietly.

"Yes," Licinius said. "Something the First Director left behind. It can repair any metal. Any metal the Holy See can produce."

Alfonso's eyes widened.

"Can it repair the Thunder Fang?"

"It can."

Cesare looked up at Licinius.

"Where did you find it?"

"In a disused passage behind the Wall of Sighs." Licinius paused. "The First Director had sealed it in a small box. There was a cog stamped into the lid of the box."

He paused again.

"There was also a note inside the box."

"What did it say?"

"It said—" Licinius looked at Cesare.

"*For whoever needs it most.*"

The room was quiet for a few seconds.

Cesare reached out and picked the small piece of alloy up.

It was cool in his hand. There was nothing remarkable about it.

Alfonso had already brought the broken Thunder Fang barrel over and set it down next to Cesare's hand.

"Chief," he said.

Cesare pressed the Perfect Solder to the place where the metal was broken.

——

The moment the alloy touched the break, the whole room lit up.

Not electric light. Not fire.

Gold.

A flood of gold, springing from the place where the two pieces met — the same gold, warm and ancient, the color of clotted blood, that the metal in the back of Cesare's neck was made of. The walls of the modification room went bright as snow.

Cesare's hand was almost numb with the shock of it, but the alloy seemed to have been pulled into the break by a force of its own; it was moving, slowly, of its own accord, knitting the two pieces of the barrel back into one.

"This—" Alfonso stepped back, his face a mask. "What—"

Licinius had gone still.

Only Cesare did not move. He watched the gold pour from under his fingers. Slowly, the violet of his eyes began to fill with the same gold-red thread that had moved in them when he had first touched the Excalibur fragment. The sleeve at the back of his neck was hot now — *hot* — as though someone had set a coal against his spine.

The voice of Ronsenthaldt came back into his head.

*You were not born for vengeance.*

And behind it, another voice.

A woman's voice. Soft, with the scent of lotus in it.

*The roots of the World Tree are in the east. The leaves are in the west.*

*Only when the two are joined can anything be ended.*

Cesare's eyes opened.

The gold was already fading.

The Perfect Solder was gone.

The Thunder Fang barrel lay whole on the bench.

No seam. No scar. As if it had never been broken.

"Chief—" Alfonso took a step forward, staring at it.

Cesare did not answer.

He turned, and looked toward the open door of the modification room.

The bone-white light in the corridor was still pulsing. Down the hall, the technicians of the Minerva were hurrying past in clusters, voices sharp, the sound of boots and shouted orders.

Franco had said it. The highest-grade alert meant that the Consistory had already issued its open decree.

Cesare looked down at the repaired barrel in his hand. He looked at the broken half of Excalibur in its black silk box, on the bench.

Tomorrow.

Tomorrow he would go to war.

But he was not tired yet, tonight.

He was going to fit the new barrel into the Thunder Fang.

And then he was going to go and find the men who would stand beside him.

— *He still had family. He still had friends.*

— *He had to make sure they all came back alive.*

The barrel, in the bone-white light, gave off a low gold gleam of its own.

From somewhere far off, down the corridors, a bell began to ring.

The Minerva bell.

Four in the morning.

第160章 不朽之王的第一次苏醒

凌晨四点的密涅瓦机关像一头被惊醒的铁兽。

西泽尔没去看那面屏幕。

等待审判，或等待反抗。

叹息之墙。

"这里不是骷髅地。"西泽尔说。

"这是熔岩之心地下一层的封闭实验场。"薇若兰说，"三百年前达芬奇·波吉亚亲手设计的，用来做不朽之王系统的最初测试。"

西泽尔猛地转头看向她。

"我母亲？"

"她不是你的母亲。"薇若兰的声音很轻，但每个字都像铁钉，"她是初代总工程师三百年前标注在设计图上的第一个实验体。达芬奇·波吉亚留下那张图的时候，你的母亲还没有出生。"

铁门缓缓开启。

新红龙。

它比西泽尔在密涅瓦机关大库里见过的那具更完整——通体赭红色鳞片状甲板在暗红灯光下泛着暗金色的光泽，肩部刻着叶尼塞夔龙纹，背部的双动力核心舱还在微微发出蒸汽逸散的嘶嘶声。

但它的右臂——

右臂上装着一把枪管极细的连射铳，铳身镀着淡金色的秘银膜。那是阿方索用缴获的雷霆牙残骸改装的备用枪管，但此刻它的颜色不再是银灰——而是温暖的、像凝固血液一样的金色。

完美焊料的颜色。

"上去。"薇若兰说。

西泽尔没有犹豫。

他沿着新红龙胫部的攀爬阶梯向上走，每一步都伴随着金属靴底撞击铍青铜的清脆声响。新红龙的骑士舱在他接近时自动张开，像一头沉睡的铁兽张开了胸腔。

他坐了进去。

黄金套管与神经接驳端口接触的瞬间，他听到了自己的心跳。

不是比喻——他真的听到了自己心脏跳动的回声，从胸腔传遍全身，每一跳都像是有什么东西在敲打他的脊椎深处。电极从背后刺入，沿着金色针头游走，和他的神经系统接驳。

"开始神经校准。"薇若兰的声音从外部通讯器中传来，"放松，不要抗拒甲胄的侵入。"

他放松了。

新红龙的"龙骑兵套装"开始在他身上合拢——胸甲、腹甲、胯甲，依次锁定，秘金骨架和精钢外板将他完全包裹。黄金套管完全嵌入后颈，镀秘银的表面与神经末梢咬合，那种热度从颈椎一路烧到了脑干。

"神经耦合度……"薇若兰的声音顿了一下，"百分之七十二……七十九……八十五……九十三……"

"好快。"通讯器里传来佛朗哥沙哑的声音，他显然在升降梯外面的某个监控室里。

"九十六……九十八……"薇若兰的声音开始变调，"一百零三……一百零七……"

西泽尔的身体开始发烫。

不是外部温度——是内部。他的血液在沸腾，脊椎里的电流像一条发光的蛇在爬行，从骶骨一路窜上后脑，又从后脑灌入四肢。每一根神经都在尖叫，每一条血管都在燃烧。

他闻到了莲花的气味。

不是密涅瓦机关仓库里那股工业油污和红水银的刺鼻味道，是真正的莲花——锡兰王都莲花池里盛开的白色莲花，沾着清晨的露水，在他童年的记忆最深处开着。

"一百一十二……一百一十五……"薇若兰的声音已经在颤抖了。

"不可能……"佛朗哥的声音传来，带着难以置信的颤抖，"龙德施泰特的历史纪录是九十二……一百二十……"

"接入未知意识。"薇若兰的声音突然变了。

主控台上的神经诊断仪表盘剧烈跳动，所有指示灯从绿色瞬间切换成红色——然后熄灭。仪表盘的指针疯狂旋转了几圈后猛地定在了某个刻度上，一个从未在密涅瓦机关数据库中出现过的代码被自动打印在纸带上：

残枝01号（锡兰末代星见）

"停——"薇若兰伸手去抓外接电击的开关。

但西泽尔的手先动了。

他不知道那只手是怎么动的——他感觉不到自己的手指，但他能看到它们。新红龙的手甲从座位扶手上抬起，金属手指张开，像是在抓什么东西。然后他听到了自己的声音——

不，不是他的声音。

那是一个女人的声音。轻柔的，像莲花香气一样的，从他的喉咙里涌出，带着一种古老到近乎神话的韵律：

"教皇把我囚禁在圣女塔。"

每一个字都像一块烧红的铁。

"把我的心做成欧米茄的核心。"

新红龙的眼睛亮了。不是原本的暗红微光——是一种浓烈的金红色，像两团燃烧的莲花，映在圆形实验场铍青铜墙壁上，像两轮小小的太阳。

"把我的血做成红水银的引火剂。"

西泽尔的紫瞳深处，浮现出另一双眼睛。

金红色的，竖瞳的，完整的一双眼睛——

那不是他的眼睛。

那是苏伽罗的眼睛。锡兰末代星见的眼睛，末代王女的眼睛，被教皇囚禁在圣女塔中数十年、最后被剖出心脏做成欧米茄核心的那个女人的眼睛。那双眼睛在他的瞳孔深处燃烧着，像两颗埋在灰烬下的余烬被风吹得复燃。

新红龙开始动了。

它从圆台上站起来，金属关节发出咯咯的声响，胸口的甲板像鳞片一样一片片张开，释放出大量灼热的蒸汽——不是普通的红色蒸汽，是金色的，带着莲花香气的蒸汽。它在圆形实验场里踉跄地迈出一步，铍青铜的地面被它的靴底踩出深深的凹痕。

"电击！"佛朗哥的声音从通讯器里炸了出来。

薇若兰的手已经碰到了外接电击的开关——那个黄铜把手一旦拉下，三万伏的高压电流就会从备用电极直接灌入新红龙的骑士舱，将里面的一切神经联结瞬间烧断。

她的手指扣住了把手。

"别碰她！"

西泽尔的声音突然从新红龙的外部扩音器中炸出来——但那不是苏伽罗的声音，是他自己的声音，嘶哑的、带着金属质地的回响。新红龙巨大的铁手猛地伸出，五根金属手指扣住了薇若兰的手腕。

力道之大，几乎要捏碎骨头。

薇若兰痛得吸了一口冷气，但她没有叫出声。她低头看着那只手——金色纹路正在金属手甲上游走，像活物一样。她的手电筒照亮了西泽尔的脸。

他的脸在流泪。

不是汗水，不是红水银蒸汽凝结的水珠，是真正的泪水——从那双紫瞳深处浮现金红色竖瞳的眼睛里涌出来，沿着苍白的脸颊滑落，滴在秘金面甲上，嗤嗤地蒸发。

"她是我妈妈的一部分。"西泽尔说，声音平静得可怕，但每个字都在颤抖，"她在说话。让我听完。"

新红龙的动作停滞了。

那双金红色的竖瞳缓缓闭合，金色蒸汽也慢慢消散。苏伽罗的声音从扩音器中再次响起，但这次更轻了，更远了，像从极深的水底浮上来的气泡：

"世界树的根在东方，叶在西方。"

"只有两者合一，才能结束一切。"

话音落下。

金红色纹路从西泽尔的瞳孔深处褪去，竖瞳消失，只剩下他自己的、带着血丝的紫瞳。新红龙胸口的甲板合拢，蒸汽停止喷出，金属关节的咯咯声消失了。整具甲胄像是被抽走了灵魂，沉重地、缓缓地、带着金铁交鸣的巨响坐回了圆台上。

西泽尔昏了过去。

他的手从薇若兰的手腕上松开——金属手指完全失去了力道，手甲垂落在身侧，握成了松散的拳。

薇若兰低头看了一眼自己的手腕。

青紫了一圈。

"……混蛋。"她轻声骂了一句，但眼睛却红了。

圆形实验场里恢复了寂静。热风从通风管道里吹过来，带着硫磺和古老金属的气味。暗红灯光在新红龙赭红色的甲板上投下长长的影子。

十五分钟。

阿方索在升降梯外面数着秒。他的手一直按在腰间的枪套上，背靠着冰冷的铁壁，听着铁笼子另一侧传来的闷响。当他数到第十四分五十八秒时，铁笼子的门缓缓打开了。

佛朗哥从里面走出来。

他的脸色比之前更白了，灰色长风衣上沾满了红水银蒸汽凝结的水珠。他一手撑着升降梯的铁壁，一手捂住嘴，咳嗽声像是把肺都要咳出来。手帕上那块暗红色扩大了一倍。

"老板怎么样？"阿方索问。

"活着。"佛朗哥咳完，喘了几口气，"不朽之王系统首次激活完成。耦合度峰值——一百二十。"

阿方索愣住了。

"一百二十？"

"一百二十。"佛朗哥用手帕擦掉嘴角的血，"比龙德施泰特的九十二还高二十八个百分点。但更让我担心的是——"他转过头看向铁笼子里，"刚才系统接入了一个未登记的意识体。"

"什么意识体？"

"锡兰末代星见的残枝意识。也就是……苏伽罗。"

阿方索的脸色变了。

佛朗哥没有继续说下去。他只是抬头看着升降梯铁笼的顶部——那里有一个小小的通风口，暗红灯光从通风口里透出来，带着微弱的金红色光晕。

"还有一件事。"他喃喃道。

"什么？"

佛朗哥没有回答。他盯着通风口的方向，眼睛一眨不眨。

阿方索顺着他的目光看过去——

在通风口的金色光晕中，有什么东西在动。

那是新红龙的右臂——阿方索亲手装上去的那只龙臂。那根被完美焊料修复如初的雷霆牙枪管，正在自行旋转。

没有人触碰它。

枪管缓慢地、匀速地转动了三圈——三圈，一百八十度，一百八十度——然后抬起枪口，对准了圆形实验场的穹顶。

一声轻响。

不是爆炸，不是雷鸣，而是一声极轻的、像莲花花瓣绽放一样的嗤响。

枪口喷出了一道极细的火焰。

不是普通的红水银火焰——那火焰是金色的，带着莲花、蜜蜡、玫瑰和古老金属混合的奇异香气。它从枪口射出，穿过圆形实验场十几米的空间，在穹顶的铍青铜板上划出了一道完美的弧线——像一朵在钢铁上绽放的金色莲花。

香气弥漫开来。

佛朗哥盯着那道火焰，喃喃自语：

"红水银引火剂的力量？不……"他转过身，眼睛里映着金色的光，"是锡兰星见血的力量。"

穹顶上那朵金色的莲花还在发光。

【English】
Chapter 160 — The First Awakening of the Immortal King

Four in the morning, and the Minerva Bureau woke like a startled iron beast.

The pale signal-lamps on the ceiling were still pulsing, their sickly white light catching the brass frames of the overhead screens, where line after line of the Conclave's open summons scrolled past — All personnel of the Minerva Bureau are to lay down arms and surrender within three hours. Those who refuse will be tried for treason. The technicians stood in small clusters, exchanging glances that said nothing and everything. The iron-gray corridors were thick with low cursing and the urgent percussion of metal boots on stone.

Cesare did not look at the screens.

He sat at the workbench in the modification room, and in front of him lay the things he had carried out of the fire: the barrel of the Thunderfang, repaired to seamless wholeness with a solder no one in the Bureau had ever seen before; a golden ticket; the spare parts of a black-handled sidearm; and a silver ring set with a chip of cold jade from the frost-plains, the broken edge of the stone still sharp as a winter knife. The barrel was the worst of them to look at. There was no crack, no seam, no hint that it had ever been broken. Under the pale lamps it carried a faint warm gold on its surface, as though it had been soaked in some old, old blood.

"Fit it back," he said.

Alfonso was already wearing his goggles. From beneath the workbench he dragged out the right arm of the New Red Dragon — the whole machine, in truth, was a stopgap the Bureau had hammered together in a single night, two sizes smaller than the proper model, a third of its russet plating riveted on in haste, the left shoulder still missing a plate that left the silver cables beneath exposed to the air. But the right arm was whole, and the weapon-mount in the wrist was the exact shape and size of the Thunderfang's barrel.

"Boss, I've re-bolted the barrel to the steam conduit," Alfonso said, his hands busy on a hex-bolt, "but the new barrel's almost three-tenths lighter than the original. The ballistics are going to drift. I need to recalibrate the—"

"Tomorrow."

"But the first deployment is tomorrow morning."

"Then tonight."

Alfonso said nothing more. He knew that tonight, from the boss's mouth, meant no one in this room was going to sleep. Yet his own eyes were already streaked red with broken capillaries, and his hands were steadier than they had any right to be. Each hex-bolt seated itself with a clean click, like a drummer tapping a snare.

Don Juan leaned against the doorframe, peeling a chestnut roasted to a deep amber. He was not, tonight, performing. No quips, no flourishes, no mischief — a thing so rare in him that it would have been funny, if the room had been in the mood for funny. Quintilian sat on the floor beside him, cradling a small tin can in his lap. The can held ginger soup that Mother Elen had sent by courier, and it had gone cold. Every few minutes Quintilian lifted the lid and breathed in the steam.

"What about the Excalibur shard Rundstedt sent us?" Don Juan asked. "Do you want that fitted too?"

Cesare shook his head.

He reached into the black brocade case and took out the shard. It was smaller than his palm, the broken face of it like the edge of shattered ice, still crusted with a stain of dried gold blood. Held in the hand it felt like a sleeping heart. When its broken edge brushed the new gold coupling at the back of his neck, the shard warmed, and the faint gold-red lines around the coupling stirred and brightened.

"This one," he said, "I keep for myself. It is not a weapon."

Don Juan did not ask again.

The clock on the modification-room wall read 4:15. In the corridor the pale lamps at last went out, and what replaced them was a deeper red — the Bureau's own lowest state of alert. The Conclave's open order had been delivered. What came next was the wait.

Wait for a trial, or wait for a fight.

"We're going," Cesare said. He tucked the shard against his chest and stood.

"Where?" Quintilian asked.

"Vesuvius."

The three words fell into the room like three iron nails driven into oak.

Alfonso's hands stopped on the bolt. Don Juan's hands stopped on the chestnut. Quintilian looked up slowly, his brown eyes lit in the red.

"Tonight?" Don Juan said.

"Tonight." Cesare was already at the door. "Professor Franco needs to test the Immortal King system. The plan was to do a final check tomorrow morning, before deployment, but—" he paused a half-beat, "—there is no tomorrow."

From the end of the corridor came the heavy iron groan of a door unsealing. It was the Bureau's private lift to the deep foundations under Vesuvius — a great steam-driven cage normally reserved for the special test articles that required the heat of the Magma-Heart below.

Franco stood beside it. The hem of his long gray coat was lifted by the hot air rising from the vents, and beneath it his hands were wrapped in strips of sticking-plaster. When he saw Cesare's group approach he coughed twice, drew a handkerchief from his pocket, and held it against his mouth. When he took it away there was a dark red print in the middle of it.

"You came," he said. His voice was rougher than usual. "Good. Another hour and I would have had to blow the lift. It descends to the first sub-level of the Magma-Heart — the only place in the mountain where tonight's test can be run."

"Why only there?" Cesare asked.

"Because the first time the Immortal King system wakes," Franco said, and his violet eyes were very cold, very tired, and very full of something he did not name, "it may lose control."

"Lose control to what degree?"

"I don't know." Franco looked at him. "The first words Leonardo Borgia wrote on the system's blueprint, three hundred years ago, were: Whoever wears this will not outlive three years. The second words were—" his throat moved, "—Once the system activates, if the knight's ancient consciousness is roused, it cannot be ended by ordinary means."

"So you mean to seal us into the first sub-level."

"I mean to seal the whole first sub-level." Franco said. "Every entrance shut, every gate electrified. No living thing — including an out-of-control you — comes out."

Alfonso's hand drifted, almost without his noticing, to the holster at his hip.

"I go with him," Don Juan said.

"No." Franco shook his head. "The instant the system activates it will produce a high-frequency neural pulse strong enough to drop anyone within a hundred meters unconscious inside three seconds. Only Veronica can stay at the main console to operate the emergency electrocution — only she can stay conscious long enough inside those three seconds."

"And me?" Alfonso asked.

"You wait outside the lift." Franco looked at him. "The Thunderfang you rebuilt is fitted to the New Red Dragon's arm, yes?"

"Fitted."

"Good. If we don't give a signal inside fifteen minutes, you drag the New Red Dragon out of the lift, take it up to the surface, and surrender."

Surrender.

Out of Franco's mouth, the word landed harder than any curse. No one in the room moved to answer it.

Cesare stood in front of the iron cage, looking down at the slowly opening throat beneath it. Hot air came pouring up out of the shaft, carrying with it the smell of sulfur, of red mercury vapor, and of some older, stranger metal. The cage swayed on its cables in the rising heat, the wire hawser groaning somewhere in the dark.

He stepped in.

Veronica was already inside.

She was not, today, in her cheongsam. She was in the standard black coverall of a Minerva Bureau technician, her long hair pulled into a high tail that left the long column of her neck bare. On her left wrist she wore six electrode rings; in her right hand she held a forked neural-diagnostic cable. When she saw him she did not smile, only lifted her chin a little.

"Ready?" she asked.

"Are you?"

"Me?" Veronica tilted her head, and the corner of her mouth made the smallest possible curve. "I've been disassembling Seraph-class armors since I was eight. There is nothing left in this world I am not ready for."

The cage began to descend.

The wind rose, the heat rose, layer after layer of it, and the cage dropped down into the mountain. Cesare closed his eyes and felt the push of the acceleration against his chest. The new gold coupling at the back of his neck began to warm — not from outside, but from inside itself, as if something that lived in the metal was beginning to stir.

"What is the 'ancient consciousness' Franco was talking about?" Veronica asked suddenly.

"Sukara," Cesare said.

Veronica was quiet for a long time.

The cage began to slow. From below, a dark red light rose to meet them — the eternal glow of the Magma-Heart, filtered through the thick high-temperature glass that lined the ventilation shafts running through the mountain's interior. The cage came to rest at last in front of a black iron door, five meters tall, ancient and thick, its face unmarked by any of the Bureau's six-winged owl, but layered instead in soot-black iron seals, each seal stamped with one of three holy emblems: the inverted World Tree, the closed-eye lotus, the entwined gears.

This was not Golgotha.

"This is not the chamber at Golgotha," Cesare said.

"It's the sealed testing floor of the first sub-level of the Magma-Heart," Veronica said. "Designed three hundred years ago, by Leonardo Borgia himself, for the very first activation of the Immortal King system."

"How do you know that?"

"Because three hundred years ago, the knight who first put on the armor of Saint George the Dragon-slayer was activated for the first time in this very room." She looked at him. "Nirala."

Cesare turned his head sharply.

"My mother?"

"She is not your mother." Veronica's voice was quiet, but every word fell like a nail into wood. "She is the first experimental subject, marked on Leonardo Borgia's design three hundred years before your mother was born."

The iron door began to open.

Behind it lay a vast circular space, the dome of it so high that the eye lost it in the dark. The walls were beryllium-bronze plates, layer upon layer of them, every plate carved through with holy sigils and gear-patterns. On the central platform of the chamber stood a suit of mobile armor, glowing faintly in the dark red light.

The New Red Dragon.

It was more complete than any of the Bureau's archived models. Its plating of overlapping russet scales caught the dim glow and threw it back as a dull gold. Across its shoulders ran the engraved pattern of the Yenisei kui-dragon, and from the twin power-cores in its back came the soft hiss of steam venting into the dark. Across the muzzle plating, two dim sensor-eyes glowed the cold standby cyan-blue of an unawakened system.

But the right arm—

On the right arm was mounted a rapid-fire gun of very narrow bore, the barrel plated with a film of pale silver-gold. It was the spare barrel Alfonso had forged from the salvaged Thunderfang — yet the silver-gray of the metal had changed. In this light it was warm. It was the color of blood that had been allowed to cool.

The color of the perfect solder.

"Get in," Veronica said.

Cesare did not hesitate.

He climbed the ladder set into the New Red Dragon's shin, each step ringing under his metal-soled boot against the beryllium-bronze. As he approached, the cockpit of the armor opened of its own accord — the way the chest of a sleeping iron beast opens.

He sat down inside.

The instant the gold coupling at the back of his neck met the neural port, he heard his own heartbeat.

Not as a figure of speech. He heard it. The echo of his own heart traveled out of his chest and into every part of him, each beat feeling as if something far down in his spine was being struck with a hammer. Electrodes slid in along his back, fine as golden needles, and locked themselves into his nervous system.

"Beginning neural calibration," Veronica's voice came over the external comm. "Relax. Don't fight the armor's intrusion."

He relaxed.

The Dragon-Rider's panoply began to close over him — breastplate, belly-plate, cod-piece, locking one after another, the secret-gold skeleton and the fine steel outer plating sealing him in. The gold coupling seated itself fully into the back of his neck, its silver-plated surface meshing with the nerve-endings there, and the heat of it ran from his cervical vertebrae all the way up into the brain-stem.

"Neural coupling," Veronica said. "Seventy-two … seventy-nine … eighty-five … ninety-three …"

"Fast," Franco's rough voice said from the monitoring room somewhere above the lift. "Faster than—"

"Ninety-six … ninety-eight …" Veronica's voice was changing pitch. "One hundred and three … one hundred and seven …"

Cesare's body began to burn.

Not from the outside. From within. His blood was coming to a boil. The current in his spine was like a luminous snake climbing — out of the sacrum, up through the back of his skull, and then pouring down into the four limbs. Every nerve in him was screaming. Every vein in him was on fire.

He smelled lotus.

Not the industrial oil and red-mercury smell of the Bureau's storerooms. Real lotus — the white lotuses of the royal ponds in the Ceylon capital, dew still on the petals, blooming somewhere in the deepest well of his childhood memory.

"One hundred and twelve … one hundred and fifteen …" Veronica was trembling.

"That is not—" Franco's voice came through the comm, shaking with disbelief. "Rundstedt's historic record was ninety-two. He never—"

"Unknown consciousness interfacing," Veronica said. Her voice had changed.

On the main console, the neural-diagnostic display went wild. Every indicator lamp flicked from green to red and then died. The needle on the meter spun through three full revolutions and then slammed to a stop at a value the Minerva Bureau's database had no record of. A code never before seen was printed automatically onto a paper tape:

BRANCH 01 — LAST STARGAZER OF CEYLON

"Cut it—" Veronica reached for the emergency electrocution lever.

But Cesare's hand moved first.

He did not know how the hand had moved. He could not feel his own fingers, but he could see them. The gauntlet of the New Red Dragon lifted itself off the arm of the cockpit, the metal fingers spreading as if grasping at something. Then he heard his own voice—

No. Not his voice.

A woman's voice. Soft, and faintly smelling of lotus, rising out of his throat with a cadence so old it was almost a hymn:

"The Pope imprisoned me in the Saintess Tower."

Every word was a piece of red-hot iron.

"They made my heart into the core of the Omega."

Where the dim cyan-blue of the sensor-eyes had been, light came on — not the dull red of the standby, but a deep gold-red, like two burning lotuses, thrown back from the beryllium-bronze walls of the chamber like two small suns.

"They made my blood the primer for red mercury."

Deep in Cesare's violet irises, another pair of eyes appeared.

Gold-red, slit-pupiled, complete, a second pair—

Not his eyes.

Sukara's eyes. The eyes of the last Stargazer of Ceylon, the eyes of the last princess, the woman the Pope had kept locked in the Saintess Tower for decades before finally taking her heart from her chest to make the Omega's core. Those eyes burned in the depths of his pupils, like embers buried in ash that a wind had found again.

The New Red Dragon began to move.

It stood up off the central platform, the metal joints in it grinding like teeth, the plates over its chest opening one by one like scales to release great gouts of scalding steam — not the ordinary red steam, but golden steam, steam that smelled of lotus. The armor took a single staggering step across the chamber floor, and the beryllium-bronze beneath its boot dimpled inward under the weight.

"Electrocute!" Franco's voice exploded from the comm.

Veronica's hand had already closed around the brass lever of the external electrocution. The moment she pulled it down, thirty thousand volts would pour from the standby electrodes straight into the cockpit of the New Red Dragon, and every neural link inside it would burn out in less than a heartbeat.

叶素理。

这个名字在他的记忆里并不陌生。马斯顿的教堂，暴雨夜，那个穿东方丝绸长袍的男人从阴影里走出来，手里拿着一枚铜钱——不是教皇国的铜钱，是东方式的圆形方孔钱，上面铸着山纹和水纹。他当时站在人群之外，脸上的表情被兜帽遮住了大半，只露出一双冷静到近乎残忍的眼睛。

还有那面旗帜。

紫底白狼头。中山之狼。

他记得那个旗帜就挂在教堂角落的柱子上，被雨水打湿，垂下了一半。

"要让他进来吗？"广播里的值班军官又问了一遍。

佛朗哥看了西泽尔一眼。

西泽尔沉默了几秒钟。他伸手把冰原寒玉银戒指摘了下来，在暗红色的灯光下转了一圈，又戴回去。戒指内圈刻着的那个名字被灯光映成了一条细细的暗纹。

"让他进来，"他说，"但准备好武器。"

舱室里所有人都看着他。

"叶素理不是来送礼的，"西泽尔把银匙放回碗里，声音平静得像在陈述天气，"他是来看我们怎么死的。"

舱室的惨白灯光还在闪着。佛朗哥咳了一声，把那张手帕重新塞回白大褂的内袋里。

"我去安排，"他说，"你们都留在这里。"

舱门在他身后合上了。铆钉之间那些细密的铜管嗡嗡地响着，像是整座密涅瓦机关都在低声说一个名字。

叶素理。

【English】
Chapter 161 — The New Seraphim's Tactical Division

Cæsare woke first to the smell of cream.

It was thick, almost indecent, laced with a thin bright thread of strawberry. The kind of sweetness that belongs in a child's hand on a hot afternoon, not in the belly of a war machine. He lay still for a long moment, letting the scent find him, and only then did the other senses come back — the low iron ceiling, the dim red glow of a half-power steam-lamp, the thin grey blanket over his chest with the faint raised weave of a six-winged owl along its hem. Minerva-issue. Rest-cabin standard. He had been in enough of them to know.

Copper piping ran between the rivets along the bulkhead. Inside the pipes something hummed — a low, patient sound, almost a heartbeat. The floor under his cheek was cold plate. The bed beneath him was narrow and hard. Someone had tucked the blanket in too tight, the way a sister does who is not quite sure if the patient is still breathing.

He turned his head.

They were all there.

Valentina sat on the folding stool at his bedside, golden hair loose over one shoulder, the white of her boots cool against the dark iron floor. The smell of honeysuckle came from her — faint, familiar, out of place in a metal room. She held an iron first-aid box against her chest like a child clutches a doll. When his eyes found her, something moved through her deep-blue gaze — a quick, bright tremor of water, gone before it had a name.

Alfonso leaned against the bulkhead with his arms folded. His face was unreadable, but there were broken veins in the whites of his eyes. He had not slept. Quintilian sat across from him on a second folding stool that groaned under his shoulders, his brows drawn down hard, and the moment he saw Cæsare's eyes open the tension in his jaw went out of him all at once, like a great hound that has finally heard its master's voice. Don Juan crouched in a corner for once without a word, turning a small red flower by its stem between his thumb and forefinger. Belle stood the farthest back, hands folded, eyes faintly reddened, mouth set tight as if she had taken a vow of stillness.

And, closest of all, sitting on a tool-chest dragged to the head of the bed with one leg crossed over the other, Violaine was watching him. Her white coat fell open at the hem and a black-stockinged calf disappeared into a pointed patent-leather boot. Her hair was the colour of bitter chocolate, a few strands fallen across her breast. In one hand she held a small silver bowl heaped with pink ice cream; in the other, a slim silver spoon.

She smiled, the way a cat smiles when the canary has done something unexpected.

"So. Awake." She lifted the spoon to his mouth. A perfect peak of strawberry ice cream trembled at its tip. "You were out for almost two hours. I had begun to think you'd sleep through till tomorrow."

He tried to say *I'm fine*. His throat was paper. Before the sound could form, the spoon was at his lip. The cold of the cream ran across his tongue, sweet and faintly bitter, a thread of vanilla threading through the strawberry, and slid down into the hollow of his stomach like a coin dropped into still water.

He blinked.

"Eat," she said, very softly. "You forgot?"

He blinked again. The memory came up through a kind of fog — a summer on Crete, the back of the Notting Hill primary school, the small ice-cream shop with the green awning. He had been ten. He had just come off his first successful simulated neural-link with a Seraphim and his face was the colour of old plaster. Violaine had carried him out of the Babel training cradle on her back. Halfway down the corridor he had said he wanted ice cream. She had said, *when you have won a real test, I will buy you one.*

That had been years.

"Today you won," Violaine said, and the spoon came again. "The King of the Undying system activated for the first time. Coupling at one hundred and twenty per cent. Twenty-eight points above von Lunderstedt's record. Franco says it was, from a neural-link standpoint, a textbook success."

A pause. The faintest edge of teasing in her voice.

"So eat. Don't tell me you have no appetite — you burned in two hours what a grown man would burn running three marathons."

He ate. Spoonful by spoonful, the warmth of his mouth wrapping itself around the cold of the sweet. Strawberry. He thought of Crete. He thought of the young novitiate who used to hide his pills in candy wrappers. He thought of Linoya's rough, warm palm.

He did not say thank you. His fingers, at the edge of the blanket, only closed a little tighter.

"You nearly lost yourself," Violaine said, when most of the bowl was gone. Her voice dropped so low it lived only in the space between the two of them. "The residual consciousness inside the system — the Sugaro fragment — it is old. Far older than anything we have ever linked. For a moment I thought it was going to overwrite you entirely. Franco's hands were shaking on the master console. I had my finger on the emergency cut-out."

She lifted her eyes to his. The amber of her irises was very dark in the red lamplight.

"But it withdrew. On its own. We did not cut it out. It left of its own will."

She said it again, as if saying it twice could make it less impossible.

"That means you have begun, in some measure, to command that force. The system will remember the parameters of your body. The next activation will be more stable."

He said nothing.

"Did you feel it?" she asked. "The force."

"Like a pair of eyes looking at me," he said. "Not hostile. Just… very far away. As if it stood on the other side of an ocean and watched."

Violaine was quiet for a few seconds. She set the silver bowl down on the small iron table by the bed, and reached out and laid the pad of her thumb against his forehead. Her finger was cool; her fingertip was rough with the calluses of a woman who had spent her life inside machines.

"Those eyes will not harm you again," she said. "Not in that way. It withdrew because you let it withdraw. Not because you begged. Not because you were afraid. Because you called out *mother*."

His breath stopped a half-beat.

"The Sugaro consciousness recognised you in that moment," she said. "It severed the link inside the system of its own accord. Franco says nothing like it has ever happened in the history of neural-linking. Not the system suppressing the consciousness. Not the consciousness suppressing the system. Something else. A kind of…"

She weighed the word in her mouth.

"…contract."

The cabin door opened.

Franco Borgia came in carrying an iron box. His face was paler than usual, two unhealthy spots of red on his cheekbones, the look of a man who had not yet finished being ill but had decided to work anyway. His uniform collar was open; a vein in his neck stood out blue as a river on a map. He dragged his right leg a little as he walked.

His eyes were very bright.

"Good, you're awake," he said, glancing at Cæsare, and went straight to the iron table in the middle of the cabin. "I have no time to wait for you to recover. Eat while we talk."

He opened the box. Inside, on a bed of fireproof cotton, lay five careful metal figures.

Cæsare levered himself up onto one elbow. Valentina slid a pillow behind his back. Quintilian carried his stool to the table without being asked. Alfonso stepped out of the shadows into the light. Don Juan unfolded to his feet, the little red flower still turning between his fingers. Belle did not come closer, but her gaze moved over the five figures as if she were memorising a name.

Franco lifted them out one by one.

The first was the largest, a dragon-shaped suit in burnt red. Twin flame-thrower nests nested in the shoulders like a sleeping pair of birds. Twin steam-core heat-fins rose from the back. The right arm ended in a reworked Thunder Fang; the right hand in a longsword that came to the waist — a Dragon Fang blade. He set it in the centre of the table.

"New Red Dragon," Franco said. "Pilot — Cæsare Borgia. Core output. Command. Undying-King mode."

The second was a deep-cyan phalanx shield, its face plated in a silvered metal, the closed lion's head cast into the upper-left corner, the edges riveted with beryllium-bronze corner-pieces. The torso was a wall. Left hand, the shield; right hand, a war-hammer.

"Augustus," Franco said. "Pilot — Quintilian. Function — frontal containment."

Quintilian answered with a low sound, the way a man does when he has been trusted with something he did not expect to be trusted with.

The third was a precision humanoid in silver-grey. Two folding long-barrel muskets on its back. A tool-kit and spare steam-lines at the waist. The joints were incised with fine gearwork. The right hand held a light repeating firelock with a sighting-scope. The left hand was empty — and the fingers were unnaturally long, each one engraved with a tiny screwdriver slot.

"Solomon," Franco said. "Pilot — Alfonso. Function — field maintenance and long-range fire. Flank support."

Alfonso nodded. His eyes moved down the model to the finger-joints, the way a man who works with his hands reads the teeth of a gear.

The fourth was a dark violet, smaller than the others, almost a teardrop in its lines, no ornament on it at all. The left hand held a curved sickle-blade thin as a new moon; the right, a short spear. Two rows of black throwing-knives were slung across its back.

"Rose-Ghost," Franco said. "Pilot — Don Juan. Function — assassin profile. Behind-enemy infiltration. Destruction of Prometheus observation mirrors, steam-piping, and popliteal balance-lines."

Don Juan whistled. "I like this one."

The fifth was mirror-black. No prominent weapon. No visible weakness. Shoulder, waist, knee, elbow — every joint had been polished like a piece of jewellery. It stood at the edge of the table like a body waiting for a soul.

"Blade-Dancer," Franco said. "Unmanned reserve suit. Remote-piloted by Tarasya. The moment a man falls in the front line, it takes his place."

Franco looked up from the five figures and met Cæsare's eyes.

"This is the first fully bespoke squad in the history of the Minerva Mechanism," he said. "Not mass-produced. Not pulled out of a warehouse to fill a roster. Every screw on every suit was made to the body, the neural latency, and the fighting habit of one man, and one man only."

He let a beat pass.

"So all five of you must come home. Not four. Five."

The cabin was quiet for a few seconds.

Franco reached into the bottom of the box and drew out a copper plate. The names and the assignments had been etched into it by a precision mechanical engraver, the lettering as even as print.

Cæsare took the plate. It caught the red light and gave back a dull, heavy glint. His eyes moved from *Cæsare Borgia — New Red Dragon — Core Output* to *Tarasya — Blade-Dancer — Unmanned Reserve* and stayed there a long time.

"Londer was right," he said, finally. His voice was low but the cabin heard every word. "I was not born for revenge."

Don Juan's fingers paused on his flower.

"I was born to protect you," Cæsare said. "Adele. Every man who chose to follow me."

He did not say *so I will not avenge*. That thing he kept, where he kept the things he would not let go of. But he had laid something heavier on top of it.

"So moved," Alfonso said, briefly.

"So moved," said Quintilian.

"So moved," said Don Juan, and then, after a beat — "But boss, if you *do* die—"

"Don Juan," said Violaine, without looking up, "one more word and I will have Tarasya drop you into the Blade-Dancer's shakedown programme."

Don Juan shut his mouth.

Franco looked at them. The smile he gave was a small, tired, strangely light thing — the smile of a man who has just realised, against his better judgement, that he is in good company. He took a folded handkerchief from the inside pocket of his white coat, pressed it to his mouth, and coughed. The cotton came away faintly marked with something dark. He closed his fist around it without a word and slid it back into his pocket.

"I am not finished," he said. "There is one more thing."

He turned to look at Valentina, still sitting quietly at the bedside.

"Your Highness. It is your turn."

Valentina rose.

She wore no court dress, and not the olive field-jacket of the Yenisei military. She wore a close dark-blue training-suit, a belt at the waist, and an empty sword-scabbard hung from the belt by a frog. Her golden hair was bound with a simple black ribbon into a high tail. The line of her jaw was very clear in the lamplight.

"Cæsare," she said, "I have an announcement to make."

Her voice was not loud. The cabin stilled anyway.

"Tomorrow I will be on the field as well."

The air thinned a degree.

"In a temporary conversion of a Divine Wrath III chassis," she said, looking straight at him. "Franco has already completed the preliminary adaptation of the neural-link system. Endurance is two minutes. Two minutes — long enough to walk you out of the killing-ground alive."

"Princess —" Cæsare began.

"This is your business," she said. "It is also mine."

She stepped to the bedside and drew the short sword from the empty scabbard at her hip. It was not the Dragon Fang. It was a small ceremonial blade in the Yenisei style, a kui-dragon worked into the grip.

"My mother, at the end, told me: when you meet the boy who can change the world, spend everything you have helping him. I promised her. I do not break my word."

She held the blade crosswise at her breast and gave him a Yenisei knight's salute — right hand to the heart, left knee a little bent.

"Tomorrow, on the field, I will spend my two minutes keeping you alive. This is my vow."

Cæsare was quiet for a few seconds.

"If your chassis fails mid-fight," he said, "I will carry you out on my shoulder."

The corner of Valentina's mouth moved.

"Good."

The copper-framed public-address on the cabin wall lit up.

The colour of the lamp was the colour of bone, and at once, in every corridor of the Minerva Mechanism, the same bone-white glare came on. A low buzz went through the iron floor, the kind of buzz that comes up from very far down.

"Attention, all Minerva Mechanism personnel," said the duty officer's flat, cold voice. "A command from the Cardinal-Conclave to relay — the Zhongshan Foreign Minister, Ye Suli, has requested entry to the outer perimeter of the Minerva Mechanism. He claims to carry a gift, to be delivered in person to the young lord Cæsare Borgia. The gate is requesting instructions. Do we admit him?"

The cabin stopped breathing.

Cæsare's hand, halfway to the bowl, stopped too.

*Ye Suli.*

The name was not new to him. The church in Marston, that rainy night. The man in the eastern silk robe stepping out of the shadow with a coin in his hand — not a coin of the Pope's realm, but the round-and-square coin of the East, with mountains and waters cast into the bronze. He had stood at the edge of the crowd. Most of his face was inside a hood. Only the eyes had been visible, and the eyes had been the eyes of a man who has made a calculation of how many of us will be dead by morning.

And the flag. Purple ground, white wolf's head. The wolf of Zhongshan. He remembered it drooping on a pillar in the corner of the church, soaked through with rain.

"Repeat, gate — do we admit him?"

Franco looked at Cæsare.

Cæsare was quiet for a few seconds. He reached up and drew the silver ring from his finger — the Glacier-Ice jade silver, the one with the name graven on the inside of the band — turned it once in the red light, and slid it back on. The name inside the band caught the lamp and became a thin dark line.

"Let him in," he said. "But have the weapons ready."

The cabin was looking at him.

"Ye Suli did not come to bring a gift," he said, and his voice was the voice of a man describing the weather. "He came to watch us die."

The bone-white light kept flickering on the wall. Franco coughed once, tucked the handkerchief back into the inside of his coat.

"I will see to it," he said. "All of you stay here."

The cabin door closed behind him. The rivets and the copper piping went on humming. The whole of the Minerva Mechanism, it seemed, was quietly, over and over, saying one name.

*Ye Suli.*
