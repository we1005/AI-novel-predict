【中文版】

第一百五十八章　锡封的祷文

傍晚的光从彩绘玻璃窗里筛下来，落在翡冷翠圣母堂的长椅上，像一层稀薄的金箔——愈往石板地那端去愈薄，到跟前便什么都不剩了。

西泽尔独自坐在最后一排，背对着门，一动不动。

前方那尊银质圣像一只手举在胸前作降福状，另一只手托着什么——隔得这样远，看不真切。圣像的左眼是一颗凸透镜，光线落到上面便折射出一粒针尖大的亮点，灼灼地钉在他脸上，随着日落缓缓游移。

他已经坐了半个小时。

口袋里那张纸条被体温焐得微微发软，纸角已经开始起毛。纸条是今晨送到的，没有封印，没有署名，没有火漆，只有两行铅笔字——

*"圣马可石桥下，第三根桥墩，午夜。"*

*"事关机械神教。不要带人。"*

字迹工整得像是印刷体，但西泽尔认得这种笔锋。他见过。

是伊格纳修斯的字。

那个在圣索菲亚大教堂被西泽尔亲手刺穿喉咙的男人，那个据说早已死透了的机械神教使徒。一个月前，西泽尔从那具尸体上取回了母亲的项链，链坠上"瑟茜"两个字的刻痕至今还印在他掌心。每当他攥紧拳头，那几个字母便压进肉里。

一个女巫的名字。一个母亲若想让女儿变得危险，会起的名字。

长椅尽头的门被推开，几名穿灰袍的书记官鱼贯而入，领头的那位把一本烫金封面的经书贴在胸前，像抱一个孩子。他们在西泽尔身后停下，似乎认出了他，低声交谈了几句，瞥了他一眼——便识趣地绕到另一侧去了。

"……那就是西泽尔少爷。"

"别看他。"

"听说枢机卿亲自过问过他的事……"

声音压得极低，但在空旷的教堂里，石壁传声像碗盛水。每一个字都清清楚楚地送到了他背后。

西泽尔没有回头。他的目光从圣像的银眼移开，落在自己交握的双手上。指节苍白，骨节分明，左手中指上有一道新结的痂——三天前伤口的棕色薄壳。那是在利维坦飞艇的检修舱里，被一根裸露的铜刺划破的。

他还记得那道伤口是怎么来的。

他跟着唐璜去检修那艘从君士坦丁堡缴获的飞艇，唐璜说是例行检查，可飞艇底层的蒸汽管道里藏着一样东西——一只锡封的匣子。匣口封得严丝合缝，不敲开便分不清盖与身的接缝。匣子的每一面都刻着机械神教的齿轮徽记，匣盖内侧用极细的针尖刻着一行祷文。

他只来得及看清前几个字——*"愿吾主之轮永转"*。

那行字，他在一本书里见过。

在薇若兰的藏书室里。

那本书没有封面，浅色的皮装帧已经磨得近乎毛毡，被塞在书架的缝隙里，紧挨着《弗洛伦萨花卉图鉴》和《托勒密天文手稿》之间，像是被刻意藏在那里的。西泽尔当时只是随手翻过，没有在意。可那行祷文——

*"愿吾主之轮永转。"*

——此刻像一根针，从记忆深处刺出来。

"西泽尔。"

一个声音从侧廊传来。

西泽尔抬起头。薇若兰靠在廊柱上，巧克力色的长腿在脚踝处交叠着，白色长发垂在旗袍的高领旁，衬得那张脸冷峻而锋利。她手里没有拿冰淇淋，而是捏着一根点燃的细烟。

"你在这里坐了快一个小时。"她说，"唐璜在到处找你。"

"我知道了。"

"昆提良说你的甲胄还没去例行校准。"

"明天。"

"阿方索说你今天没吃东西。"

"不饿。"

薇若兰吐出一口烟。烟雾飘进一束彩绘玻璃的光里，染上一层淡淡的锈红——像日头将落时被雾染上的那种颜色。她没再催，只是侧头看了他一眼。那种看法很特别——看一道难题时的目光，或者看一样自己不敢放下之物时的目光。

"你是不是查到了什么？"

西泽尔没有回答。

他站起身，长椅在他手底下轻轻吱呀了一声。口袋里那张纸条被他攥得更紧了一点。

"薇若兰。"

"嗯？"

"那本没有封面的书，"他说，"架子上插在《花卉图鉴》和《天文手稿》之间的那本。你是从哪里得来的？"

彩绘玻璃外的天色已经暗了大半，教堂里的光斑正一寸一寸地从金色转为铜红。

薇若兰夹着烟的手指顿了一顿。

"什么书？"

"浅色皮装的那本。塞在缝里的。"

她没有立刻作答。烟灰在烟卷末端结成一截，颤颤巍巍地悬着，迟迟不肯坠落。

"……我父亲的遗物。"她说，"你翻过？"

"只看了几页。"

"那几页上写了什么？"

西泽尔没有说。

他看着薇若兰的眼睛。这个时辰，这种光里，那双眼睛颜色极深——两口看不见底的井。井水里藏着很多东西，有些他看得见，有些他从未被允许靠近。

此刻他在那双眼睛里瞥见了一点东西——一小簇警觉的火星——然后她便把它收了回去。

"西泽尔，"薇若兰的声音放轻了半度，"你最近见过什么人？"

他想起口袋里那张纸条。

*"不要带人。"*

"没有。"

他撒了谎。

——

圣马可石桥横跨阿尔诺河，三根桥墩立在暮色里，桥墩上爬满了枯藤和青苔，吃得肥厚。

西泽尔到的时候，桥下没有人在等。

河水不紧不慢地拍打着桥墩，溅起细碎的水声。河面上漂浮着几盏未燃尽的河灯，是白日节庆留下的残骸，纸糊的灯壁被水泡得半透明，露出里面早已熄灭的烛芯。

他把甲胄的护臂扣紧，蒸汽阀压到了最低功率，只留一双夜视的青蓝色镜片在暗中亮着，把世界冲到他眼前化作一片青灰：石头的每一条棱都锋利而过实，每一道影子都顶着自己一圈小小的蓝色光晕。

*如果这是个陷阱，他至少要看见杀他的人是谁。*

桥墩的阴影里，有什么东西动了一下。

不是人。

是机器。

一具矮小的机械体，从桥墩背后缓缓转出来，像一只甲虫从叶子的边缘绕出来。它的躯干是一只铜质的八面体，每一面都密密麻麻地刻着齿轮状文字的纹样，顶部伸出一根细长的天线，天线末端亮着一星稳定的红光，像一只独眼。

机械神教的下位使徒，"叩门者"。

西泽尔后退半步，右手已经按上了腰间的短铳。

"叩门者"没有攻击。它在距离他五步远的地方停下，八面体开始绕自己的轴缓缓旋转——一面又一面刻着齿轮字纹的面转过去，最终停在了刻有那行祷文的那一面。

然后，它开始说话。

声音不是从机体里发出的。它从水里来。水面下藏着一只扬声器，把某个人的声音传导上来，闷闷的，微微失真，辅音被磨得软了，像说话人嘴里含了半嘴石子。

"西泽尔·波旁。"

"有人告诉我，你母亲在翡冷翠过得并不顺。"

西泽尔的手指扣住了扳机。

"那枚链坠，"那声音说，"背面的刻字——并不是你以为的那样。"

一盏河灯从两座桥墩之间漂过去，里头早已灭掉的烛芯忽然重新亮起。不是火焰。是淡蓝色的磷光——深海里游动的水母的那种颜色，是西泽尔镜片的那种颜色。灯亮着，它冷冽的光在河面上徐徐游移，像一团迟缓的蓝色火焰。

"你可曾凑近看过？"

"叩门者"的天线开始缓缓向水面垂落。顶端那点红光在阿尔诺河的水面上拖出一条长长的光痕。

"时辰未到，西泽尔少爷。"那声音说，"可你来了。"

"你一个人来了。"

西泽尔没有动。

河水拍打桥墩的声音一拍一拍地落进他耳朵，像黑暗中某处有人在慢慢计数。

"你想告诉我什么？"他问。

"我想告诉你的是，"那声音说，"那张纸条的书写者，今晚，原本不是要见你。"

"他要见的那个人——"

那声音停了一停，似乎只为享受这停顿。

"——一直就在你身边，已经有些日子了。"

"叩门者"的天线垂下，红光熄灭。八面体在原地又转了最后半圈——慢，郑重——停在了另一面。

西泽尔的瞳孔骤缩。

它停下的那一面，刻的并不是机械神教的祷文。

是翡冷翠枢机院的密文。

他认得那种密文。

他在阿方索的私人文件匣里，见过同样的字符——同样的细密工整的小字，印在一只没有标名、也没有封蜡的文件夹上。那是一个人留在抽屉里、只要旁人在场便绝不去打开的那种文件夹。

"叩门者"一动不动。

蓝色的灯顺水漂着。

而在不远处的黑暗里，另一双眼睛也睁着。

【English】

Chapter 158 — The Tin-Sealed Prayer

The evening light came sieved through the stained glass of Santa Maria del Fiore and lay across the empty pews like a leaf of gilt that thinned to nothing before it reached the flagstones. Cesare Borgia sat alone in the last row, his back to the door, and did not move.

A silver saint stood at the altar, one hand raised in benediction, the other cradling something at his chest Cesare could not, from this distance, make out. The left eye of the saint was a convex lens, a vanity of some earlier century, and where the light struck it the eye threw a single bright point no larger than a needle's head, a moving star that wandered over Cesare's face as the sun went down.

He had been sitting half an hour, or close enough.

The note in his pocket had gone soft with the warmth of his thigh, and the corners of the paper had begun to fray. It had been delivered that morning. No seal, no signature, no wax. Just two lines in pencil, regular as print.

*Beneath the Ponte San Marco. Third pier. Midnight.*

*It concerns the Mechanist Church. Come alone.*

He knew the hand.

He had seen it before — on letters, on ledgers, on the inside of a Mechanist catechism he had once read in a room he was not supposed to be in. The penmanship of a man who had been taught by monks.

It was the hand of Ignatius.

The apostle of the Mechanist Church whom Cesare himself had put a knife through, in the galleries of Hagia Sophia, a year ago — or what was reported to be a year ago. The man was supposed to be dead. Cesare had felt the blade go in, had felt the soft wrongness of windpipe giving way, and had walked out into the late afternoon with the blood still wet on his cuff. A month later, retrieving the body for the Cardinalate's enquiry, he had cut his mother's necklace from the apostle's neck and carried the medallion home. The engraving on the back of the pendant still pressed its letters into the meat of his palm whenever he clenched his fist.

C-I-R-C-E.

That was the name he had always been told was cut there. *Circe.* A witch's name. The kind of name a woman gives a daughter she means to make dangerous.

And yet that dead man's hand had, this morning, written to him in pencil.

The door at the end of the nave opened. Three grey-robed clerks came in, the lead one carrying a gilt-edged breviary against his chest like a child. They stopped when they saw the figure in the last pew. They spoke low, they glanced at him, and then — choosing wisdom — they moved off to a side chapel and let him be.

"— that is the young Borgia."

"Don't look at him."

"They say the Cardinal himself has taken an interest in his case—"

Their voices were barely a murmur, but the cathedral was very empty, and stone carries sound the way a bowl carries water. Every word arrived at his back like a tap on the shoulder.

Cesare did not turn.

He let his gaze drop from the silver eye of the saint down to his own folded hands. The knuckles were pale, the joints pronounced, and on the middle finger of his left hand there was a fresh scab, the brown crust of a wound three days old. He had cut himself on a strip of bare copper, in the maintenance bay of a captured Leviathan. He remembered the exact moment the metal took him — the small bright flare of pain, the oath he had not sworn, Don Juan's hand on his shoulder saying *Steady, boy, steady.*

He remembered the rest of that day more clearly than he wanted to.

Don Juan had taken him down into the Leviathan's bilge under pretext of a routine inspection. The airship had been taken off the Turks at Constantinople three weeks before, and most of her had been gutted for parts already, her steam-rails cannibalised for the Seraph cores that powered the riding suits of the Order. But in the lowest of the trunks, behind a false wall of riveted plate, Don Juan's men had come upon a small box sealed with tin — tinned over the join so thoroughly that, until you broke it, you could not tell where the lid stopped and the body began. The box was stamped, on every available surface, with the cog-and-spoke of the Mechanist Church. And on the inside of the lid, scratched with a fine needle-point, there was a line of prayer.

He had only had time to read the first words before Don Juan took the box from him.

*May the wheel of our Lord turn forever—*

He had seen that line somewhere else.

In Violante's library.

The book had no cover. Its binding, in pale leather worn down almost to the colour of bone, had been wedged into a gap on a shelf between a florilegium of Tuscan flowers and a Ptolemaic astronomical treatise, as though it had been deliberately shelved there to be passed over. Cesare had pulled it out that afternoon only because its spine was loose and he had reached past it for a different volume. He had read three pages, no more. The line was there. He was certain of it now.

*May the wheel of our Lord turn forever.*

The memory of it came at him as a needle finds the place it has been waiting for.

"Cesare."

The voice came from the side aisle.

He looked up. Violante was leaning against a column with her long brown legs crossed at the ankle, a thin cigarette between two fingers, the white fall of her hair against the high collar of her gown making her face look sharper than it was. No ice. A cigarette, then — a thing she kept for the end of a day, not the middle of one. She had been on her way somewhere when she had stepped inside.

"You've been here almost an hour," she said. "Don Juan is hunting for you."

"I know."

"Quintilian says your cuirass has not been re-calibrated since the campaign."

"Tomorrow."

"Alfonso says you have not eaten."

"I was not hungry."

Violante exhaled. The smoke drifted into a bar of the stained-glass light and took on a faint rust colour, the way mist takes a colour when the sun has nearly gone. She did not press. She looked at him instead, and the look had a particular quality he could not quite place — the look one gives a puzzle, or a thing one is afraid to set down.

"Did you find something out?"

He did not answer.

He stood. The pew gave a small groan under his hand. In his pocket, the note was crushed tighter between his fingers.

"Violante."

"Mm."

"That book without a cover," he said. "The one on the shelf between the florilegium and the astronomical treatise. Where did you come by it?"

The light shifted. Outside, the sun had dropped behind the campanile, and the colour on the floor was no longer gilt but copper, the colour of old blood on linen, the colour of the inside of a sealed tin.

Violante's cigarette paused halfway to her mouth.

"What book?"

"The one in pale leather. Wedged in."

She did not answer at once. A length of ash had formed at the tip of her cigarette, and it balanced there, trembling, refusing to fall.

"It was my father's," she said, after a moment. "Among his effects. Did you open it?"

"A few pages."

"What was on them?"

Cesare said nothing.

He was watching her eyes. In this light, at this hour, they were very dark — the colour of two wells with no visible bottom. There was a great deal living in those wells. Some of it he had seen. Some of it he had never been permitted to approach.

What he saw now, briefly, before she folded it away, was a small flare of wariness.

"Cesare," she said, and her voice had dropped half a degree. "Have you been meeting with anyone of late?"

He thought of the note in his pocket.

*Come alone.*

"No," he said.

He lied.

---

The Ponte San Marco crossed the Arno on three piers, each of them fat with centuries of moss and dead vine. Cesare, when he arrived a little before the appointed hour, found no one waiting.

The river pushed at the piers with a patient, unhurried sound. A few of the paper lamps from the day's festival still drifted on the water — sodden now, their paper walls gone translucent, the wicks inside them long since drowned. The smell of the river was the smell of wet stone and slow decay, the smell a city wears at the edges of itself.

He tightened the vambrace on his left arm, and turned the steam-valve on his cuirass down to its lowest setting. The night-vision lenses in the helm — the pair of pale azure discs that would let him see in the dark — clicked softly into life and threw the world at him in a wash of blue-grey, every edge of stone sharp and over-defined, every shadow carrying its own little blue nimbus.

*If this is a trap, I will at least see the face of the man who kills me.*

Something moved in the shadow behind the third pier.

Not a man.

A machine.

It came round the pier in a slow, deliberate way, as a beetle comes round the edge of a leaf. Its body was a small copper octahedron, each of its eight faces etched with a dense pattern of gear-tooth script, and from its top rose a single thin antenna ending in a steady red point, like a solitary eye. Cesare had seen these before, in the records of the Cardinalate of Florence — low-ranked servants of the Mechanist Church, used for the delivery of messages and the witnessing of things. The clerks had a name for them. *The Knockers.* He had never seen one in the field.

He stepped back half a pace. His right hand found the butt of the short pistol at his hip.

The Knocker did not attack. It stopped at five paces, and the octahedron began to turn on its own axis — slowly, with the unhurried motion of a clock-hand — face after gear-scripted face passing, and finally settled on the one that bore the prayer.

Then it began to speak.

The voice did not come from the machine.

It came from the water. There was a speaker, hidden somewhere beneath the surface of the river, and the voice was being carried up through it, muffled by current, faintly distorted, the consonants softened as though the speaker were talking with his mouth half-full of stones.

"Cesare Borgia."

"I have been told your mother has not done well in Florence."

Cesare's finger found the trigger.

"The medallion on that chain," the voice said. "The engraving on the back. It does not say what you think it says."

One of the paper lamps drifted past, between the piers. The wick inside it — long since out — suddenly relit. Not with flame. With a faint blue phosphorescence, the colour of deep-water jellyfish, the colour of the lenses in Cesare's helm. The lamp glowed, and its cold light moved on the surface of the river as a slow blue flame.

"Did you look closely?" the voice said.

The antenna of the Knocker began, slowly, to swing down toward the water. The red point of its eye trailed a long smudge of reflected light across the surface of the Arno.

"Midnight has not yet come, young Borgia," it said. "And yet you came."

"You came alone."

Cesare did not move.

The sound of the river against the piers was coming into his ears one beat at a time, like a slow count being kept somewhere in the dark.

"What is it you want to tell me?" he said.

"I want to tell you," the voice said, "that the man who wrote you that note was not, tonight, intending to meet you at all."

"The man he intended to meet—"

The voice paused, as though for the pleasure of the pause.

"—has been at your side for some time."

The antenna of the Knocker dropped. The red point at its tip went out.

The octahedron turned once more — a half turn, slow, deliberate — and settled on a different face.

Cesare's pupils contracted.

The face it had settled on was not engraved with Mechanist prayer.

It was engraved with the cipher of the Cardinalate of Florence.

He knew the cipher. He had seen the same characters, in the same careful miniaturist hand, on a folder in the private document-box of Alfonso Borgia — a folder marked with no name and sealed with no wax, the kind of folder a man keeps in a drawer he is careful never to open when anyone else is in the room.

The river pushed at the piers.

The blue lamp drifted on.

The Knocker did not move.

And somewhere in the dark, very close, a second pair of eyes was open.
