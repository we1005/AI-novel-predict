第157章 红水银

礼车在夜色中驶离翡冷翠，沿着台伯河北岸的旧驿道一路向北。

瓦莲京娜把车开得又快又稳，仿佛她不是刚刚从一座围城里捞人出来，而是去参加一场夜间的舞会。风把她的金色长发吹得飞舞，粉色的发带随之飘荡，发间那股清冷的忍冬香气被夜风卷到后座，昆提良坐在那里吸了吸鼻子，觉得公主的头发是世界上最好的东西，比他磨过的所有剑刃都好闻。

艾莲已经睡着了，蜷缩在他怀里，小手揪着他的衣角。昆提良怕惊醒她，连呼吸都放得极轻。

西泽尔靠在副驾驶位上，看着窗外倒退的黑色山影。他的脸色比夜色还白，颧骨的阴影被月光刻得锋利。

从翡冷翠的灯火里走出来，外面的世界反而更黑。没有城市的光污染，银河在头顶铺开，像一条被打翻的牛奶河。他已经很久没有看过这样的星空了——密涅瓦的训练塔里没有星星，地牢里更没有。

然后他看见了那道光。

台伯河的方向，一道红色的光柱笔直地射上夜空，比星光更亮，也更冷。它停了几秒钟，又缓慢地旋转起来，像一只在黑暗中睁开的巨眼。

红水银信号灯。

密涅瓦机关的紧急召唤信号。

它亮得那么刺眼，仿佛一只红色的眼睛穿过几十公里的夜色，盯着他看。

西泽尔的心跳漏了一拍。那种光他太熟悉了——他在密涅瓦受训的三年里，每一道红水银信号亮起，就意味着一场真正的战争即将开始。

"红水银。"他低声说。

瓦莲京娜没有回头，只是把车速又提了一档。

"看见了，"她说，"要打仗了。"

三个字落进夜里，谁也没再接话。

密涅瓦机关的侧门藏在台伯河北岸一座废弃水塔的背后，铁门上爬满了常春藤，铁皮锈成了深褐色。铁门下方藏着一道不易察觉的钢轨，是为重型设备进出预留的检修口——如果不是知情者，几乎不会有人把它当成入口。

瓦莲京娜把车停在离水塔三百米外的松林里，摇下窗户，朝西泽尔点了点头。

"我送你们到这儿，"她说，"再往前，是你们的事了。"

西泽尔看了她一眼。月光下，这个来自叶尼塞的公主笑得明亮，像是一个送完朋友回家、没什么大不了的人。但他知道她在赌——她把大使馆的车开进翡冷翠，已经是把罗曼诺夫家族和教廷的梁子结下了。

"瓦莲京娜，"他说，"多谢。"

"别谢我，"瓦莲京娜把粉色的发带在指尖绕了一圈，"等你们成了英雄，再来谢我。"

西泽尔没有笑。他推开车门，跳了下去。

阿方索和唐璜从后座翻出来，昆提良抱着艾莲，动作放得极轻，绕过车头，站到西泽尔身后。

"老师，"艾莲在半梦半醒间呢喃了一声，把脸埋进昆提良的胸口。

西泽尔回头看了她一眼，眼神里有什么东西软了一下，又迅速硬了起来。

"走。"

侧门旁边有一个不显眼的检修口，半截铁梯向下延伸进黑暗中，底下传来机器低沉的轰鸣声和蒸汽管道咝咝的呼吸。

阿方索走在最前面，他的白手套上还有没擦干净的硝烟。唐璜跟在西泽尔身侧，黑色风衣的下摆在井口的风里猎猎作响。昆提良抱着艾莲走在最后，每一步都走得很慢，怕颠醒了孩子。

铁梯向下延伸了大约三十米，空气渐渐变冷，金属的腥味开始浓起来。

到了最底下，是一条狭长的混凝土通道，通道尽头是一扇厚重的铁门。铁门上方有一盏昏暗的钨丝灯，灯光下站着一个人。

佛朗哥。

佛朗哥是密涅瓦机关的档案官，五十来岁，头发花白，永远穿一件深灰色的旧军装，军装上绣着密涅瓦的徽章——一只举着长矛的猫头鹰。他看见西泽尔的第一眼，没有惊讶，也没有寒暄。

他只是拍了拍胸口，从那件旧军装的内袋里抽出五份牛皮纸档案袋，用力拍在西泽尔的胸口。

"啪"的一声，沉闷而结实。

"西泽尔·布尔加科夫，"佛朗哥的声音冷硬得像在念一份判决书，"从这一刻起，你恢复密涅瓦机关正式骑士军籍。这是你们的档案，全部密封，密级最高。档案里是你们这一百二十七天里所有被除名、被追捕、被通缉的记录——现在它们作废了。"

他又从另一个口袋里摸出一张折好的纸，递给西泽尔。

"这是战争动员令。教皇国和帕提亚的边境已经打起来了，红水银信号你也看见了。今晚十二点，所有在册骑士必须在地下装甲厅完成着装，三点出发。"

佛朗哥顿了顿，又看了西泽尔一眼，眼神里有某种说不清的复杂情绪。

"别让我失望，小子。"

西泽尔把五份档案袋抱在怀里，没有说话。他向佛朗哥行了一个标准骑士礼，然后推开身后那扇厚重的铁门。

铁门之后，是一条更长的金属通道，通道尽头是另一扇更大的铁门，铁门后透出昏黄而浑浊的光。

铁门开了。

光从门后涌出来，连同那沉闷的、机械的呼吸声。

地下装甲厅。

装甲厅大得不像话，像一个被掏空的地下教堂。穹顶极高，吊着数十盏古老的铁艺吊灯，灯光是昏黄的，落在地上和那些冰冷的金属上，把一切都染成一种近乎凝血的颜色。

厅中央有五座巨大的金属支架——海格力斯之架。支架是能承受十吨以上装甲重量的液压固定系统，关节处渗出青蓝色的蒸汽，发出咝咝的低鸣，像五头正在熟睡的巨兽的呼吸。

五具新型炽天使卧在支架旁。

它们通体漆黑，肩甲上印着密涅瓦的金色徽记，眼睛部分是两片未点亮的青蓝色宝石，在昏黄的灯光下泛着冷冷的幽光。每一具都比西泽尔记忆中的老型号更高大、更厚实，蒸汽管道从背后的散热孔中蜿蜒而出，像一束束盘在钢铁上的金属藤蔓。

西泽尔停下了脚步。

"这是……"阿方索的声音有点哑。

"新机。"唐璜说，"咱们的新机。"

他们走到了各自那一具炽天使面前。

阿方索站在他的所罗门王面前。所罗门王是他们当中最特殊的一具——它没有传统意义上的胸甲和肩甲，整个左臂是一根由无数机械关节组成的巨型机械臂，机械臂的末端不是手掌，而是一具多管蒸汽火控系统，十二根粗大的枪管排成扇形，枪口在灯光下闪着黑光。阿方索伸手摸了摸那根机械臂，火控系统的蒸汽阀立刻被感应，咝的一声喷出一道白雾。他闭上眼睛，用手指轻叩金属表面，像在和一位老朋友说话。

"十二管都通了，"他低声说，"主蒸汽压力比旧机提高了一倍。"

昆提良把他的奥古斯都拍了一下。奥古斯都是一具异常高大的炽天使，比其他四具高出近一个头，肩甲两侧各挂着一柄战斧，斧刃是黑铁与精钢的合金，在灯光下泛着冷厉的银白色。昆提良伸手握住其中一柄战斧的斧柄，金属的重量透过手套传到他的掌心，他的眼睛立刻亮了。

"好家伙，"他低声说，"够沉。"

他没有用力，只是轻轻抚摸了一下斧刃的边缘，又放下了。怕吵醒怀里的艾莲。

唐璜站在他自己的那具炽天使前，没有急着检查武器。他从黑色风衣的内袋里摸出一朵白玫瑰，小心地、缓缓地把玫瑰插入炽天使的胸甲缝隙里。白玫瑰的花瓣在昏黄的灯光下显得苍白而鲜嫩，像是在这钢铁巨兽的胸口开出了一朵真正的花。

"一路顺风。"他轻声说。

他不知道这朵花是送给谁的——也许是那个叫碧儿的姑娘，也许是东方公主，也许是这具即将上战场的钢铁之躯。但他把花插得很稳，仿佛这具炽天使是他的马车，而玫瑰是车徽。

西泽尔走到了最里面那具炽天使前。

红龙。

他的红龙。

和其他四具相比，红龙的颜色更深，几乎是一种近乎凝固的暗红，像是浸透了无数人的血。肩甲上没有任何徽记，胸甲的中央有一道深深的金色划痕，那是他第一次实战时，被一名帕提亚骑士的星陨锤擦过留下的。

他伸手触碰到红龙的胸甲，金属传来一阵冰凉，然后是一种熟悉的微震。

红龙认出了他。

青蓝色的宝石眼睛缓缓亮起，喷出两道淡蓝色的蒸汽。

"红龙。"西泽尔低声说。

就在他抬步，要走向海格力斯之架的那一刻——

"砰！"

装甲厅的铁门被人猛地从外撞开。

金属的巨响在地下教堂般的大厅里反复回荡，五具炽天使的眼睛同时被这一声震动激活，青蓝色的光在装甲厅里四处乱跳。一道刺眼的手电光从门缝中劈了进来，照在西泽尔的脸上。

"西泽尔·布尔加科夫，"一个冷冷的声音从门外传来，"把手从炽天使上拿开。"

佛朗哥的声音。

但他身后还跟着另一道更冷、更沉、带着某种不容置疑的权威的声音。

"密涅瓦机关最高战时条例第七款，"那个声音说，"恢复军籍之骑士，需经枢机院重新审议，方可合法持甲。"

西泽尔的手停在了半空。

阿方索的手立刻从所罗门王上缩了回来。昆提良抱着艾莲，下意识地把女孩藏到了身后。唐璜的右手已经按在了腰间那把从不离身的细长刺剑上。

装甲厅里的空气冷了下来。

那盏门后手电光里，隐约可见一个戴着银色面具的身影。

Chapter 157 — The Mercury Signal

The engine of the carriage didn't so much idle down as it died, a sudden cessation of noise that made the ringing in their ears seem louder than the roar had been. The air inside the vehicle was thick, a mixture of ozone from the battery packs, the damp wool of their coats, and the faint, sweet scent of honeysuckle clinging to Valentina’s hair. She cut the ignition. The silence that followed was heavy, pressing against the windows where the city lights of Florence blurred into streaks of gold and grey.

For a long moment, no one moved. The princess looked back at them, her expression settling from that manic playfulness into something harder, colder. She wiped a speck of dust from her cheek, though there was nothing there.

We’re here.

She pushed the driver’s door open. The night air rushed in, cool and smelling of wet stone and river mud. Cezar stepped out first, his boots crunching on gravel. His legs felt heavy, weighed down by the adrenaline crash. Behind him, the rest of the unit filed out, forming a loose perimeter. Even now, surrounded by the neutral ground of a diplomatic mission, every man checked the blind spots.

To their left, across the wide expanse of the Tiber, the skyline was punctuated by the steady pulse of a lighthouse. It wasn’t a beam of white light, but a deep, arterial red. Mercury vapor lamps. The signal was flashing from the eastern bank. One second on, three seconds off. A heartbeat for the whole city to see.

They were being watched.

Valentina nodded once, her gaze lingering on Cezar. You understand what you are walking into.

I understand.

He turned his back on her and led the group toward the service entrance of the facility. It was a low, squat structure built of reinforced concrete and rusted iron, tucked behind the high walls of the embassy district. There were no guards here, only the hum of ventilation fans and the shadow of a catwalk running along the roofline.

The gate slid open with a groan of grinding gears, revealing a dark tunnel illuminated by emergency strips of dim green light. Cezar walked in, followed by Alfonso, Quintilian, and Don Juan. The air inside changed instantly—recycled, dry, and metallic. The smell of gunpowder that had coated them on the streets was scrubbed away, replaced by the sterile tang of coolant and grease.

They stopped in front of a heavy iron door marked with the sigil of the Minerva Institute. A keypad blinked beneath a frosted glass panel.

Before anyone could touch it, a figure stepped out from the shadows of the corridor. He wore a grey uniform with a high collar, his face pale and sharp, devoid of warmth. Franco. An officer of the bureau, someone who dealt in logistics rather than lead. He held a stack of thick folders bound in leather.

Without a word, Franco strode forward and thrust the bundle against Cezar’s chest. The impact knocked the wind out of him. He caught the weight with both hands before it fell.

The papers were hot.

These are your records, Franco said. His voice was flat, like a mechanic reading a parts list. Restoration of rank. Full clearance. All debts paid by the Crown. Signatures forged by the Chancellor himself.

Cezar didn’t look at the folders immediately. He looked at Franco.

Why?

Franco didn’t answer. He glanced down the corridor, toward the inner sanctum where the lights were brighter. Because the Chancellor knows you’re going to die tomorrow. And if you do, I want my paperwork clean.

He turned and walked away, leaving them standing in the hum of the corridor.

Cezar opened the top folder. The paper smelled of ink and official wax. Inside, the photograph was grainy, taken from a surveillance drone. It showed him in the street, the smoke rising around him. Beneath it, a stamp read REINSTATED. The date was current. The time was ten minutes ago.

It meant they had already fought the bureaucracy before they even entered the building.

Come on, Cezar said. His voice sounded strange to his own ears, too loud in the quiet space.

They moved deeper into the facility. The floor plates were steel grating, vibrating slightly under the weight of machinery below. As they passed through the archway, the room opened up into a vast hangar. The ceiling was lost in darkness, supported by ribs of blackened iron girders. In the center of the space stood the Hercules Rig—a massive stationary frame used for testing powered armor. It was shaped like a gorilla, limbs articulated with pistons, painted in matte black.

Surrounding the Hercules Rig were five other frames. They were sleeker, more elegant, designed for speed rather than brute strength. Seraphim Class. Their wings were folded tight against their backs, gleaming silver against the gloom.

This is where we sleep tonight, Quintilian said. He ran a hand along the rail of a nearby crate, smiling as if it were a lover. Tonight.

He stopped at the rack reserved for him. A battle axe lay on the table. It was heavy, the haft wrapped in worn leather, the blade etched with runes that looked more like scratches from a diamond cutter than any art form. He lifted it. The momentum pulled at his shoulder. He swung it in a short arc, feeling the balance point settle perfectly into the bones of his wrist.

I like this one, he said. It remembers how to kill.

Across the room, Alfonso was already seated on a bench, stripping the gauntlet from his left forearm. Underneath the leather glove, the mechanical interface glowed blue. He picked up the attachment that waited for him: a Solomonic Mechanism arm. It was complex, a web of copper wire and hydraulic fluid encased in ceramic plating.

When he attached it, the servos whined softly, adjusting to his muscle memory. He flexed his fingers. The metal responded instantly, tracking his movement with precision. He raised his arm, aiming it at a dummy target fifty meters away. A laser sight flickered green. He squeezed his thumb trigger.

Click. The mechanism locked. Ready for fire.

Don Juan stood apart from them, near the exit of the hangar. He was still wearing the dusty coat from the carriage, the fabric stained with road grime. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a single object. A white rose. It had been pressed between pages, dried and fragile, its petals brown at the edges.

He placed it gently on the dashboard of a nearby maintenance cart.

What is that? Cezar asked, approaching him.

A promise.

To whom?

To the ones we leave behind. If I come back, I’ll plant it in the garden. If I don’t, it will stay here.

Cezar studied the flower. It seemed absurdly delicate amidst the roaring machinery and the smell of oil.

Finish the preparations, Don Juan said. He turned and leaned against the wall, closing his eyes.

That left Cezar alone in the center of the room. The silence here was different from the one in the carriage. It was charged, static electricity humming in the air. He walked toward the far corner where the final set of armor waited.

The Red Dragon.

It was not painted red. The paint was scorched and faded, a dull orange-brown from heat and age. The wings were spread wide, resting on supports. This was the prototype, the one designed for the front lines of the Eastern War. It was larger than the others, bulkier, built to absorb damage that would shatter lesser units.

Cezar approached the helmet. He touched the visor. It was cold to the touch, a sheet of polished obsidian glass.

He sat on the edge of the platform. The metal creaked under his weight. He closed his eyes and let the memories flood back. The fire in the city. The smell of blood. The sound of the cavalry charging. He had run from this place for years, hiding in the shadows, playing games of politics and survival. He told himself he wanted peace. He told himself he wanted to live quietly.

But when the call came, when the world burned, he knew exactly what to do.

He stood up. He began to remove his civilian clothes. The coat, the shirt, the trousers. They fell to the floor in a pile. He stood in his undershirt, his skin pale and scarred. He stepped into the boots of the suit first. They were heavy, lined with thermal gel.

He slid the leg plates on. The magnetic locks engaged with a sharp clack. Then the torso. The chest piece was a shell of black alloy, etched with the crest of the Minerva Institute. He buckled the straps, pulling them tight until the material bit into his skin.

Every movement was deliberate. There was no panic left in him now. Only a cold, crystalline clarity. He was a soldier again. Not a politician, not a fugitive. A weapon pointed at a target.

He reached for the helmet. Before he placed it on his head, he paused.

From the hallway behind him, a sound echoed. Heavy boots on steel grating.

Cezar turned slowly. The Red Dragon loomed over him, a silhouette in the dim light. The sound grew louder, rhythmic. Three steps. A pause. Two steps.

Whoever it was, they weren’t trying to be quiet. They were making noise on purpose.

Cezar gripped the handle of the helmet in his right hand. His left hand went to the pistol holstered on his thigh.

The hallway remained empty for a moment. Then, the air pressure shifted. The ventilation fans seemed to stall. The lights flickered, dimming for a fraction of a second before surging back to full brightness.

Someone was coming.

And they were bringing trouble.

Cezar didn’t move. He didn’t shout. He simply waited, his breath shallow, his muscles coiled.

The sound of the boots stopped right at the threshold of the hangar.

Then, a massive impact shook the foundations of the building. The doors to the armored hall were slammed open from the outside, not by a key card or a code, but by force.

Iron bars bent outward. Sparks flew as the locking mechanisms sheared. The heavy steel doors crashed inward, slamming against the interior walls with a thunderous boom that rattled the teeth in Cezar’s skull.

Standing in the breach, silhouetted against the harsh light of the corridor beyond, was a figure clad in tactical gear. But it wasn’t a human shape. The figure was bulky, draped in layers of canvas and chainmail, holding a rifle that hummed with unstable energy.

Behind the figure, in the darkness of the corridor, dozens of small red lights blinked into existence. Rifles. Sniper scopes. Grenades.

They hadn’t barged in to talk.

The figure raised the rifle. The barrel glowed orange.

Cezar didn’t think. He didn’t finish suiting up. He dropped the helmet onto the floor and lunged for the support pillar beside the Red Dragon.

The shot rang out, a crack of thunder that shattered the glass of the nearby control console. Shards of debris rained down like snow.

The enemy didn’t miss. They fired again, sweeping the entire room in a controlled burst.

Cezar rolled behind the pillar, the bullets chipping away the concrete at his feet. Dust billowed up, choking the air.

Through the haze, he saw the figure step fully into the hangar. Another figure appeared behind him. Then another. They weren’t Minerva security. They were professional killers. The kind that wore no insignia and took no prisoners.

The leader spoke, his voice amplified by the comms system in his mask.

You have three seconds to surrender, Cezar.

Cezar peeked around the edge of the pillar. His eyes adjusted to the gloom. He saw Alfonso raising his mechanical arm, lining up a shot. He saw Quintilian gripping the axe, ready to sprint. He saw Don Juan reaching for the rose on the dashboard, a useless gesture against the violence erupting around them.

Three seconds.

Cezar looked at the Red Dragon. It sat dormant, waiting for life.

He looked back at the doorway. The muzzle flashes were getting closer.

If they wanted him, they would have to take him from the ashes.

He reached out and grabbed the helmet. He shoved it onto his head. The world went dark for a split second before the heads-up display lit up, painting the room in ghostly greens and greys.

The HUD scanned the room. It highlighted the threats in bright red. Six targets. Two snipers in the upper gallery. Four advancing in the doorway.

Cezar drew the pistol. He didn’t aim at the figures. He aimed at the ceiling. Above him, a large electrical conduit box hung suspended by chains, dripping with sparks.

The gun roared. The bolt struck the box.

Fire erupted. The cables snapped. The conduit fell, crashing down onto the group in the doorway. Metal screeched against metal. Smoke poured out, thick and acrid.

Cezar stepped out from cover. He didn’t wait for the Red Dragon to power up. He didn’t wait for backup.

He ran toward the breach.

The gunfire intensified, turning the air into a wall of lead. He ducked, weaving through the chaos. The helmet’s sensors screamed warnings about overheating systems. He ignored them.

In the distance, the red mercury signal on the Tiber flashed again. On. Off.

Like a heartbeat.

The door to the hangar slammed shut again, sealing them in with the enemy.

Cezar stopped. He turned around.

The door was gone. The hinges had been ripped off. Now, a solid wall of steel blocked the exit.

And from the other side of the wall, he heard a sound that froze the blood in his veins.

It was the sound of a drill. Cutting through the metal.

They weren’t trying to kill him yet. They were trying to trap him.

Cezar looked at the Red Dragon. It was finally powering up. The engines spooled with a deep growl that vibrated through the floor plates. The wings began to fold outward, unfolding like the skeleton of a bird waking from sleep.

The machine was alive.

Cezar stepped onto the platform. He placed his hands on the controls.

He looked back at the drilling sound. It was getting faster. Closer.

Whatever was on the other side of that door, it wasn’t waiting anymore.

The drill broke through. A jagged hole appeared in the center of the steel. Through the gap, a pair of glowing blue eyes stared back at him.

Cezar gripped the levers.

The Red Dragon stood.
